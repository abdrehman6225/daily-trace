Thinking Machines trains a MoE with a 4-bit forward pass through pretraining and RL

This is a rare production write-up of low-precision RL. It shows how to keep the gradients unbiased and why communication became the bottleneck once the matrix math got faster.

Jack Cook and colleagues at Thinking Machines trained Inkling 2 Small with an NVFP4 forward pass in the MoE expert layers. NVFP4 is NVIDIA's 4-bit float format, which uses one scale factor per block of 16 values. They used the same numerics from the first pretraining step to the last RL rollout, so the quantized checkpoint needs no calibration afterwards. The baseline was full MXFP8, an 8-bit format with one scale per block of 32 values. Against it, training ran 8% faster in pretraining and 20% faster in RL. In pretraining the cost was about 2% compute efficiency: the MXFP8 run would reach the same loss with roughly 2% fewer FLOPs. In RL they saw no difference in reward. On GB300, NVFP4 runs at 15 PFLOPS, against 5 for MXFP8 and 2.5 for BF16. Running both the forward and backward passes in NVFP4 hurt accuracy too much.

The backward pass uses transposed tensors. Block scaling along the other axis puts each value in a different block, so it gets a different scale and a different quantized value, which biases the gradients. NVIDIA's fix uses 16×16 scale tiles, which keep the forward and backward tensors bit-identical. The team found those tiles too coarse, because outliers push many values to zero. Their fix is to de-quantize, transpose in high precision, and re-quantize to MXFP8 with stochastic rounding, which rounds up or down at random in proportion to distance. Gradients then run as MXFP8 matrix multiplications. The forward and backward tensors only need to be equal in expectation, not bit for bit.

Faster matrix math exposed expert-parallel communication as the new bottleneck. NVLink moves 900 GB/s while the GPU computes at 15 PFLOPS, so a GPU can do about 16,000 FLOPs in the time it receives one byte. Tokens are dispatched in NVFP4. For combine, MXFP8 broke on outliers. Instead they apply a 32-dimensional random Hadamard transform, a rotation that smooths out outliers, and then quantize to block-scaled S2F6, an 8-bit fixed-point format. This happens inside the final step of the w2 GEMM, and the receiver de-quantizes and sums on the fly. Combine traffic falls from 16 to 8.25 bits per value.

Under the hood

  • The last 16.6% of layers stay at higher precision, in MXFP8. NVIDIA's recipe uses 15%; one sixth fits their 6× layer-scaling ladder.
  • Activations use Four Over Six (picks between two scale factors per block to cut error); weights do not.
  • Outer scales are per-expert for weights and per-token for activations, giving batch invariance and no causality leakage.
  • The in-house kernel stack, BBBK, is deterministic and uses GB300's 2SM UMMA instruction (K=96), with a 6-stage operand pipeline and a 7-stage scale pipeline.
  • Inkling 2 Small pretrained in 68 hours on GB300 NVL72 racks. FP4 backward passes and Vera Rubin are next.

Takeaway. Put low precision on the high-volume forward pass, and keep gradients unbiased in expectation with stochastic re-quantization. Once compute gets faster, measure bytes per FLOP on the interconnect, because communication becomes the new bottleneck.

DeepSeek-V3's architecture in 190 lines of plain JAX, trained on a MacBook

A short, readable reference for MLA, DeepSeekMoE and bias-based expert balancing, with an ablation that tests the paper's balancing claim.

This is the DeepSeek-V3 architecture written from scratch in 190 lines of JAX. Parameters are plain dicts of arrays, with no Flax or Equinox. It includes MLA (Multi-head Latent Attention, which caches one compressed latent per token instead of full keys and values: 480 → 144 numbers per token per layer here), decoupled RoPE, one shared expert plus 16 routed experts with top-4 routing, auxiliary-loss-free balancing and multi-token prediction (λ = 0.3). The 36.2M-parameter model (13.6M active per token) trained for about 2.6 h on a MacBook Air M5 and reached a validation loss of 3.51. To keep it simple, every expert runs on every token (no sparse dispatch), and MLA skips weight absorption.

Under the hood

  • Balancing ablation at step 2000: with the routing bias, each expert took 5.7–6.9% of the load (an even split is 6.25%). Without it, the spread was 0.09–18.6%. Validation loss was 3.921 vs 3.923.
  • 39 tests cover cached vs full forward pass equivalence, causal masking, routing, MTP targets and bf16 compute on f32 master weights. Training ran at about 10.7k tokens/s on Metal.
  • From the HN comments: one user ran it end to end on an RX 7600 XT through ROCm JAX. It needed HSA_OVERRIDE_GFX_VERSION=11.0.0 and Triton GEMM turned off to avoid a GPU hang.

Takeaway. Before building on MLA or MoE routing, read the mla, route and update_bias functions in this repo. To balance experts, adjusting a per-expert bias on which experts get picked kept loads even here at no cost in loss, which suggests trying it before adding an auxiliary loss.

Antithesis's search-based fuzzer beats NetHack on about 80% of seeds in five minutes

A six-year record of hand-built reward functions, search over game states and harness bugs on a hard long-horizon environment, useful if you build agents or reward functions.

Antithesis reports that it now finds a winning run on nearly 80% of NetHack seeds within 5 wall-clock minutes, with a median under 2 minutes on the seeds that finish. Its fuzzer keeps a pool of saved "interesting" game states and searches forward from them. The value function, the score it uses to rank states, was written by hand. Most of the post is war stories. The fuzzer abused random polymorphs, it lost a year to a buffer overflow in the team's own code, and a reward overflow made dropping items look good. The fetched text stops right where the LLM-agent part begins.

Under the hood

  • The fuzzer explores from a spread of saved states, not one frontier, and can rewind a chosen state slightly to roll out again from an earlier one. This covers for an imperfect value function.
  • When you polymorph into your own race, the game applies random changes to level and stats that average out to zero. The fuzzer kept only the lucky rolls and climbed from level 1 to 22.
  • The reward was a 32-bit integer. Past 2^31 it wrapped negative after deep descents, so dropping items became the most rewarding move. Nobody noticed for a long time.
  • A fixed-size monster buffer in their own instrumentation caused segfaults below Gehennom level 10 for about a year. Making it 10x larger fixed them.
  • Runs chained from the previous night's best state failed when old build artifacts aged out of the retention policy.

Takeaway. Add plots and overflow checks to reward and value functions, because a search will exploit their bugs without telling you. Pin the build artifacts behind any long-lived chain of checkpoints.

unsure: five fast probabilistic data structures for Go, with benchmarks

Rate limits, cache admission, distinct counts and p99 latency, each in kilobytes, plus implementation tricks worth reusing in any language.

A new Go package, unsure (github.com/nalgeon/unsure), implements five probabilistic structures: Bloom filter, count-min sketch, HyperLogLog, t-digest and Top-k. Each core is 100–300 lines, uses only the standard library, and is benchmarked against a common Go library. Its blocked Bloom filter confines each lookup to one 512-bit, cache-line-sized block, and runs about 4× faster than bits-and-blooms for 1–8% more memory. Count-min uses conservative update (raise only the counters below the new estimate) and 32-bit counters: half the memory, 2–2.7× lower error.

Under the hood

  • HyperLogLog uses Ertl's histogram estimator, so Count takes constant time: 57 ns vs 4.7 ms for axiom at p=18. Axiom's sparse mode wins below about 16K items.
  • T-digest (merging variant, δ=100, about 8 KB) merges 7–9× faster than influxdata with 40% less memory at similar rank error.
  • Top-k is HeavyKeeper (2 rows, decay base b=0.8) plus a min-heap. On flat Zipf s=0.6 streams it reaches 90–97% precision vs 38–70% for keiler.

Takeaway. Hash each item once with wyhash and get each row's position by multiplying by a random constant, and size the structure so each lookup touches one cache line. For rate limiting or cache admission, a count-min sketch with conservative update covers 1M keys in about 0.5 MB.

Thousands of Lovable apps leak Supabase data through the public API key

A large scan of AI-generated apps shows what happens when the browser can reach the database and every table is readable unless someone locks it down.

Crissy Field, a German security firm working with Handelsblatt, found 159,041 Lovable projects in Common Crawl (Dec 2025–Sep 2026). 34,545 used Supabase. 24,664 sent Supabase rows on page load with no login. Using the same API key the page already ships, extra read-only requests found email-bearing records in 10,560 tables across 7,403 of those sites (30%). The exposed data included a forum with 80,000+ profiles and 200,000+ chat messages, 1,000+ passport records (number, expiry, nationality), ticket sales with payment details and ticket codes, and 280,000 customer emails. The authors stress that 30% is not a confirmed leak rate.

On HN, Lovable's Head of Security said the root cause was insufficient Row-Level Security (RLS: Postgres policies that decide which rows each caller may read). He said Lovable's pre-publish scanner and SAST (static code analysis) target exactly this, with an option to block publishing. Commenters blamed Supabase's old permissive defaults, reportedly changed October 30 but maybe not for existing projects. The report notes that generated apps share the same patterns, so one working exploit carries over to many apps.

Under the hood

  • Method: read-only requests with the client-side key, downloading only part of each dataset, so the real record counts may be higher.
  • Email addresses were the signal for personal data. That counts public contact addresses and test data but misses records without emails. The sample is not representative.
  • Lovable's own database feature (late 2025) was not examined. Similar misconfigurations may exist there.
  • Lovable's Terms & Conditions make customers responsible for security, even though its marketing says no technical knowledge is needed.

Takeaway. If the browser holds a database key, row-level policies are your only access control. Make every table deny access by default, and add an automated test that tries to read each table with the public key and no user session.

ggml hits 1.0: deprecated API removed, FP8 added, and MoE work on every backend

The tensor library under llama.cpp marks 1.0 by removing its deprecated API, adding FP8 and running some MoE experts on the CPU when GPU memory is tight.

ggml 1.0.0, the tensor library behind llama.cpp, deletes its deprecated public API and the old DIAG_MASK ops. It also bumps the RPC protocol so incompatible peers are rejected. New features: configurable YaRN truncation (rounding of the context-extension correction bounds) and FP8. The MoE expert cache can now run some cache-miss experts on the CPU and copy the results back asynchronously. The scheduler stops uploading inputs nobody reads. On Hacker News, one user's 50K-token benchmark dropped from 4:22 to 3:59 after upgrading. Under the hood

  • Vulkan gets an Intel FlashAttention prefill kernel, SYCL a device-side MoE scheduler, and Metal fused DeepSeek-V4/GLM MoE routing.

Ratatui maintainer proposes cell-edge borders as a terminal attribute

A small protocol-design case study: borders stored per cell like a background color, interned behind a 16-bit ID, with feature detection and graceful fallback.

Josh McKinney, who maintains the Rust TUI library Ratatui, proposes a draft protocol for drawing thin lines on cell edges, so borders no longer take up a whole row or column. A border is a cell attribute: up to 12 segments per cell (two halves of each edge plus four arms from the center), each with its own color, width and style. Because it lives in the cell, it scrolls, erases and reflows with the text. It works as a ~1,650-line Ghostty patch and as Ratatui demos. The author says AI wrote much of the post and code, and he hasn't benchmarked rendering yet.

Under the hood

  • Wire format: one namespaced DCS string, ESC P 1 + b cell-border;OP;.... A pen held with the cursor applies borders to printed cells, and SGR 0 clears it.
  • Support check: the app sends a capability query, then DA1 (the standard device-attributes request). If DA1 answers with no cell-border reply before it, the app falls back to box-drawing characters.
  • Storage: a 16-bit border ID fits in existing cell padding. It points into a shared table of ~100-byte sets that is never freed, so the worst case is ~10–15 MB.
  • Shared edges merge at render time: higher priority wins, then the wider stroke, then the cell to the right or below. Multiplexers that don't support it should drop the sequences.
  • HN pushback: overstrike mode (DECSET 20, supported in mintty) would let border characters be drawn over text. Others worry terminals are turning into browsers.

Takeaway. To add a per-item property cheaply, intern the distinct values in a shared table and store a small ID in space the struct already wastes. Then items without the property cost nothing. Ship new protocol features with a capability probe and a fallback, and make extension fields safe to ignore.

Stochastic rounding: why 4-bit training can still have unbiased gradients

4-bit matrix math is fast, but the FP4 (E2M1) format can only represent the magnitudes 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4 and 6. To make that usable, formats like NVFP4 give each block of 16 values a shared FP8 scale. The usual way to quantize is round-to-nearest (RTN). It gives the smallest error for any single value, but the error is biased: values that fall in the same spot between two grid points always round the same way. Over millions of steps those errors add up instead of cancelling, and an update smaller than half a grid step disappears every time.

Stochastic rounding (SR) fixes the bias. Take a value x between two grid points a < b. Round up to b with probability (x−a)/(b−a), and down to a otherwise. The expected result is exactly x. For example, 2.4 lies between 2 and 3. RTN always returns 2, which is an error of −0.4 every time. SR returns 3 with probability 0.4 and 2 with probability 0.6, and 0.6·2 + 0.4·3 = 2.4. Add up 1,000 such values: the RTN total is 2,000, while the SR total stays close to 2,400. SGD-style optimizers only need gradients that are correct on average, so SR's noise averages out the way minibatch noise does.

The trade-offs: SR adds variance, needs random bits for every element (often generated in hardware), and has a larger error than RTN on any single value. The common recipe is RTN for forward weights and activations, where the error on each sample matters, and SR for gradients, where bias adds up across steps. It is often combined with a random Hadamard transform, which spreads outliers out before block scaling. Use SR whenever values are re-quantized repeatedly or summed over many steps.

Further reading: Gupta et al., "Deep Learning with Limited Numerical Precision" (ICML 2015); NVIDIA, "Pretraining Large Language Models with NVFP4" (arXiv 2025)

Google's /teamwork harness: partitioned contexts, hard verifiers, and long multi-day agent runs

If you build agents, two harness design choices here are worth copying: give each agent only the context its step needs, and check its output with tools it cannot change.

Google Research reports results from Antigravity's /teamwork, a multi-agent harness running on Gemini 4 Argon. The design rests on two ideas. First, many attempts run in parallel and are checked by falsifiers: Lean 4, unmodified test suites, simulators and hardware counters. Second, each agent gets only the files and tools for its step, and critics audit results from clean contexts. Claimed outputs include a Lean proof of the Courtade–Kumar conjecture, about 800k lines of safe Rust finishing the Zircon kernel port (it boots on arm64), and a libgav1 Rust port 20–30% faster than the C++.

Under the hood

  • Rewrite pattern: a module is scheduled only after its dependencies compile, and each leaf is linked against the original, unmodified test suite. This stops agents from stubbing out dependencies or editing tests.
  • DuoDiscover: 23-second proxy evaluators stand in for 7–32-minute TPU runs and are checked with Kendall rank correlation (whether they rank candidates the same way). Result: 4.09x geometric-mean speedup on 17 JAXBench kernels.
  • HN: commenters say auditor agents still falsely report tasks as done. One user said team modes copy context into every agent and used up a weekly token budget in about two days.

Takeaway. Check agent work with tools the agent cannot edit, such as test suites, proof checkers and benchmarks, and run reviewers in fresh contexts without the producer's reasoning. For short-run tasks, use a cheap proxy metric and keep checking that it ranks candidates the same way the expensive one does.

vLLM runs multimodal encoders as separate instances, scaled apart from prefill and decode

A production pattern for separating pipeline stages: pass metadata instead of payloads, share caches across workers, and make a failed handoff fail only the request it belongs to.

vLLM v0.32.0 extends EPD (Encoder/Prefill/Decode disaggregation), which runs the vision and audio encoders as their own instances. In metadata-only requests, prefill receives layout fields such as image_grid_thw instead of the raw media, so it skips decoding and preprocessing again. The embeddings themselves travel over NIXL or Mooncake. Mooncake Store lets encoders reuse each other's cached outputs. On DeepSeek V4.1 Flash using GB200, EPD beat an 8-GPU PD baseline by 6.6–9.5% throughput per GPU at concurrency 64–128, with the encoder GPUs counted, and cut P90 time-to-first-token by 44–47%. At concurrency 16 it lost.

Under the hood

  • Audio on Qwen3-Omni: metadata-only raised throughput from 62.21 to 73.13 req/s and cut mean latency from 980 to 853 ms. Accuracy fell from 75.90% to 75.65%, not yet explained.
  • Shared cache: on a best-case run where every image moved to another encoder, repeated encodings fell from 512 to 0 and p50 time-to-first-token from 1.209 to 0.819 s. Concurrent cold requests can still duplicate work.
  • When a consumer holding only metadata misses its embedding, it fails that request instead of falling back to local encoding, which could crash EngineCore. Encoder-only loading read 2 of 96 shards (683 s down to 0.31 s).

Takeaway. When splitting a pipeline into stages, have the downstream stage take the structural metadata upstream already computed instead of reprocessing the raw input. Fail only the affected request when a stage has no way to recover, and benchmark across load levels: the split lost at low concurrency.

Google's A2UI 1.0 lets agents and apps call each other's functions

If you build agent SDKs, A2UI shows how to let a model generate UI without trusting it: a compact language for the model, server-side compilation and validation, and a component catalog the app controls.

Google released A2UI 1.0, the first stable version of its open protocol for agents that answer with interactive screens. The agent describes the screen, and the app draws it using only components it has registered. New in 1.0: the agent and the app can now call each other's functions, through callRendererFunction and callAgentFunction. According to the linked inference-formats docs, the model writes a compact language called Express, which the server compiles and validates into the JSON the app receives. Compared with having the model write that JSON directly, Express used 45% fewer input tokens and 63% fewer output tokens, with the same 98.1% pass@1 (correct on the first try).

Under the hood

  • Benchmark: 54 test cases, gemini-3.8-flash, temperature 0. Express cut median input tokens from 10,980 to 6,000 and output tokens from 807 to 297.
  • Each catalog function declares who may call it: rendererOnly (the default), agentOnly or rendererOrAgent. This is checked at runtime, and an app rejects a call it should not accept with the error INVALID_FUNCTION_CALL.
  • Breaking changes from 0.9: protocol keys now start with @ (such as @path), theme is removed, and the MIME type is now application/a2ui+json. One view can mix components from several catalogs. Renderers exist for React, Angular, Lit, Flutter and SwiftUI. The Android Jetpack Compose renderer is still on 0.9.1.

Takeaway. When a model has to produce structured output, have it write a short format and let the server compile it into the full schema and validate it. Send validation errors back to the model so it can fix them.

Meta's Intelligent Delivery sends ad-serving compute to the requests that gain most from it

A production example of a shared model predicting the value of compute for each request, a layered set of allocators acting on it, and agents tuning the whole system.

Meta's Intelligent Delivery decides where each unit of compute goes across requests, regions, hardware and ranking stages. Meta credits it with about 10% extra ads ranking performance from 2017 to the first half of 2026, on top of model and system gains. At its core is the Resource Core Model, with 6B sparse and 20M dense parameters. It runs once before candidate retrieval, and the whole funnel shares its output. Fine-tuned versions of it predict each request's outcome, compute cost and latency. Before this, engineers wrote allocation rules for coarse request segments. Replacing those rules found high-impact requests about 20% better in offline benchmarks.

Allocation runs through a hierarchy of decision engines. Traffic moves between regions every few minutes until each region gets the same efficiency from its last unit of capacity. Each workload is matched to the hardware type (SKU) that measures best for it. On each host, a constrained optimizer works within a tail-latency SLO and cuts costly, low-impact work first as load rises. Because these decisions affect each other, changes are tested in a closed-loop hardware testbed. Agents read source code, design docs and a decade of experiment logs, and contributed to about 20% of optimizations in the first half of 2026. Engineers approve every change.

Under the hood

  • Stages hit different limits: retrieval runs out of memory, ranking runs out of compute, model inference runs out of accelerator throughput. No single SKU is fastest; each workload–SKU pair is benchmarked.
  • The fleet spans Intel, AMD and Arm CPUs plus NVIDIA, AMD and MTIA accelerators, across several generations each. Per-host enforcement reaches from the kernel scheduler up to application thread pools.
  • An earlier constrained-optimization autotuner could cover only a few key settings and was slow. On the most automated mechanism, agents now do about 80% of the engineering work, cutting the loop from months to weeks.
  • Next: one model that both reads a request and serves it in real time. GPU-served models like GEM need new controls because GPU capacity comes in whole, fixed blocks.

Takeaway. Have one shared model estimate each request's marginal value and cost, then let separate allocators at each layer act on that estimate. Test changes end to end, because tuning one layer alone can make the whole system worse.

PostgreSQL patched 52 CVEs in one minor release; upgrading takes more than new binaries

The CVE fixes are only half of it: C extensions need recompiling, some indexes need rebuilding and incremental backups need a fresh baseline, or scans can return wrong results.

On November 12, PostgreSQL shipped 18.7, 17.12, 16.16, 15.20 and 14.25, the last PG 14 release, with 52 CVEs and 140+ bug fixes. A normal quarterly release patches three to five. Ruohang Feng (Pigsty) ties the surge to AI-assisted auditing; the 18.7 release notes credit OpenAI and Anthropic/Claude. 32 CVEs score CVSS 8.8, many allowing code execution. Most are C type confusion, use-after-free, heap overflow and integer wraparound bugs, reaching even pg_dump and pg_restore.

Under the hood

  • CVE-2026-6460 needs only object-creation privileges; the full fix means recompiling C extensions against new headers. Don't disable composite_type_mismatch to skip that.
  • Parallel pg_dump against a standby could corrupt both the dump and the standby; incremental backups could be corrupt. Take a fresh full backup after upgrading.
  • Rebuild GiST gist_trgm_ops and B-tree indexes on unbounded bpchar, and check GIN indexes. Also check partitioned tables for unintended NOT NULL NO INHERIT.
  • The planner's join-removal logic was rewritten, a change this large is unusual in a minor release.

Takeaway. Treat this as a planned upgrade with a checklist: new binaries, rebuilt extensions, reindexes and a new full-backup baseline. Firewalls don't block every path: malicious backup files and low-privileged roles can still exploit these bugs.

MemCredit picks agent memories by counterfactual decision value, not similarity

For a memory engine, this tests whether removing a memory changes the agent's action, and shows that semantic relevance tells you almost nothing about that.

A UESTC and NTU paper accepted at WWW 2027 asks one question about each memory: would the frozen agent act worse without it? To answer it offline, the authors replay two branches from the same ALFWorld environment snapshot. The branches differ only in whether one atomic memory appears in the prompt. The other memories in the prompt (the reference context) are sampled at 0–100% of the token budget, so duplicate memories show up instead of hiding each other. Each label is a reward difference: +1 for a win, −1 for an invalid or repeated failed action, 0 otherwise. These labels train an ExtraTrees regressor (an ensemble of randomized decision trees). At inference, relevance only recalls the candidate pool. A greedy loop then adds the memory with the highest predicted credit per token, rescores everything after each insert, and stops when no candidate clears a threshold.

With Qwen3.6-27B-FP8 on ALFWorld, the success rate within 50 steps (SR@50) is 86.57% against 65.67% for BM25 at a 500-token budget. At 750 tokens it is 90.30%, against 81.34% for BM25 and 85.82% for MemArbiter. On WebShop it scores 0.612 against MemArbiter's 0.586 while using 152.1 memory tokens per decision instead of 427.6. Of 1,041 interventions, 86.9% changed nothing. Relevance correlated with utility at ρ=−0.011.

Under the hood

  • Utility averages 0.176 when the agent has just failed and 0.029 otherwise. A failure-only heuristic still reaches just 70.15% SR@50.
  • Blending relevance into the final score (λ=0.5) drops SR@50 to 78.36%, against 84.33% for utility-only. Relevance helps recall, not ranking.
  • The estimator is weak: Spearman 0.220 and AUROC 0.744. Relevance-only features score AUROC 0.483.
  • Each label comes from a single one-step paired run. Repeated runs of the same setup vary by up to ±3 pp because of GPU nondeterminism. Under GPT-5.4, BM25 edges out MemCredit on SR@50.

Takeaway. Split memory retrieval into two stages: high-recall similarity search, then a cheap learned scorer trained on with/without replays of real decisions. Select greedily by value per token and rescore after each pick so duplicate memories lose priority.

NOPO: train on verifier failures only when passing checks proves little

If your RL reward comes from partial checks such as unit tests, constraint checks or self-verification, rewarding passes can teach shortcuts; this NeurIPS 2026 poster learns only from failures.

Jiacheng Xu, Shuo He, Fuxiang Zhang, Chaojie Wang and Bo An name a common RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards) setting one-sided verifiability. Cheap checks can reliably prove a solution wrong, but a pass only means the check found nothing, and many passes are false positives. Standard RLVR reinforces those passes, so it can reward shortcuts that get through partial tests.

NOPO (Negative-Only Policy Optimization) treats verifier-positives as unlabeled and updates the policy only by pushing down verified negatives. It has two safeguards. The first is a per-sample NPO term (negative preference optimization, a loss from model unlearning) whose gradient shrinks once a failed sample's probability falls below the reference policy. That limits over-suppression. The second scales each group's update by how many of its samples passed. The abstract reports better pass@k than baselines on code, logic and math benchmarks when oracle rewards are unavailable, with unit-test, constraint-check and LLM self-verifiers. The gains hold under inference-time scaling and transfer across benchmarks. Only the abstract was available. OpenReview blocked the fetch, so the baselines and numbers are unknown.

Under the hood

  • Asymmetry: a verified negative is a trusted counterexample, while a verified positive is only a candidate that has not been falsified.
  • The per-sample term self-attenuates: its gradient shrinks once a negative's likelihood falls below the reference model's.
  • Group weight: each rollout group's update is scaled by its count of verifier-positive samples.
  • Evaluated with three verifier types: unit tests (code), constraint checks (logic), and LLM self-verification (math).

Takeaway. If your reward checker can only falsify, treat its passes as unlabeled rather than as positives, and learn mainly from trusted failures. Use a loss that stops pushing once a failure is already below the reference policy.

Haochen Liu, Qianhao Zhu, Zixuan Wang, Dingming Li, Tongbo Chen, Peng Qiu, Yingqi Qu, Weiming Lu et al. — Zhejiang University

Zhejiang University and Baidu tested "decision models", which return a probability per option or a 0–1 score instead of free text, in three roles: actor, judge and teacher. Acting on ALFWorld, JEV finishes 42.9% of tasks. Used as a frozen GRPO teacher that scores every action once the episode ends, it lifts Qwen2.5-1.5B from 72.8% to 82.9% on ALFWorld and from 56.8% to 71.4% on WebShop. The question matters: asking "did this action help?" works, while "will the task succeed?" gives 69.3% on ALFWorld.

Under the hood

  • Advantage = GRPO outcome advantage + 0.5 × (score − group mean). It is not divided by std, so when the judge gives flat scores the update barely changes.
  • With the full trajectory visible, the "did it help" question scores subgoal-completing actions 0.36 above do-nothing actions. The "will it succeed" question gives a gap of only 0.05.
  • The taught 1.5B model beats a 7B model trained on outcome rewards only. Judge queries cost $22–30 per training run. Tested only on text environments with episodes of at most 30 actions.

Takeaway. To pick a step-level reward judge, test it on recorded trajectories, not on how well it acts. Show it the whole episode, ask what each step contributed, and center its scores within each rollout group.
