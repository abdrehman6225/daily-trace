MaxIM: training a bounded-memory summarizer with dense information-theoretic RL rewards

Why read · Google Research (NeurIPS 2026) fixes the sparse-reward credit-assignment problem in RL-trained agent memory with critic-scored rewards for each update, beating MemAgent and task-only RL on retention and grounding.

Jihwan Jeong, Guy Tennenholtz, Yinlam Chow, Chih-Wei Hsu, Craig Boutilier

The setup: a trainable summarizer π(z_t | z_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, o_t) keeps a text memory capped at K tokens, and a frozen black-box agent acts using only z_t and the latest observation. RL on the downstream task reward alone works poorly because that reward arrives only at the end of a dialogue or document. The policy gets no signal about which of 20+ earlier updates threw away the fact that mattered. The authors (Jeong, Tennenholtz, Chow, Hsu, Boutilier) prove that the value gap between full-history and bounded-memory policies is bounded by sequential information loss I(Y_t; H_t | z_t), where Y_t is the task-relevant target. From that bound they get two dense rewards. Predictive sufficiency rewards a summary that makes the target likely. Recursive consistency maximizes MI (mutual information) between the new summary and its immediate inputs, so the policy cannot hallucinate text that merely correlates with the target.

One unified critic (Gemini 2.5 Flash) is trained offline with cross-entropy on synthetic data that includes the oracle targets. This is "asymmetric" because the critic sees hindsight targets that the summarizer never sees. Each reward is a PMI (pointwise mutual information) score: the critic's log-likelihood with the real input minus its log-likelihood with a null placeholder such as "(no summary available)". Both rewards come from teacher-forced forward passes only, with no critic generation, and the critic is never called at test time. The policy is trained with KL-regularized PPO and GRPO-style leave-one-out group advantages over G=8 rollouts. Returns are discounted backward, and the shaping weight is annealed to zero mid-training.

On arXiv reading with K=500, MaxIM scores 66.39% Abstain-MCQ accuracy (a metric that penalizes wrong guesses), against 54.44% for task-only RL and 52.90% for MemAgent. On early-section details it reaches 48.55% vs 32.23% for task-only RL. On WildChat, unshaped RL nearly matches MaxIM on the terminal belief-accuracy reward, yet it does worse than having no memory at all on user satisfaction. Its intermediate beliefs mislead the assistant. Limits: every component is Gemini, judging is done by GPT-4.1, the overall WildChat gain is small (+0.05 Sat.), and training requires oracle targets.

Under the hood

  • Rewards: r_pred = length-normalized log q(Y|z_t) − log q(Y|z_null), so easy targets don't score well regardless of the summary. r_cons = log q(z_t|H̃_t) − log q(z_t|H̃_null), scored in the forward direction. Scoring backward (reconstructing the input from z_t) does not penalize fabricated claims added next to true ones.
  • Shaping: each reward goes through a centered sigmoid calibrated on the base policy, so a baseline-quality summary earns 0. The consistency weight is kept below the sufficiency weight (w_c < w_s), shaping is scaled by 1−γ (γ=0.9), and a cosine λ_s schedule anneals it away. Decaying between 45% and 60% of training gives 66.39%, vs 57.10% with no annealing, 61.18% decaying early, and 61.12% decaying late.
  • Budget scaling: at K=100 MaxIM scores 34.26%, above Prompt-Only at K=500 (31.30%). At K=500 it beats Prompt-Only at K=2,000 (64.32%), and it saturates at 67.04% at K=800. In chunks 21–25 it still incorporates 26.8% of each new chunk's claims, while the baselines saturate once the buffer is full.
  • Best-of-8 MI SFT, which greedily keeps the single-step update with the highest reward, scores only 29.05%. Myopic selection cannot plan how to spend memory capacity across chunks, so the multi-step RL is what makes the difference.
  • Grounding on WildChat: 4.27% contradicted claims vs 9.35% for MemAgent and 7.04% for task-only RL, with 40.3 grounded claims vs 21–23. Ablations: removing consistency (Suff-Only) costs 14.85 pp on arXiv. Shaping alone with no task reward (MI-Only) reaches 50.30%, but it packs in claims with low selectivity.

Takeaway. If you train a memory or summarizer policy with RL, add dense rewards to the sparse terminal reward: score each update with a frozen critic as a log-ratio against a null-input baseline, and anneal those rewards away so late training optimizes the real task. Also evaluate memory in closed loop at intermediate turns. A memory that scores well on terminal accuracy can still give the agent false beliefs mid-episode.

GRPO advantages, and how averaging can turn tool calls negative

The problem: PPO needs a learned value network (critic) to estimate how much better an action was than expected. For LLMs that critic is about as large as the policy and hard to train when the only reward comes at the end. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) removes the critic. For each prompt it samples a group of G responses, scores each one, and uses the group itself as the baseline.

Step by step: (1) sample G trajectories for one prompt; (2) compute rewards r_i; (3) normalize: A_i = (r_i − mean(r)) / std(r); (4) give A_i to every token the model generated in trajectory i, while tool outputs are masked out; (5) maximize the PPO clipped ratio objective using those advantages, usually with a KL penalty toward a reference model; (6) average the per-token losses. Step 6 matters. You can average per sequence first and then across the group, or average over all tokens together (token-mean, as in DAPO). These choices weight long trajectories differently.

Small example: G=4, rewards 1,0,0,0. The mean is 0.25 and the std is 0.433, so the success gets A=+1.73 and each failure gets −0.58. The advantages sum to zero per trajectory. Now say the success made 2 tool calls and each failure made 6. Summed over tool-call tokens, that is 2×1.73 = +3.46 against 18×(−0.58) = −10.4. The net gradient pushes tool calling down, even though calling tools well is how the agent succeeded. Today's paper fixes problems like this with small changes to the objective, such as a uniform shift that clamps tool credit.

Trade-offs: GRPO is simple, uses no critic and works well with verifiable rewards. But all tokens in a trajectory share one advantage, so credit is coarse. Groups where every sample gets the same reward produce no signal, and length and averaging effects add bias. Use it when rewards are checkable and episodes are short-to-medium. For long multi-turn agents, add per-step signals (as MaxIM does) and log advantage statistics for each token type.

Further reading: DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models (Shao et al., 2024); DAPO: An Open-Source LLM Reinforcement Learning System at Scale (Yu et al., 2025)

Building malloc in four stages, then benchmarking it against glibc

Why read · A measured walk from a bump allocator to free lists, trimming and mmap explains why glibc's malloc runs at about 27 ns, why RSS stays high after you free everything, and which knobs change that.

Ben Andrew starts with strace and glibc. Calling malloc(16) a thousand times makes only two brk syscalls, both on the first call: one reads the program break (the address where the heap ends) and one grows the heap by 132 KiB. An empty syscall costs 575 ns, sbrk(16) costs 610 ns and malloc(16) costs 26.9 ns, about 22.7x faster than sbrk(16). Most of the speed comes from rarely going to the kernel. He then builds a toy allocator in four stages. (1) A bump allocator calls sbrk on every request and never reuses memory. (2) Chunks get size headers, plus a first-fit free list that splits large chunks to fit and merges adjacent free chunks on free. (3) trim lowers the break once the top free chunk reaches 128 KiB, keeping 64 KiB in reserve. (4) Requests of 128 KiB or more get their own anonymous mmap, marked by a flag bit in the size header, so free can munmap them.

The memory lessons matter more than the code. Because of demand paging (the kernel only backs a virtual page with RAM on its first write), an sbrk of 1 GiB costs almost nothing until you touch it. After that it costs one fault per 4 KiB page, 262,147 faults in total. Once pages are written they stay resident after free. Only the top of a heap can be handed back, so a single live 16-byte chunk above a freed 1 MiB block pins the whole 1 MiB. Over a long run that is how fragmentation grows RSS until a memory limit kills the process. Moving large blocks to mmap breaks that pinning, but each new mapping pays page faults again and cannot be merged with its neighbours.

In the benchmark (4000 live blocks, 500k random free/alloc steps, 90% of requests 16–512 B), glibc takes 0.0789 µs per step. The toy free list takes 5.12 µs, the trimming version 11.2 µs and the mmap version 11.8 µs, because they walk one linked list two or three times per step. glibc avoids that with size-segregated bins and a per-thread cache (tcache). The tcache is also why glibc still holds 19.0 MiB after every block is freed: cached chunks count as in use and block trimming. HN commenters (loeg, senderista) note that brk-based single-heap designs are dated. jemalloc and mimalloc do not use brk at all, and mimalloc's codebase is suggested as an approachable place to see how modern allocators work.

Under the hood

  • glibc chunk size is the request plus an 8-byte header, rounded up to a multiple of 16, with a 32-byte minimum. A 1-byte or 24-byte request takes 32 bytes, 25 takes 48 and 1000 takes 1008. The low 3 bits of the size hold flags, one of which (M) marks an mmapped chunk.
  • Freed chunks store the free-list next pointer in what used to be user data. The 32-byte minimum exists so a chunk has room for those list pointers once it is freed.
  • RSS after all blocks are freed: bump 1425 MiB, free list 17.8 MiB, trim 0.188 MiB, mmap 0.188 MiB, glibc 19.0 MiB. With the tcache off, glibc drops to 0.250 MiB and each step takes 0.121 µs, about 1.5x slower.
  • GLIBC_TUNABLES=glibc.malloc.tcache_count=0 turns off the tcache. glibc.malloc.mmap_threshold=131072 fixes the mmap threshold at 128 KiB. That makes glibc 10.7x slower (0.843 µs per step), because glibc normally raises the threshold to the size of each freed mmapped chunk. That adaptation is why it made 51 mmap calls against the toy's 5040.
  • glibc also creates extra heaps (arenas) with mmap and spreads threads across them so they don't all contend for the main heap's lock.
  • Benchmark setup: Intel Xeon E3-1230 v3, built at -O2, a fresh process per allocator, one write per page of each new block. Request sizes: 90% are 16–512 B, 9% up to 16 KiB, 1% 128–256 KiB.

Takeaway. If a long-running Go-adjacent, Python or C service holds high RSS after load drops, suspect fragmentation and allocator caching (tcache, a raised mmap threshold) before suspecting a leak. Measure anonymous RSS, try GLIBC_TUNABLES or malloc_trim, or switch to jemalloc or mimalloc. When you write your own pool or arena, segregate free lists by size class so you never walk one global list.

e-means: k-means that tracks centroids with mini-batch moving averages, now in Faiss

Why read · If you build IVF indexes or codebooks, this matches or beats Lloyd's clustering quality in 1.2–3 epochs where Lloyd needs 25, and you don't tune anything new.

The authors include Matthijs Douze and Hervé Jégou of Faiss, and the work grew out of earlier Meta FAIR experiments. e-means swaps Lloyd's full-pass centroid recompute for mini-batch exponential moving average updates: S_j ← (1−α)S_j + α·s_j, with the same rule for the count N_j, and the centroid is S_j/N_j. Lloyd is the corner case B=n, α=1, and the authors' grid search found that corner far from optimal. Batch size B and starting step α0 come from fitted scaling laws in (n, k, epochs T), so there is nothing new to tune. Other pieces: better initialization, a cosine-decayed α, a cheap finalization stage, and dead-cluster revival that splits large clusters. It reaches a test loss below Lloyd-at-25-epochs in 1.2–3 epochs. End to end that is 10–45× faster on GPU than Flash-KMeans and 8–15× faster on CPU than Faiss. Gradient-descent centroid updates were unstable at large learning rates. EMA updates keep each centroid inside the convex hull (the smallest region enclosing a set of points) of the points assigned to it.

On HN, saagarjha said mini-batch k-means with EMA updates isn't new and that beating Lloyd is a low bar. The test that matters, in that view, is how the method scales with data and workers, since allreduce communication grows. Users reported a 146× GPU speedup on an IVF index and 2.5× faster bulk reindexing in antfly. Someone relaxing a mesh on a sphere found it worse: with no dataset to sample, mini-batches only add noise to centroids that can be computed exactly.

Under the hood

  • Revival ablation (1M vectors, k=16,384, 5 epochs) on DEEP: proportional split leaves 47 dead clusters (0.3%) at test MSE 0.38762. EnCodec-style resampling from the batch leaves 2,343 (14.3%) at 0.39124. No revival leaves 4,313 (26.3%) at 0.39448.
  • Scaling laws: B = c_B·k^0.13·n^0.88·T^0.81 and α0 = min(0.824·k^−0.46·B^0.90·n^−0.45·T^−0.11, 1). Median regret against the best grid point is 0.07–0.20% on held-out datasets (SIFT, SPACEV, arXiv, AG-News). A 'quality law' variant for B gives up some throughput for lower loss.
  • Usage: from faiss.contrib.e_means import Emeans, then Emeans(d, k, nepochs=T).train(x) on a PyTorch tensor. NumpyEmeans is the NumPy version. Default is 5 epochs. Paper: arXiv:2611.09501.

Takeaway. If you train IVF or vector-quantization codebooks with Lloyd for 20–50 iterations, try Faiss's Emeans at its 5-epoch default and compare test MSE and dead-cluster count. If you use mini-batch k-means anywhere, replace dead clusters by splitting a large cluster chosen in proportion to its size, not by resampling random points from the batch.

Amazon swaps Sonnet 4.6 for a 4B decision model in ticket labeling

Why read · A production case where a closed-choice classifier replaced an LLM agent at 44x lower cost, plus a rollout switch pattern you can reuse.

Two engineers on Amazon's internal ticketing system replaced Claude Sonnet 4.6 with Strands Decider, a "decision model" from AWS's Strands agents project. A decision model generates no text. You give it some state, a question and a set of options, and it returns a probability for each option plus a confidence score. The job was to sort tickets (escalations, alarms, security findings, capacity requests) into 20 labels plus a "none" option. They first tried Decider 2B, then switched to the Gemma 4 E4B build because it scored better on a test set. After tuning confidence thresholds, they say E4B beat production Sonnet on held-out tickets. The post gives no accuracy figures. Average latency per call fell from 4,215 ms to 897 ms. Tokens per ticket fell from 6,724 to 2,010, with output dropping from 170 tokens to 1. At list prices, cost went from $0.0222 to about $0.0005 per ticket: roughly $22,200 versus $500 per million tickets, 44x cheaper. This is AWS's own blog, so read it as a success story the vendor chose to publish.

On HN, one commenter pointed out that they built an SVM ticket classifier six years ago (Linnaeus, ICSE 2021) that trained in minutes and classified in milliseconds. In their view the industry is now coming back around to cheap classifiers. Another did the maths: saving about $21,700 per million tickets means "millions a year" needs roughly 92M+ tickets annually. Others found it confusing that the post calls the Gemma E4B build "Strands Decider" after saying they moved off Decider 2B. Several asked why nobody tried Haiku or a cheap open model first, or why Amazon didn't just train its own classifier on tickets it had already labelled.

Under the hood

  • Serving: SageMaker endpoint on ml.g5.xlarge (one A10G, 24 GB) at $1.408/hr. The model uses about 19 GiB, takes about 640 ms per request and handles about 5,600 tickets per instance-hour. That is $250 per million tickets at full use, or about $500 with headroom for bursts. Pipeline: SQS → Lambda → SageMaker, defined in CDK with Beta/Gamma/Prod stages.
  • The context window is 4,096 tokens, and the label descriptions alone were longer than that. They rewrote each label as a one- or two-sentence criterion. On long tickets, the oldest comments are dropped to make the input fit.
  • Rollout: the Lambda reads a three-way AppConfig flag on every message. BEDROCK means Sonnet only. DUAL calls both models in parallel, applies Sonnet's label and logs disagreements with Decider. DECIDER means Decider only. Rolling back takes a 45-second config change.
  • Gotcha: changing only the order of JSON keys moved output probabilities by up to 0.19, so every request is now sent with sorted keys.

Takeaway. If an LLM call is really choosing from a fixed catalog at high volume, try a small classifier or decision model. Run it in parallel with the LLM on real traffic and judge every disagreement, behind a config flag you can flip back. Also make the input format deterministic (for example, sorted JSON keys) before you compare runs.

Stanford's Next-Concept Modeling generates text coarse-to-fine, beating diffusion LMs on speed

Why read · A non-autoregressive text generator from Hazy Research that is 10–31× faster than the best diffusion language models at roughly 100M parameters.

Stanford (Ré, Mirhoseini) and Together AI (Arora) propose Next-Concept Modeling (NCM). A multiscale residual VQ-VAE turns text into K levels of discrete codes. Each level holds more codes than the one before, and the last level has one code per token. A transformer then generates one level at a time and predicts every code within a level in a single forward pass, conditioned on the sum of the earlier levels. This is next-scale prediction, a technique from image generation, applied to text. At about 100M parameters, NCM beats DUO, the strongest diffusion language model (DLM) baseline on LM1B, on generative perplexity while running 21× faster. It is competitive on OpenWebText at 10× faster, matches AURORA-LM's ROUGE-L on XSum at 31× faster, and beats autoregressive (AR) and diffusion baselines on Sudoku. The speedup has two sources: there are few steps, and the early coarse steps process only short sequences.

On HN, cubefox noted that the paper hardly compares against standard next-token prediction. That suggests NCM may not beat AR outside globally constrained tasks like Sudoku. Others pointed out that a new tokenizer means retraining from scratch, and one commenter had not yet managed to run the code.

Under the hood

  • Two fixes make the hierarchy work for text. Nested dropout trains the tokenizer while randomly cutting off the finer levels, which forces the coarse levels to carry meaning on their own. Conditioning corruption trains the generator with some conditioning codes swapped for random ones, so it tolerates mistakes from earlier levels.
  • The tokenizer reconstructs Sudoku boards with 100% exact accuracy and other text with token-level accuracy close to 100%. Codebook utilization ranges from 62% to 90%.
  • On LM1B, the tokenizer and generator together saw 13.36B token positions against 66B for the baselines (about 4.9× fewer). Going from a 97M to a 317M to a 725M generator improved quality at a fixed token budget. Wall-clock times were measured generating 1,024 sequences at batch size 16 on one H100. Code is at github.com/HazyResearch/ncm.

Takeaway. NCM's case is made against diffusion models, not against autoregressive decoding, so it is not yet a drop-in replacement for an AR model. It is worth watching for tasks that need global planning or a fixed output length. The residual-codebook plus nested-dropout design is also a reusable way to get representations you can read at coarse or fine detail, for example to compress agent memories.

ChatGPT Desktop reportedly forwards your local SSH agent to remote agent hosts

Why read · If your agent runs on a remote box, your forwarded SSH agent lets it push to your private repos without asking you, so you need to think about where the key confirmation happens.

The HN post is short. It says that when ChatGPT Desktop drives an agent on a remote machine, it forwards your local SSH agent (the process that holds your unlocked private keys and signs auth requests for other programs). The remote agent can then authenticate as you, for example to push to private GitHub repos. The only evidence is a review comment on openai/codex PR #28131. One commenter notes that this PR was closed, not merged, so the source doesn't settle how the shipping product behaves. The discussion: agent forwarding is a standard SSH feature used with jump hosts (intermediate bastion servers), but it was designed for humans and scripts. Agent guardrails tend to rely on blacklists, which leak, so tools as broad as ssh, kubectl or gcloud need real sandboxing. The suggested fix is to require a human approval for each signature.

Under the hood

  • With agent forwarding, the private key stays on your laptop, but any process on the remote host that can reach the forwarded socket can ask it to sign. That access lasts as long as the session.
  • Commenter fix: on macOS, keep SSH keys in the Secure Enclave and require a fingerprint for every new session, so an agent can't open connections without you noticing.
  • Another option: the Bitwarden desktop app's built-in SSH agent can ask for confirmation each time a key is requested.

Takeaway. If agents run on remote hosts, don't forward an SSH agent that signs without asking. Use keys that need a human approval for each signature (Secure Enclave with a fingerprint, or Bitwarden confirmations), or give the agent its own repo-scoped deploy key.

Google adds TPU 8tx for confidential inference and dual-die TPU 8i Max

Why read · The chips themselves are thin on specs; the useful part for agent and memory work is the software, mainly the open-sourced TPU Sync KV-cache tiering layer and idle-session suspension.

Google has added two systems to its eighth TPU generation, alongside TPU 8t (training) and 8i (inference) from April. TPU 8tx uses the 8t silicon with 5th Gen AMD EPYC hosts. It keeps data and weights protected while they sit in memory being processed (confidential computing) and uses 8i's low-latency Boardfly network. Google claims up to 2x lower latency than 8t with a "near-zero" performance penalty. TPU 8i Max puts two 8i dies on one package, doubling compute, SRAM and HBM bandwidth in the same rack footprint. The post gives no absolute FLOPs, memory sizes, prices or $/token figures, and HN commenters pointed this out. The more relevant news is software: TPU Sync (open-sourced) spreads KV cache across HBM, host DRAM and shared storage, and the Agent Compute Engine suspends agent sessions while they sit idle.

Under the hood

  • TPU Sync treats chip HBM, host DRAM, shared storage and the network as one memory hierarchy. It moves KV caches off-chip in milliseconds, streams context between disaggregated prefill and decode nodes, and syncs RL weights. Google claims it cuts KV transfer latency and TTFT by more than 90%. An HN commenter compared it to open-source projects such as Strata for Qwen models.
  • GKE dynamic slicing uses optical circuit switching to join TPU cubes into larger slices or split them into smaller ones without rebuilding nodepools. Google claims job startup up to 5x faster and fault recovery up to 4.5x faster. Cloud Pathways, the single-controller runtime behind Gemini, is now generally available on GKE, with sub-minute slice hot-swapping and elastic resharding.
  • TorchTPU is open source and comes with TPU backends for vLLM and SGLang. There is also a new open-source XLA Serving Engine: you train in Python and serve from a C++ runtime on TPU, GPU or CPU.

Takeaway. If you serve stateful agents, the design pattern is the part to copy: move KV cache from HBM to DRAM to storage, and suspend sessions while agents wait on tools or people. Don't size anything from the hardware claims until Google publishes per-chip specs and $/token numbers.

Anthropic ships Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, cheaper and up to 30% faster

Why read · Anthropic's top tier gets cheaper and faster, and the launch pushes a routing setup where Fable advises or orchestrates smaller models. Both matter if you run long-horizon agents.

Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2. They are the same underlying model with different access. Fable carries cybersecurity and biology safeguards and is open to all users. Mythos has those safeguards relaxed and is available only to organizations accepted into the Life Sciences and Cyber Verification Programs. Anthropic says 5.2 generates output up to 30% faster than 5.1 and is priced lower, at $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens. The announcement doesn't state the 5.1 price. An optional fast mode makes token generation up to 2.5x faster for $16/$80, double the base price.

Anthropic doesn't present it as a default model. It recommends starting with Opus 5.5 as the 'daily driver', moving up to Fable or Mythos for ambitious, open-ended technical work, and moving down to Sonnet 5.5 or Haiku 5.5 for simpler work. Fable 5.2 can also run in two multi-model roles. As Advisor, smaller Haiku, Sonnet or Opus models consult it on hard problems. As Orchestrator, it hands simpler tasks to smaller models. On cost, at Medium effort (the default in Claude apps and Claude Code) it needs fewer tokens than 5.1 for hard tasks. On FrontierCode 2.0, Fable 5.2 at High effort scored about level with Opus 5.5 at Max effort for roughly half the cost per task. At Xhigh it scored highest of any released model tested. The other benchmarks named are WANDR (agentic search), BenchCAD 2.1 (hardware engineering), Humanity's Last Exam and Chartography (chart reading). The fetched text includes no scores for them, only the claim that it is 'a step above Opus 5.5 in almost all' of them.

The capability claims come from Anthropic's own prerelease use, not independent testing. It says over 90% of changes merged into its core codebase are now written by Claude, with human guidance. A team of agents running an earlier 5.2 checkpoint migrated a production service from Python to Rust, and the service now starts about 26x faster. In one experiment, Fable 5.2 coordinated more than 1,000 subagents over eight days, starting from an empty folder, and produced an operating system that boots on a laptop. LinkedIn's Firepool keeps GPU sandboxes warm for agent and RL loops

Why read · A production design for tool-call, verifier and rollout sandboxes that takes pod startup off the critical path without leaking state between callers or holding idle GPUs.

Ankit Goyal, Santosh Jha, Zhuo Zhi, Youmin Han, Cong Gu, Chen Xie, Animesh Singh

Agent tool calls, verifiers and RL rollouts often run for a few seconds on a GPU, but a fresh pod takes 20–35 s to start on LinkedIn's H200 nodes even with the image cached. Most of that is platform init containers, including a 5 s credential fetch. With eight pods starting on one node it grows to about 50 s, and without a cached image it averages 1.3–7.5 minutes. Firepool runs one broker per Kubernetes cluster. Jobs are stored durably in MySQL. Live pods are tracked in an in-memory index behind a single read/write lock, and a dispatch loop hands each job to an agent running inside the pod.

Three ideas carry the design. The first is late binding: every hardware pool (SKU) uses one shared base image, and the job's code, extra Python dependencies and multi-pod topology (torchrun ranks or Ray daemons) are attached when the job starts. That costs about 0.4 s. The second is owner-only reuse: a used pod serves only the caller that ran on it last. When the caller starts a new session (a new lease_tag), its next job runs once on the old pod and then the pod is retired. Between jobs the pod agent kills the job's process tree, checks /proc to confirm it is gone, and wipes /dev/shm. If it cannot confirm a clean reset, the pod is deleted. The third is zero-VRAM standby: up to three idle pods hold fractional scheduler shares on an active pod's GPU, with no CUDA context and no GPU memory allocated, ready to take over the card in under a second.

Pools scale on queue length, not utilization, because warm pods sit idle by design. Over a 14-hour production peak, 94.7% of 3,206 pod requests were served without creating a pod. Against a fresh pod per job, agent loops finished 13× faster and RL loops 10.8× faster. Standby promotion cut the handover wait from 51 s to 0.94 s. The authors list the limits: custom images dominate the 8-GPU pools and get no benefit, standby does not yet work for multi-GPU gangs, and recovery has been tested against broker crashes but not network partitions.

Under the hood

  • Claim path: finding a pod, checking quotas and recording the lease all happen under one write lock, held 1.5–2.6 μs at the median (≤8 μs p99) with 100k tracked pods. That supports 110k–220k claims/s at about 1 KB per pod. The real throughput limit is the version-checked MySQL commit (483 jobs/s), and no user code runs before it succeeds. A per-SKU lock design was rejected because some rules span SKUs: per-caller GPU limits, per-accelerator budgets and standby exclusivity.
  • Placement order inside a SKU: an idle pod last used by the same caller, then a never-used idle pod, then a standby on an active GPU, then a new pod. Requests are rounded up to the smallest SKU that fits, which keeps lookups constant-time. Exact shapes would need a best-fit search under the lock.
  • Standby on H200 uses HAMi with Volcano for fractional GPU shares. The pod agent lifts the per-GPU memory cap only in the job process's environment, not the pod spec, so the job can use all 141 GB. A per-GPU gate lets one pod run at a time, and an active pod plus its standbys count as one physical GPU in every budget.
  • Scaling rule, run every 30 s: add = min(need, ceiling−physical, step, headroom), where need = max(floor−physical, demand−eligible, 0). Pods that are still starting count as supply, so the controller does not launch a new wave each pass while pods take 22–200 s to come up. Pods held for their owner count toward the floor and ceiling but are not offered to other callers.
  • Cost: a 60–120 s keep-alive holds 0.48–0.68× the capacity of an always-on pool and adds +0.01 to +0.27 s median latency. A 900 s keep-alive with no idle cap holds 1.15×. In the simulator, with 64 environments, separate pools per environment held 2.15× capacity against 1.06× for one shared pool. Warm pools stop paying off once job bodies run past about 200 s for RL or 100 s for agents.

Takeaway. If you build sandboxes for agents or RL, keep one generic warm pool per hardware type and attach code, dependencies and topology when each job starts. Scale on queue length, not utilization, and count pods that are still starting as supply. Reuse a pod only for the same caller and session, and delete any pod whose reset you cannot verify.

GPU debugging has mostly meant vendor-specific forks such as AMD's ROCgdb and NVIDIA's CUDA-GDB, so each vendor rebuilds common debugger features on its own. Meta's approach puts an accelerator plugin interface inside lldb-server. That is LLDB's companion process: it launches or attaches to the program and talks to the LLDB client over the GDB remote protocol, which covers memory reads, registers, breakpoints and execution control. The plugin's job is to translate a vendor's device-debug API into concepts LLDB already has: processes, threads, registers, memory, modules and stop reasons. The client stays vendor-neutral, and vendors only write the hardware-specific part. CPU-only programs keep using the existing native path, and the post says this adds no runtime overhead.

The key design choice is to avoid one merged process model. Mixing the two would make every register read, memory access, breakpoint and step depend on which device is selected, and it would force one memory map even though 0x1000 can mean different locations on the host and the device. So the CPU and the GPU become two separate LLDB targets, each with its own architecture, threads, registers and address space. Both still show up in one LLDB or IDE session. The session is set up like this. LLDB discovers the available plugins. A plugin asks for internal breakpoints in the host process at runtime events, for example the point where a GPU debug session can begin. When the host hits one, the plugin initializes the vendor API and returns connection info. LLDB then creates and connects the device target. In the HIP dot-product demo, target #0 is x86_64 and target #1 is amdgcn. A pending breakpoint on compute_element resolves when the GPU module loads, and bt shows GPU frames with idx=32.

Most of the common framework is already upstream in LLVM: the plugin interface, the GDB-remote packets for discovery, initialization, event coordination and GPU connection setup, pairing of CPU and GPU processes, multiple address spaces, and mock tests. The post says the remaining pieces will follow soon. GPU core dumps, which allow postmortem debugging, are supported through a common base class. Meta says GPU debugging work across its mixed GPU fleet already uses this model.

Under the hood

  • The plugin boundary is defined in terms of debugger operations (breakpoints, connections, modules, execution sync), not in terms of any GPU platform. This is what lets one interface cover very different instruction sets and runtimes.
  • Coordination between host and device uses structured actions. Plugins request CPU breakpoints, provide connection info, report device modules and synchronize execution, and the client never sees vendor API details.
  • A mock accelerator plugin lets the LLVM test suite check discovery, breakpoint coordination, connection creation and basic device state without any GPU hardware.
  • Support for multiple address spaces means a host pointer and a device pointer with the same numeric value are never confused.
  • GPU core-dump plugins share a common base class for LLDB integration. Vendors supply the device thread, register, memory and module state.

Takeaway. When two subsystems have truly different models (address spaces, execution semantics), give each its own instance of an existing abstraction and coordinate them with a small protocol, rather than merging them behind conditionals. Build a mock backend early so the shared layer can be tested without the real hardware.

Why read · A NeurIPS 2026 paper shows that the usual way multi-turn GRPO assigns credit can make tool-call tokens net-negative, then fixes it with three small changes to the objective.

The TMLR Group at Hong Kong Baptist University (Bo Han) studies why tool-integrated RL with MT-GRPO gets worse with training. MT-GRPO copies each trajectory's final reward to every turn in that trajectory. Accuracy first rises, then drops below plain non-agentic GRPO, and the share of tool calls that execute successfully falls. They prove that the total credit on tool-call tokens is negative whenever incorrect trajectories contain more tool tokens per turn than correct ones, even when most trajectories are correct. Two effects make this worse. Token-mean loss averaging gives longer trajectories more weight, and those are often the wrong ones. And when a call fails, the model retries, so the retries inherit the negative credit even when they execute fine. TACA adds three fixes. Advantage Projection shifts tool-token advantages up just enough that their mean within each prompt group is not negative. Decision Contrast compares rewards of tool-using and direct-answer rollouts and nudges the advantage on the opening markers <python_code> and <answer> accordingly. Supervised Replay adds a likelihood loss on tool calls taken from earlier correct trajectories.

Under the hood

  • Mechanism: total tool credit C_tool = (Mσ/N)(m̄⁺ − m̄⁻), where M is the number of generated turns, N the number of tokens in the loss, σ the reward standard deviation, and m̄⁺/m̄⁻ the tool tokens per turn in correct and incorrect trajectories. The sign therefore depends only on whether wrong rollouts have more tool tokens per turn. The extra bias from token-mean versus trajectory-mean averaging is Cov(L, (m/L)·A)/E[L]: it pushes credit down when longer trajectories carry less tool credit per token.
  • Results versus MT-GRPO: Qwen2.5-1.5B averages 33.24% across six math benchmarks (+4.61 points) and Qwen2.5-3B 43.51% (+3.61). On the 1.5B model, AIME24 goes from 3.81% to 8.43% and AIME25 from 1.38% to 8.12% against the strongest baseline. TACO unit-test score rises from 41.98 to 53.70, WebShop success from 56.8% to 67.2%, Qwen2.5-7B from 42.59% to 45.97%, and Llama-3.2-1B from 13.61% to 15.17%. Tool-execution success during training is 86.28% versus 79.45% for ReTool. Ablations on the 1.5B model: without Advantage Projection the average falls to 30.27%, without Decision Contrast to 30.58%, and without replay it drops 3.03 points.
  • Setup and limits: 4 A800 GPUs, 32 prompts × 8 rollouts per batch, learning rate 1e-6, at most 6 turns of 2,048 tokens each, 3-second code-execution timeout, training on MATH through AlphaApollo, terminal rewards only. All models are 7B or smaller, and math uses a single code-execution tool. Baselines are GRPO, ReTool, MT-GRPO, GiGPO and SimpleTIR. Code: github.com/tmlr-group/TACA.

Takeaway. If you train agents with GRPO-style terminal rewards, log the mean advantage on tool-call tokens for each prompt group and batch, and compare token-mean with trajectory-mean averaging. If tool credit is going negative, clamping it with a uniform shift is a one-line fix that keeps the relative differences between trajectories.

Deletion Tuning: zeroing under 0.05% of weights matches full fine-tuning

Why read · If post-training can work by only choosing which pretrained weights to drop, you get adapters a few MB in size and much less forgetting, which matters for continual learning in agent memory.

Jack Lu, Zhenbang Yang, Mike Lasby, Tejas Pote, Yani Ioannou, Mengye Ren

This NeurIPS 2026 paper comes from NYU (Mengye Ren's Agentic Learning lab) and Calgary (Yani Ioannou). It trains only a binary mask over the gate_proj weight of each SwiGLU MLP block and leaves every other weight frozen. Each weight either keeps its pretrained value or is set to zero. The method matches full fine-tuning (FFT) at the largest scale tested in three regimes: GRPO (RL with verifiable rewards), SFT, and on-policy self-distillation (SDFT). On GSM8K with Llama3-8B it scores 83.55% vs FFT's 83.24%. On SimpleRL-Zoo math with Qwen2.5-7B it scores 52.32% vs 51.89%. It also matches FFT on Qwen2.5-14B and on the Qwen3-30B-A3B mixture-of-experts model. Prior-task accuracy stays within 1 point of the base model, while FFT, LoRA and random sparse tuning lose up to 5.4 points. The catches: smaller backbones recover only 90–99% of FFT, and training stores one logit per masked weight, so it needs more training memory than LoRA.

Under the hood

  • How it trains: there is one logit G per weight, and the training-time weight is W·σ(α·G). The sharpness α ramps linearly from 1e3 to 1e4 so the soft mask converges to the hard mask 1[G>0]. Rollouts use the hard mask. Logits are initialised from Wanda pruning scores (weight magnitude × input-activation norm), with about 0.18% of weights deleted at the start. A small regulariser (λ=1e-5) favours keeping large weights, and the final sparsity is not fixed. Switching from soft to hard mask at deployment changes accuracy by at most 0.08 points.
  • Storage: the adapter is a bit-packed mask compressed with zstd. It is 1.0 MB for Llama 1B and 8.4 MB for Llama 8B, which is 3,600–4,700× smaller than an FFT checkpoint and smaller than rank-1 LoRA. That works out to about 0.03 bits per masked weight. Switching tasks means writing zeros into the base weights.
  • Ablations and geometry: Wanda pruning alone, with no training, stays within 2.4 points of the base model, while the trained mask gains up to 53.2 points, so the learning does the work. Forward KL (divergence from the base model on the new task, a known predictor of forgetting) stays ≤0.05, versus 1.0–1.5 for FFT. Random sparse tuning edits about the same share of weights (0.05% vs 0.035–0.046%) yet its KL reaches 0.4–1.3, which points to deletion itself, not edit count, as the cause of the low forgetting. The weight changes look like FFT's. Stable rank, roughly how many directions an update spans, is 66–93; LoRA's is 1–2 and random sparse tuning's is 410–650. About 0.43–0.46 of the changed positions fall on the base model's largest-magnitude weights, versus 0.41–0.46 for FFT and 0.5 for random positions.

Takeaway. If you need many per-task or per-user adaptations of one base model without losing what it already knows, a learned binary mask on gate_proj is a cheap baseline to try before LoRA. Track forward KL on the new task as an early warning for forgetting.

ByteDance Seed's HiRes widens the residual stream without widening the layers

Why read · This adds a scaling knob, residual width, that the abstract says buys about 1.3x FLOPs efficiency using only dense projections and norms, with no custom kernels.

Ziteng Wang, Jianqiao Lu, Xinyu Ma, Jintao Zhang, Jun Zhu, Yiyuan Ma, Jianfei Chen — ByteDance Seed

In a standard Transformer, the residual stream (the state carried from layer to layer) has the same width as the compute inside each layer. ByteDance Seed's Hierarchical Residuals (HiRes) separates the two. Layers are grouped into blocks. Inside a block, ordinary d-wide PreNorm layers (normalization applied before each sublayer) run unchanged. Between blocks, a wider D-wide PostNorm residual (normalization applied after the add) carries the state. Learned read and write projections move between the two widths at block boundaries. The authors' fitted scaling laws imply 1.30x FLOPs efficiency over plain residual addition across model sizes and depths. On OLMoE-1B-7B pretrained for 500B tokens, HiRes gets 0.0208 lower training loss and 1.14 points higher downstream accuracy. They call it competitive with VWN, mHC and AttnRes, other methods that also widen the residual, at lower activation memory and I/O cost. Only the abstract was available. It gives no D/d ratios, block sizes or names for the downstream tasks.

Under the hood

  • Individual Transformer layers are untouched. All of the change sits at block boundaries, in the read/write projections and the outer PostNorm residual.
  • It is built only from dense projections and normalizations, so it needs no specialized kernels or infrastructure changes. Per the abstract, it uses less activation memory and I/O than VWN, mHC and AttnRes.
  • Reported OLMoE-1B-7B result at 500B tokens: −0.0208 training loss and +1.14 downstream accuracy points versus vanilla residuals.

Takeaway. If you train or fine-tune your own architectures, residual width is a cheap variable to sweep separately from model width. Before adopting it, check the full paper for the block size, the D/d ratio and the memory overhead.

StepFun trains expert merge weights inside multi-teacher distillation

Why read · A small reparameterization lets a distilled student reach the lagging expert's capability that ordinary on-policy distillation never moves toward.

Sinuo Wang, Jiacheng Lu, Zeyu Zhang, Maojia Song, Rui Sun, Beidi Luan, Yutong Xie, Jing Li et al. — StepFun

StepFun and Adelaide University study multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). In MOPD, a student samples its own responses and the expert for each prompt's domain scores them token by token. The paper finds that MOPD leaves some domains behind. P2-MOPD changes how each linear projection is parameterized: W_m = B_m + Σ_k α_{k,m} τ_{k,m}, where τ_k is a frozen task vector (expert weights minus base weights). The full weights B and one learnable mixing scalar per expert per module α are trained together under the same MOPD loss. This differs from task arithmetic, a training-free merge with fixed coefficients that can only reach points inside the experts' span. On Qwen3-1.7B and 4B across medicine, law, finance and instruction following, 1.7B reaches 94.7% average recovery against 80.9% for MOPD started from the same merge. Worst-domain recovery rises from 32.0 to 88.0 (1.7B) and from 47.2 to 89.4 (4B). The limits: only 100 steps, small models, and every expert must be fine-tuned from a shared base.

Under the hood

  • Why it works: weight-only MOPD moves just +0.03 along the medical task vector, while P2-MOPD moves +0.40 to +0.42, 13 to 15 times further. Each learnable scalar shifts a whole module along that vector. Through the medical coefficient, MedQA recovery rises by +48.2 points at 1.7B. Learning only the medical coefficient recovers almost all of the gain (95.0 vs 95.8). Random directions in place of task vectors do no better than MOPD.
  • Both channels are needed. With frozen weights, learning only the coefficients improves on the uniform merge by +10.0 and +16.9 points at 4B and 1.7B. The full method gains +51.9 and +62.3. Both channels use the same gradient: the coefficient gradient is the inner product <G_m, τ_k,m>, so there is no second backward pass.
  • Cost: about 10^3 extra scalars, folded into a standard checkpoint, so inference is unchanged. At 4B the actor update grows from 14.7 s to 73.8 s, but rollout generation dominates each step, so 100 steps cost 1.24x (4B) and 1.09x (1.7B) the wall-clock of MOPD. P2-MOPD matches MOPD's final quality at 0.31x and 0.55x of its wall-clock.

Takeaway. When you combine several fine-tunes of the same base, try keeping the task vectors as frozen directions with learnable per-module scales while still training the full weights. Also watch the learned coefficients: they show which expert the student is failing to absorb, without using any validation data.
