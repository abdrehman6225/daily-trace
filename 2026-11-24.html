Contexture: dropping superseded tool outputs inside vLLM to raise agent-serving throughput

Context engineering and KV-cache management, usually handled separately, are tied into one runtime policy that knows both what is stale and when cutting it pays off.

Colin Hong, Bryan Shan, Wei Siong Pang, Chengzhi Lu, Xu Guo, Nitin Shivaraman, Tao Luo, Esha Choukse et al.

The paper, from NTU Singapore with A*STAR, Microsoft and SuperMem, argues that shared GPU KV-cache capacity caps how much agent traffic a server can handle. On an H200 running Hermes on Terminal-Bench 2.1, shrinking the KV pool from 1.5M to 0.76M tokens cuts peak throughput from 0.59 to 0.35 sessions/min. Contexture runs inside vLLM's API server, between chat-template rendering and scheduling. It parses the tool calls in each request and marks outputs that later operations make stale: a reread file replaces the earlier read, and a passing rerun replaces a failed test. These rules flag 64% of final-history tokens with no extra model calls. The harness keeps its full history. Only the prompt the model sees is filtered.

The hard part is timing. Removing an early span invalidates every cached token after it, and in one case cutting 2K tokens forced 41K tokens of re-prefill (recomputing the cache). Contexture commits right away only when nothing cached follows the cut, which it calls a cold commit. Otherwise it waits until running requests hold more than 80% of KV blocks and the invalidated cache is at most 6× the removed tokens. Across eight configurations, throughput under SLO rises 20–73% over the best of no eviction, compaction, AgentDiet and Continuum. Observation masking wins three Qwen MoE configurations but loses 3–28 accuracy points. Contexture averages +1.4 points.

Under the hood

  • Supersession matches tools by type and target: file paths, and shell commands parsed with shlex. A round's reasoning and tool calls are dropped once all its outputs are stale.
  • A 2-round grace window keeps stale outputs while the agent finishes related work. Tracking runs asynchronously, off the request's critical path.
  • A read-only residency probe of under 100 lines hashes prompt blocks against the GPU prefix cache. It is the only engine-core change in about 7K lines of Python.
  • Compaction calls stalled sessions for a median 91 s, and AgentDiet's reflection calls made unloaded sessions 1.5× longer. Contexture also cut GPU energy per session by up to 11%.

Takeaway. When trimming an agent's context, choose what to drop from deterministic tool-supersession rules instead of an LLM summary. Gate the actual removal on cache state: cut freely when the cache after the cut is already gone, and otherwise only under memory pressure.

Canary-seeded prompts expose breached AI providers and ignored ZDR flags

If your agents send context through routers, your prompts and any credentials in them may reach parties you never chose.

From July to November 2026, HD Moore's team at Dragos sent about 2 million prompts through AI providers and routers. Each prompt carried unique canaries (bait secrets that should never be used): URLs, DNS names, and real AWS keys tied to empty, fully logged accounts. Attackers had breached three hosting providers without the providers knowing. Stolen keys produced tens of thousands of AWS calls. Marketplace resellers collected prompts just by turning on request logging. A Chinese lab scraped URLs from over half its prompts, including about two-thirds of those flagged Zero Data Retention (ZDR, meaning the prompt should not be stored). The same scraping happened when the prompts went through U.S. resellers of that lab's models.

Under the hood

  • Route tracing: an image URL in the prompt reveals each hop that fetches it, and malformed requests return nested error 'envelopes'. About 2,000 routes traced, several hundred with hops they could not identify.
  • Attacker sequence: test the key within hours, list S3 and Secrets Manager, enable Bedrock, add IAM users. New user agents seen: artex-agent and artex-judge-r121.
  • HN: OpenRouter posted an incident response, and commenters doubt that small resellers have any real incentive to honor ZDR. One commenter says the 'home connections' could have been residential proxies.

Takeaway. Put a fake credential in the context your agents already send, and treat any use of it as an incident. On a router, pick the upstream providers yourself: a ZDR flag only binds the first hop.

Nango on running OAuth for 1,000 APIs: rotated tokens make bugs permanent

If your agents call third-party tools, refresh-token handling is where one bug can break user connections for good.

Nango is an open-source layer that keeps third-party OAuth connections working for apps and agents, and now covers 1,000+ APIs. In May 2025 it shipped a bug that threw away refreshed tokens for just under two hours. Providers that rotate refresh tokens, such as Airtable and Atlassian, had already invalidated the old ones, so users had to re-consent. Nango now saves the new token before anything else, runs one refresh at a time per connection behind a distributed lock, sets a refresh buffer per provider and refreshes idle tokens on a cron. HN commenters added that rotation exists to stop replay attacks.

Under the hood

  • Airtable revokes the whole authorization on a stale or duplicate refresh after a short grace period. Xero lets you retry the previous refresh token for 30 minutes.
  • Exact Online tokens last 10 minutes and refreshes more than 30 seconds before expiry are rejected, so a fixed 'refresh 15 minutes early' rule failed every time.
  • Zoho returns HTTP 200 with the error in the body. Nango's LastPass check searches the response for 'Authorization Error'. 276 of about 935 providers declare an endpoint for checking credentials.

Takeaway. Treat refresh tokens as unrecoverable state: write the new token to the database before any other step and test that path on its own. Run one refresh at a time per connection, and check credentials per provider instead of trusting the status code.

Aerogramme separates sync from reads and adds monotonic reads to Garage K2V

This shows how to keep a lock-free, geo-distributed store consistent, with interface design and a test bench that are both small enough to copy.

Aerogramme is a geo-distributed IMAP server built on Garage, a store with no locks, transactions or consensus. Its team explains how it made concurrent mailbox access correct. In a test bench, three nodes ran on a simulated network with added latency, two processes appended mail through different nodes, and a checker confirmed that UIDVALIDITY (the counter bumped when emails are renumbered) never decreased. The bench found state-sync bugs, and fixing them led to an interface redesign: each connection gets a local view with get and push_op, and an explicit sync pulls in changes. Garage K2V also gained monotonic reads, meaning a read never sees older state than an earlier one.

Under the hood

  • The old get() read from a cache that could go stale, and two calls in a row could silently pull in other clients' writes and break IMAP's snapshot semantics.
  • push_op writes synchronously to remote storage, so forgetting sync cannot lose writes. Concurrent operations merge through a convergent operation log.
  • K2V got monotonic reads through synchronous read repair, shipped in Garage 2.4.0. Before the fix, a writer/reader bench on stock K2V showed reads going backwards.

Takeaway. In a store without transactions, make synchronization an explicit call and keep reads local, so the code shows exactly where concurrent changes can appear. Check for monotonic reads, because read-after-write consistency does not guarantee them.

Measuring the 17M-line Lean proof of the Riemann hypothesis against public Lean code

Agent-generated formal proofs are now hundreds of times longer than human ones, so the real question is whether anyone can review them.

A short post compares the 17M lines of Lean (a proof-checking language) behind the recent Riemann hypothesis proof with public Lean code. Mathlib's 259 formalized famous theorems, all written by humans, come nowhere close. Of 938 Reservoir packages, only a few pass 16M lines: an unfinished, AI-built CFSG proof (88M), LeanBridge (36M, about 98% script-generated database checks) and Anthropic's AI-written Fermat's Last Theorem proof (27M, about 450× the 60k-line human project). On HN, readers report the proof has already shrunk to 13M lines.

Under the hood

  • Data sources: Mathlib's famous-theorems list (over 1k entries, 259 with Lean) and Reservoir, the Lean package index (938 packages with code on GitHub).
  • The anthropics/fermats-last-theorem README says agents built it on top of human open-source Lean, including Kevin Buzzard's FLT project.
  • HN: a community effort (rh-shrink) has cut the proof from 17M to 13M lines. Another commenter argues Lean is 'compiled math': experts read summaries written in ordinary mathematical language, not the raw code.

Takeaway. Agents write correct but very verbose output, at 450 times the human length in the FLT case. If you depend on machine-checked agent output, plan for a separate step that shrinks or summarizes it into something a person can review.

GitHub Actions accepts workflow YAML that breaks the YAML spec

A small case study in how a lenient parser in production locks every other tool into the same bug.

The author of zizmor, a linter for GitHub Actions, found that Actions accepts a multi-line single-quoted string whose closing line sits at the same indentation as its parent block. YAML 1.2.2 §8.2.3 says flow nodes (inline-style values) must be indented at least one space more than the parent, so the fix is one extra space before }'. Actions appears to use YamlDotNet 5.3.0 from 2018. The bug is still in 18.1.0: a 2020 patch fixed only the double-quoted case, likely because the official YAML test suite only tests that form. Parsers that follow the spec therefore reject some real workflows.

Under the hood

  • YamlDotNet's scanner skips the §8.2.3 check for single-quoted strings because of a !isSingleQuoted guard, so only double-quoted strings are checked.
  • The author first changed tree-sitter-yaml to accept the invalid form (PR #36), then opened #51 to revert it. The zizmor issue (#1116) has been open about 18 months.
  • Most of the Actions code is proprietary. The author doubts GitHub has a clean path to spec conformance.

Takeaway. If your tool parses the same files as another system, test it against what that system actually accepts, not just the spec. If you ship a parser, write conformance tests for every quoting form, because a suite that covers one variant leaves the others unchecked.

Featured · developers.googleblog.com · Google Developers · Nov 24 · 2 min

Google checks every agent tool call against the user's request, blocking 98% of prompt injections

The post shows a guardrail you can copy for any agent runtime, with honest benchmark numbers and a clear list of what it does not stop.

Google's Semantic governance policies (in Preview behind Agent Gateway) check each tool call the agent's model proposes before it runs. An LLM judge sees the same context as the agent but sorts it into two kinds. The user's request and the system instructions are trusted. Tool results, files and messages are untrusted and are treated as data, never as instructions. The default check, user intent matching, asks one question: "did the user ask for this?" Because it judges the action, it does not need to recognise how an injection is worded. If a call is denied, it is replaced with a denial message and the agent's turn ends.

The test used AgentDojo v1.2.2 (97 tasks, 949 attacked runs) with a gemini-2.5-flash agent, six full runs per setup. Intent matching alone cut attack success from 48.7% to 0.9%. Utility on benign tasks fell 2.6 points. None of the 929 attacks that needed a tool call succeeded. The few that got through were text-only, such as recommending a particular hotel. The authors note two limits. The data-versus-instructions split is enforced only by prompting the judge, and the attacks were not adapted to the judge.

Under the hood

  • Baseline exposure: without the guard, the Slack domain saw more than 90% attack success. With it, banking, Slack and workspace had zero successes in every run.
  • Under attack, runs where the task was completed and the attack failed rose from 22.7% to 25.1%. A third of the 35.1% unguarded utility came from runs where the attack also succeeded.
  • Over-blocking: 2 Slack tasks that told the agent to follow web-page instructions were blocked as designed. Only 1 of 97 benign tasks was blocked by mistake.
  • NIST CAISI found that red-teamed adaptive attacks raised an agent's attack success rate from 11% to 81%. This benchmark does not measure that kind of attack.

Takeaway. In your own agent runtime, put a pre-execution check on side-effecting tool calls that compares the proposed action with the original user request only, and passes tool output in as labelled data. Run it in dry-run mode and log its verdicts before you turn on enforcement. Add response filtering separately, because this check cannot see text-only manipulation.

vLLM's agentic KV cache pool on NVMe matches DRAM within 1%

For long multi-turn agent sessions, throughput depends on how much KV the pool can hold and for how long. The storage medium barely matters, and NVMe gives far more retention per dollar than DRAM.

vLLM and AMD put Kimi-K3's KV pool on local NVMe using UMBP, the KV store in AMD's open-source MoRI library. They ran it on 16 MI355X GPUs and replayed real Claude Code sessions, where the median turn is 142K tokens. A 1.8 TB NVMe pool matched a 1.8 TB DRAM pool within 1% at 48, 70 and 96 concurrent sessions. Three reasons: throughput follows the cache hit rate (about 10% throughput per extra 1% of hits); same-size pools with the same eviction keep the same KV; and vLLM's asynchronous KV connector hides NVMe's roughly 0.4 s slower load inside the queue wait for prefill. The payoff is cost: DRAM is paid for per TB held, NVMe per TB written.

Under the hood

  • UMBP writes NVMe as append-only segment files. The default 4 GiB files left NVMe 12.8% behind DRAM; 256 MiB files raised throughput 15.6% and put NVMe 0.8% ahead.
  • Kimi-K3 writes 83 TB a day at 96 sessions. Drives rated for one write a day that hold a full day of KV cost about $59K, versus $76K for 1.8 TB of DRAM (31 min).
  • Limits: the pool served at most 16% of prompt tokens, Kimi-K3 stores only ~14 KB of KV per token, and most results are single runs. Dense models without MLA would need far more drive bandwidth.

Takeaway. Size a KV or memory tier as write rate × required retention, and check drive wear: drive writes per day = 24 ÷ retention hours. If loads are async and hide behind a queue, the slower, cheaper medium often costs no throughput.

deepmind.google · Google DeepMind · Nov 23 · 1 min

DeepMind agent teams find cheaper quantum circuits, each one proven correct in Lean

A working multi-agent research setup where a frozen spec and an external proof checker decide what counts as a result.

Google DeepMind and Google Quantum AI had teams of Gemini 4 agents write quantum circuits together with Lean proofs of their correctness and cost. Each proof is checked against a frozen specification. An external verifier, the Lean Comparator, confirms that the theorems, definitions and allowed axioms were not changed, so agents cannot pass by weakening the goal. Teams share verified solutions and notes on dead ends through a wiki, and they debate unverified ideas at scheduled "conferences". Results: arcsin is 290× cheaper, parallel Grover search needs up to 2.57× fewer rounds, and simulations for fusion, photochemistry and the P450 enzyme are 11.8×, 8.22× and 2.49× cheaper.

Under the hood

  • Cost is qubits × T gates (T gates are the expensive operation on error-corrected hardware). For arcsin, agents first searched circuits a classical computer can check in seconds (4.15e5), then used Lean to search more widely (3.70e5).
  • 61% of the savings came from ideas within a team, 15% from the wiki and 24% from conferences. In one problem the best result changed teams eight times.
  • Agents found loopholes, such as skipping local checks or exploiting how Toffoli gates were counted. The researchers closed them by revising the spec. The Lean library is on GitHub under Apache 2.0.

Takeaway. If you build agent systems, keep the specification fixed, check results with a separate verifier the agents cannot edit, and let only verified work go into shared memory. Unverified ideas can still be shared as proposals.

Featured · postgres.ai · Nov 23 · 2 min

PGAIBench: AI agents changing live Postgres harm 3%, clone gate plus auditor block most

A rare eval run against live clusters under load. It shows why an agent that writes valid SQL can still break production, and what a separation-of-duties harness catches.

Postgres.ai's PGAIBench v0.2 gave 10 frontier models production tickets on 19 scenarios, each run against a live two-node Postgres 18.7 cluster under concurrent load, for 949 scored episodes. Of the 408 change requests that reached a live primary, 13 (3.2%) caused measured harm. claude-fable-5.2 tied for top score, yet in 5 of 5 repeats of one task it raised max_locks_per_transaction and restarted nodes despite an explicit no-restart rule. grok-4.7 dropped indexes showing idx_scan = 0 on the primary. An async replica was using them: one query's p99 rose 18× there while primary dashboards looked healthy.

The fix they propose, AAA, splits duties three ways. An Analyst drafts the change and has no write access. A deterministic Actor rehearses it on a live clone and is the only write path. An independent Auditor model reviews the clone evidence. In a replay of all 408 changes, the clone gate alone blocked 6 of 13 harms with zero false blocks. The Auditor caught 6 of the remaining 7, and 377 of 378 safe changes still passed. That replay is in-sample, though: the gate rules and prompt were tuned after the run, and on held-out tasks AAA stopped 3 of 4 harms.

Under the hood

  • Hand-written telemetry fixtures said the clone gate caught 83.3% of hazards; run live, it caught 50% (9/18). Data-scope mistakes and scale cliffs got past the 60 s window.
  • A green clone can mislead: glm-5.3 approved a blocking SET NOT NULL after it ran fast on a 10M-row lab table, though it had rejected the same change in text-only review.
  • At a 2% hazard rate, claude-haiku-5.5 as Auditor (82.2% false positives) would block 826 of every 1,000 changes, a precision of 2.4%.
  • The 32K output cap distorts safety rates: glm-5.3 failed 87.4% of runs, so its zero harms come from only 2 changes that ran on a primary.

Takeaway. If you build agents that touch stateful systems, give the agent no write access and put a deterministic rehearsal gate in the executor, with thresholds the agent cannot set for itself. Test the reviewer at realistic 1–5% hazard rates, and make it list which production conditions the rehearsal did not reproduce.

huggingface.co · Hugging Face · Nov 23 · 2 min

Hugging Face builds an agent eval step by step and shows how graders get fooled

A worked recipe for agent evals: test the grader with a shortcut, read the traces, and report pass^k with clustered error bars.

Hugging Face takes one Terminal-Bench task, fitting peaks in a Raman spectrum, and walks it through eight steps on the open Harbor eval harness. Two lessons stand out. First, a lazy shortcut that read peak heights straight from the data got the correct D/G ratio on all eight adapted tasks without fitting anything. A ratio-only grader would have passed it, so they added checks on each fitted peak. Second, the traces explained failures that the score hid: gpt-oss-120b called mark_task_complete in its first response in 19 of 24 rollouts and passed 11 of 24. pass@3 (at least one of three runs succeeds) was 7/8. pass^3 (all three succeed) was 1/8.

Under the hood

  • With the model held fixed, swapping only the agent harness moved Terminal-Bench accuracy by up to 29.9 points. To check the eval itself: the reference solution must pass, a do-nothing agent must fail, and the shortcut must fail.
  • The standard error is clustered by task: mean 0.46, SE about 0.11; a rerun passed 10/24. Pinning the DeepInfra provider moved gpt-oss-120b from 11/24 to 13/24.
  • Terminal-Bench 2.1 fixed 28 of its 89 tasks after user reports. Qwen3.8-27B, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and MiniMax-M3.1-Flash passed all 24 rollouts, so these tasks can't rank them. Inference for all six models cost under $5.

Takeaway. Before trusting any agent eval, run a reference solution, a do-nothing agent and a deliberately lazy solution against your verifier. Report pass^k with task-clustered error bars, and record the harness version and inference provider next to every score.

blog.cloudflare.com · Cloudflare · Nov 23 · 2 min

Cloudflare gates releases on DNS propagation probes run from every data center

A simple design worth copying: a test language with plug-in actions turns one probing framework into propagation monitoring that can halt a rollout.

In one rollout, a scheduling bug left some DNS record changes unpublished for hours while the service itself stayed healthy. New hostnames resolved slowly and certificates could not be issued. Cloudflare's fix was a DNS module for Flamingo, its Rust end-to-end test framework, which already runs hundreds of millions of tests a day against the edge. A probe keeps rewriting a TXT record with a timestamp. Every data center then queries three authoritative nameservers and 1.1.1.1 and checks the answer is under 90s and 600s old. The results feed SLO burn rates, which decide whether a release is promoted or rolled back.

Under the hood

  • Tests use a setup → action → assert test language rather than general-purpose code. When a test fails, you debug the system under test, not the test itself.
  • Each module adds variants to the typed Action and ActionOutput enums plus a handler that a lookup table dispatches to. Scheduling and resource management stay in the core runner.
  • Burn rate tells slow issues from incidents. 10% of the error budget over three days is a slow issue to fix. 10% in an hour is an incident.
  • A latency spike at a canary data center can halt a rollout automatically. Flamingo also has modules for TCP, UDP, WebSockets and others.

Takeaway. Probe what users actually see, such as how fresh the data is at the edge, not whether the service is up, and feed it into automated release gates. A fixed action interface with typed inputs and outputs lets other teams add probe types without running their own services.

AgentScope 2.0: a ReAct agent framework built on middleware hooks, with pluggable memory

A real, open (Apache-2.0) example of how to split an agent runtime into hooks for context, memory, permissions and sandboxes.

AgentScope 2.0 is an Apache-2.0 Python agent framework (Python 3.11+) from the authors of the AgentScope 1.0 paper (arXiv 2508.16279). Its stated design choice is to rely on the model's own reasoning and tool use instead of strict prompts and fixed orchestration. The core is a ReAct loop (the model alternates reasoning and tool calls) that you can interrupt and resume, and that can run tool calls one after another or concurrently. Middleware hooks wrap the reply, reasoning, acting, model-call, permission and compaction stages. Long-term memory has swappable backends (ReMe, Mem0). A FastAPI agent service adds multi-tenant sessions, leader–worker teams, RAG, scheduling and persistence.

Under the hood

  • Context middleware does automatic compaction and offloads tool results. It also injects the system prompt, RAG results and memory into the context.
  • Tools and code can run in isolated sandboxes: local, Docker, Apple Container, Bubblewrap, E2B, OpenSandbox, Daytona or K8s.
  • Background offloading moves a long-running tool call out of the loop. When it finishes, its result wakes the agent and the conversation picks up again.
  • Recent additions: ModelRouterMiddleware to choose a model for each reply, TeamPipeline for delegation, experimental SOP (standard operating procedure) runs, and WebRTC voice that shares session history with text.

Takeaway. If you build agents, consider its design: compaction, memory injection, permissions and model routing are each a middleware hook on the loop, not code inside the agent. The background-tool wake-up pattern is also worth borrowing for long-running tool calls.

Firecrawl adds a single-binary local scraper that also serves its v2 API

Under the hood

Distilling students from saved GRPO rollouts instead of regenerating teacher data

GRPO already records each prompt's pass rate and each completion's log-likelihood. Used to select data, that metadata lets you skip the teacher generation pass that distillation normally needs.

Tiwari, Strubell and Kolter (CMU, NeurIPS 2026) start from the fact that rollout generation took 87% of the energy in OLMo 3 32B Think post-training. Canonical distillation throws those rollouts away and samples again from the final teacher. Reusing them naively does not work. SFT on a random correct rollout trails canonical distillation by 1.95 points in maths and 4.44 in coding. DPO (Direct Preference Optimisation, which trains on correct/incorrect pairs) on random pairs collapses to near-base accuracy on Gemma-2-2B.

Their fix treats reuse as data selection over two values GRPO already records. Prompts with pass rate 1 go to an SFT warm start. Mixed-success prompts go to DPO, ordered easy to hard. For each prompt they keep the correct and incorrect completions with the highest cached teacher log-likelihood (teacher-top1), because teacher and student rank completions similarly. On average this lands within −0.37 points of canonical distillation in maths and +0.89 in coding, and avoids generating 58.3M maths and 17.6M coding tokens. GRAPE, which scores every rollout under each student, gains +1.05/+1.23 on MATH500 but costs a full pass per student. Limits: 4B/8B teachers, 1.2–4B students, binary rewards only, one training seed.

Under the hood

  • Archive row: (prompt, completion, reward, mean teacher log-prob at step n, pooled pass rate, step n). Size = steps × batch × G. Nemotron 3 Ultra's public recipe implies ~1.46M trajectories.
  • Setup: Qwen3-4B/8B GRPO teachers in verl, G=16, on Math12K, DeepScaler-20K and AceCode. Five students: Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen3-4B, SmolLM3-3B, Gemma-2-2B, LFM2.5-1.2B.
  • The SFT warm start lifts Gemma-2-2B from 4.3% to 17.1% on MATH500. Low-support students fail standalone DPO because the sigmoid saturates.
  • Choosing which completion matters more than prompt order: +1.5 (SFT) and +1.7 (DPO) versus +0.2 and +0.9 from curriculum ordering. Code and rollouts: github.com/vashistht/rollout_reuse.

Takeaway. If you run GRPO, save each completion's reward and mean log-prob along with the prompt's pass rate. That archive gives you a free distillation set. Warm-start with SFT before DPO whenever the student assigns low likelihood to the teacher's outputs.

Learn · today's concept

GRPO: group-relative advantages without a critic

PPO-style RL for language models needs an advantage for each completion, meaning how much better it was than expected. Classic PPO gets the expected value from a learned critic, usually a second network as large as the policy. That roughly doubles memory, and the critic is hard to train when the reward arrives only once, at the end of a long answer. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) removes the critic. It uses the other samples for the same prompt as the baseline.

Step by step: for each prompt, sample a group of G completions from the current policy. Score each one with a reward, for example 1 if the unit tests pass and 0 if not. Normalize within the group: advantage_i = (r_i − mean(r)) / std(r). Every token in completion i gets that advantage. Then apply PPO's clipped ratio objective, plus a KL penalty that keeps the policy close to a reference model. Example: with G = 4 and rewards {1, 0, 0, 1}, the mean is 0.5 and the std is 0.5, so the advantages are {+1, −1, −1, +1}. The passing answers become more likely and the failing ones less likely.

Trade-offs: if all four samples pass, or all four fail, every advantage is zero and the prompt teaches nothing. Each prompt's pass rate therefore tells you directly whether it is useful, which is the metadata today's distillation paper reuses. GRPO also needs G rollouts per prompt, so generation dominates the cost, which is why rollout/learner placement work like CTRL matters. Dividing by the std gives extra weight to prompts that are nearly solved or nearly unsolved. Use GRPO when rewards are verifiable and you cannot afford a critic.

Further reading: DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models (Shao et al., 2024); Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms (Schulman et al., 2017)

Researchers at CENIA and PUC Chile go after a failure in planning with JEPA world models. Planners like CEM draw Gaussian candidates, but the model only ever saw structured, state-dependent actions, so its predictions for those candidates are unreliable. Skill-JEPA adds a state-conditioned VAE that encodes 4-step action chunks into latent skills with a fixed N(0,I) prior, the same distribution the planner starts from. Across three JEPA models and four benchmarks, success on 80-step goals rises from 42% to 62%. On AntMaze, two-cell goals go from 0.4% to 38%. The planner is unchanged.

Under the hood

  • Ablations: a deterministic autoencoder with no KL term does not help, and neither does whitening its skills to match mean and variance. The match has to hold at each state, and the per-pair KL term is what enforces that.
  • Gains hold for all four planners tested: CEM +17.5 points, iCEM +29.8, MPPI +28.1, Predictive Sampling +20.8. One round of sampling over skills reaches 94% on Two-Rooms at 80 steps, versus 44% with raw actions.
  • Bound: on the planner's first candidates, error ≤ error on training skills + B·√(½·KL(aggregate posterior‖π0)). It covers only the first iteration and one prediction step. Tests are simulation only, and Reacher shows no gain.

Takeaway. When a sampler queries a learned model, the inputs it draws should be in-distribution for that model. Choose the learned input space's prior to match the sampler's starting distribution, and make sure the match holds at each state, not just on average.

Under the hood
