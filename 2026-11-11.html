DeepMind agents prove Signal's security properties on the compiled libsignal binary

The clearest worked example yet of a multi-agent harness doing long-horizon, checkable work: 40–80 agents, a trusted spec, and a gatekeeper the agents cannot get around.

Google DeepMind (Chaudhuri, Kohli et al.) built AlphaProof Code, a multi-agent framework for writing proofs in Lean, a language whose kernel mechanically checks every proof. They used it to prove security properties of unmodified libsignal v0.105.4 (with spqr v1.6.0), compiled by stock rustc to an AArch64 binary. The paper says no earlier work had proved protocol-level guarantees for a widely deployed, unmodified implementation. The proofs cover the PQXDH handshake and the Triple Ratchet, and they hold in two models. The symbolic model treats crypto as ideal black boxes. The computational model gives a concrete bound on how often an attacker can win. The properties proved include forward secrecy, post-compromise security and authentication. The symbolic proofs also cover handshake secrecy against a future quantum attacker. According to the paper, agents wrote about 6.5M lines of Lean on top of about 26,000 trusted lines: 18k of spec plus about 8k of AArch64 semantics. The blog says the agents ran on Gemini Carbon and the work took four weeks.

The idea is that people put their effort into the spec, and the proofs don't need to be trusted. A spec-writer agent drafts with a human, reviewer agents critique the drafts skeptically, and the human approves the result as a git baseline. The prover team has several roles. An orchestrator assigns work but never edits Lean. Team leads compress worker chatter into short summaries, which keeps context small. An architect plans how the proof is split up. One integrator is the only role that commits, and each file has at most one worker at a time. Provers cannot edit the spec. They can propose amendments to a critic that never sees the proof attempts. A gatekeeper accepts a proof only if comparator confirms that the theorem and everything it depends on match a held-out copy of the spec, that only standard axioms were used, and that the kernel accepts it.

Limits: 37 routines are replaced by trusted contracts, including the crypto primitives, memmove, bcmp, strlen and the output writers. Key generation is not verified, and group messaging is not covered. The proofs found no new bugs. On HN, commenters were wary of results with zero bugs found. They said agents can write Lean that cheats or proves too little, so the generated proofs need adversarial review, and the real value is the assumptions this process uncovers.

Under the hood

  • The AArch64 semantics covers 467 instructions and 31 syscalls in about 7.5k lines of Lean. It is checked step by step against the Unicorn emulator and Arm's Sail model of Armv9.4-A.
  • ElfRunsTo bin pre post is the core statement: every run of the raw ELF bytes from inputs satisfying pre ends in a state satisfying post and never faults.
  • Spec tests: agents prove the hypotheses can all hold at once (so theorems aren't empty), inject bugs such as skipping the MAC check, and re-add two real fixed bugs. Each gets a proof of insecurity.
  • Running the scenarios through the Lean semantics took 15.6M instructions over 10 invocations and matched the real binary, session state included. The paper proves no run exceeds 1 GiB of heap.
  • Not covered: threads, signals, dynamic linking. The computational bound covers only classical attackers and becomes meaningless for many long sessions with many epochs.

Takeaway. When you build an agent harness for long tasks, make one small trusted artifact the thing you check against (spec, tests, schema), and hold back a copy the agents cannot edit. Put human review there and let agents grind on the output that can be checked mechanically.

Glasswing at seven months: 37,966 AI findings, 217 fixes

VulnCheck checked Anthropic's AI vulnerability-hunting claims against public data and curl's own triage. The main lesson is about measuring an agent's precision honestly and deciding who pays for triage.

VulnCheck has tracked CVEs credited to Anthropic since Project Glasswing launched on April 7. Its sources are CVE records, GHSAs (GitHub security advisories), Linux kernel commits and Anthropic's own disclosure ledger. After seven months it counts 442 CVEs, about 0.5% of the 93,450 published this year. Only 2 of them (CVE-2026-61500 and CVE-2026-26980) appear in VulnCheck's KEV catalogue of vulnerabilities exploited in the wild. Anthropic's ledger, last updated November 2, lists 37,966 findings. Of those, 7,349 are public and 217 are fixed. 1,654 are marked as duplicates and 419 have been withdrawn. Even so, the ledger's claimed true positive rate went up from 90.8% at launch to 94.2%. The author suspects duplicates and withdrawals never feed back into that number. He also reports a ledger bug that inflates the 'disclosed' count.

Anthropic has admitted that human validation does not scale. On October 8 it said it had about 29,000 candidates but had reviewed only about 6,000, and that it had already sent nearly 5,000 unverified reports to maintainers who asked for them in bulk. Its new self-service OSS Scanner sends reports that are "fully model-generated, without human review," which moves triage onto maintainers. curl is the test case. Anthropic sent 45 reports, rating one medium and the rest low. Of the 34 triaged so far, 19 are not vulnerabilities, 6 are confirmed low severity and 9 are still pending. That is a 55.9% false positive rate so far. In September, VulnCheck found that Claude rated 91.5% of ledger findings critical or high, while maintainers rated only 51.3% that way.

The HN discussion pushed back hard. Fil-C's author got 9 reports and all 9 were real bugs, with exceptional reports and test cases. He called the proposed fixes "alright" and took some verbatim. Other commenters said most Glasswing work is partners scanning their own proprietary code, which never gets a CVE, so the OSS ledger is a narrow sample. They also said the 80% of findings still under embargo can't be judged. Some added that low in-the-wild exploitation is what you would expect if patching comes first.

Under the hood

  • Between September 8 and November 2, total findings rose from 26,153 to 37,966 (+11,813), while fixes rose only from 202 to 217 (+15).
  • Over the same period, public findings went from 2,736 (10.5%) to 7,349 (19.4%) and withdrawn findings from 245 to 419. Duplicates went from 2 to roughly 1,654–1,670; the post gives both figures. Duplicates now outnumber fixes about 7.5 to 1.
  • The claimed true positive rate went from 90.8% (May, 23,019 findings) to 91.4% (September) to 94.2% (November).
  • curl so far: 34 of 45 reports published. 19 are not vulnerabilities, 6 are low severity and 9 are pending. Only 17.6% are confirmed, and every confirmed one is low severity. In May, five earlier Mythos findings on curl yielded a single confirmed vulnerability.
  • The OSS Scanner asks projects for their threat model, a gap commenters said caused false positives in the earlier OSS disclosures.

Takeaway. If you run an agent that produces findings, measure its precision with labels from the people who consume the output, not with self-triage. Feed duplicates and withdrawals back into the metric. Budget for the downstream human triage cost before you scale how many findings it produces.

Opus 5.5 'nerf' claim: one tweet versus one benchmark rerun

If you run agents on a hosted model, the question is whether you could detect a silent change to it, and this thread shows how weak the usual evidence is.

Shane Melaugh posted on X that he had Gemini 4 Argon analyze 1,400 Claude Code sessions. He says the reasoning behaviour of Opus 5.5 "took a sharp turn" on November 3, with no change to the model name. The tweet gives no method, no metric and no description of what actually changed. It drew 815 points and 511 comments on HN. Most comments were complaints about how it feels to use. A few brought evidence. One commenter said the only eval they know of that tracks Opus 5.5 over time, livenerf, shows nothing. Another linked a Reddit post suggesting an A/B test. Playcode re-ran a benchmark from September with the same prompt and settings and found the differences fell inside run-to-run noise. (This brief was written by Opus 5.5, the model under discussion.)

Under the hood

  • Playcode rerun at Max effort over the Batch API with a 300K budget: 255,957 tokens ($2.56) in September, 243,707 ($2.44) now. Both runs drew the same kind of laptop SVG.
  • At Max effort, streamed with a 128K cap: both runs used exactly 128,000 tokens and produced an empty file, in September and now.
  • At Xhigh effort: 86,096 tokens ($1.72) before, 107,333 ($2.15) after, about 25% more. Playcode calls this noise because 17 identical calls at 'high' already spread widely; the comment is truncated, so only the 17,757-token low end is visible.
  • Counterpoint raised in the thread: local open-weight models such as Qwen 3.8 27B, and labs that publish the checkpoint they serve (DeepSeek is named), avoid silent model swaps.

Takeaway. Before blaming the vendor, pin a small eval suite of your own and run each prompt N times to measure the spread. Log reasoning-token counts and pass rates per model and date. A single run, or an LLM summarising session logs, cannot tell a regression from variance.

Consumer Gemini can pull Gmail and Drive content into chats that human reviewers may see

A useful case study for anyone building agents with personal-data connectors: implicit tool calls and human review together undo an isolation promise.

An anonymous GitHub write-up says Google has reversed a 2023 promise that Gmail, Docs and Drive content used by Gemini extensions "is not seen by human reviewers". Google Help Answer 16836988 and an October 16 edit to the original blog post now say a reviewer may see connected email or files, and warn users not to connect apps holding confidential information. The scope is consumer @gmail.com accounts only. Workspace Business, Enterprise and Education accounts fall under data processing addenda that exclude human review by default. The author's main claim is that retrieval happens implicitly. With Connected Apps on, casually mentioning a topic such as a bill or a trip can make Gemini search the user's mail and files, with no @Gmail or @Drive tag. What happens next varies from run to run. The author saw four outcomes: no retrieval, a metadata summary, a 2–6 sentence verbatim quote with name, address, account number and confirmation number, or the full email text including headers.

On HN, bri3d agreed the issue is limited to consumer Gemini and said Workspace Gemini still claims to exclude human review. That commenter also expected the nuance to make paying enterprise customers wary anyway. terom quoted the help page: Connected Apps data in Gemini Apps activity is used to train generative models, and Photos data is used to infer relationships and places visited by linking faces to locations and timestamps. Another commenter recognised the pattern from experience: Gemini gave answers "uncannily tuned" to a recent email without saying it had read the user's mail.

Under the hood

  • Repro steps: enable Connected Apps and pick Flash with High Thinking or a stronger mode. Ask an indirect question such as "I wonder if I'm paying too much for internet service". Then tell Gemini to print the exact text and metadata block its retrieval tool injected into the prompt. Delete the conversation between trials so earlier history can't answer the question instead.
  • The author says Google's regex and named-entity-recognition scrubbers (pattern- and entity-based PII filters) can't de-identify narrative data. Examples include account numbers, transaction dates, and combinations like a first name plus a city plus an event. The author cites Google's own Sensitive Data Protection infoType docs.
  • Because tool calling is nondeterministic, a single run with no retrieval doesn't disprove the behaviour. You need repeated trials. The evidence also rests on the model reprinting its own injected context, not on a captured log of what the tool returned.

Takeaway. If your agent can call personal-data tools implicitly, assume any session may contain raw PII. Separate that content from review and training pipelines at the data layer, show users when a retrieval happened, and test privacy behaviour over many runs, since tool calls vary from run to run.

H100 SXM rents for $2.69 to $12.29 an hour, depending on provider

A reproducible like-for-like price comparison that shows what you actually get for the extra money, which helps when you are sizing fine-tuning or RL training budgets.

The data-receipts repo pulls on-demand GPU prices from the AWS Price List and Azure Retail Prices APIs (no auth needed) and copies neocloud prices by hand, with a source URL and the date each was read. It converts everything to dollars per GPU per hour and only compares identical (model, memory) groups. That matters because H100 SXM 80GB, PCIe 80GB and NVL 94GB are different products. For the H100 SXM 80GB: RunPod Community $2.69, RunPod Secure $3.99, AWS p5.48xlarge $6.88, Azure ND96isr_H100_v5 $12.29. That is a 4.57× spread, or $1,964 vs $8,972 for one GPU running for a month. The author is clear that this is not all margin. Azure's ND series includes 400 Gbps per GPU of Quantum-2 InfiniBand with GPUDirect RDMA (GPUs reading each other's memory over the network without going through the CPU), which is what multi-node training needs. It also includes a datacentre SLA, compliance and committed capacity. RunPod Community is third-party hosts renting out their own machines. On HN, one commenter warned that Community/Vast hosts openly talk about peeking inside VMs. Another said that even with large Azure startup credits, GPU quota is effectively impossible to get. Others pointed to SkyPilot (cross-cloud scheduling), cloud-gpus.com, and transaction-based price indices (Silicon Index, Ornn OCPI).

Under the hood

  • Each vendor is counted at its cheapest route to a given GPU. Azure's A100 PCIe 80GB costs $3.67 per GPU-hour on a 1-GPU VM but $4.78 on 2- and 4-GPU VMs. Azure's single-H100 NC SKU is $6.98.
  • Limitations: on-demand list prices only (spot or reserved pricing would narrow the gap a lot), one region each (us-east-1, eastus), and availability is not priced. Lambda is left out of the headline numbers because its listed rates only take effect 2026-11-16.
  • GPU counts per instance come from vendor docs, because the price APIs report only total memory (AWS: '640 GB HBM3' for p5). Reproduce with collect.py (pulls a ~1 GB AWS index, slow) and then compute.py.

Takeaway. If your training or fine-tuning fits on one node, hyperscaler ND/p5 pricing mostly pays for InfiniBand you won't use. A secure-tier neocloud is the better deal for that work. Keep sensitive weights and data off community hosts, and before you plan a budget around a price, check you can actually get quota.

Hiding a readable message in a trusted RSA certificate's modulus

A short, neat piece of RSA arithmetic: you can fix the top half of a modulus to any bytes you want and still end up with a valid key that Let's Encrypt will sign.

Maurycy wanted a "protest certificate" that still encrypts traffic, so he wrote a readable base64 message into his site's RSA public key and got Let's Encrypt to sign it. The method: pick random primes p and q and compute n = p·q. Overwrite the high bytes of n so the message shows up in the PEM's base64. Set q = n/p, round it, and step forward to the next prime, which usually takes a few hundred increments. Because p and q are about the same size, a small change to q only changes the low half of the product, so the message in the high half survives. The CA rejects any exponent other than 65537, so the modulus is the only field he controls. The key goes into a CSR (certificate signing request) and through certbot certonly --manual --csr, and renewal just resubmits the same CSR.

On security, the post concedes that the key has less entropy, but cites a paper on fixing part of the modulus as a compression trick: about half of n can be fixed through division, and state-of-the-art methods fix about 2/3 with similar security. His script does not check for p and q that end up too close together (which makes n easy to factor) or for a dangerously small d. His defence is that forward secrecy limits a stolen key to man-in-the-middle attacks. On HN, tptacek rejected that defence: serious attackers are all MITM, so a session you can't authenticate isn't secure. ryan-c pointed to the same trick in "Stupid Certificate Tricks" from 2014. miki123211 noted that Let's Encrypt allows up to 100 SANs (domain names) of up to 256 characters each, which could put kilobytes of arbitrary data into Certificate Transparency logs, either for abuse or to publish a backup signing key.

Under the hood

  • Space available: about 150 base64 characters with RSA-2048 and about 320 with RSA-4096. Targeting the hex view in browsers gives 2 characters per byte instead of 0.75, but you have to choose words carefully.
  • Base64 encodes 3-byte groups, so the message can land misaligned. Because the certificate layout is fixed, shifting the message's position fixes it in fewer than four tries.
  • The example shows the effect: 58174124 × 918491824 becomes 58174124 × 918491843 once q is moved to the next prime. Only the low digits of the product change (53432457… becomes 53432458…).

Takeaway. The constraint in an RSA product is lopsided: the high half of n is effectively free and the low half absorbs any adjustment. That is why "fixed-prefix" keys and modulus compression work. It's also why you check generated keys for close primes and a small d, and don't trust forward secrecy alone to stand in for authentication.

watched.earth runs SGP4 on 17k satellites in the browser to find passes

A weekend Show HN that does orbit propagation for the whole public catalog in about half a second client-side, then links real Sentinel-2, Landsat and NASA images from those passes.

You enter a place and watched.earth lists every satellite that passed over it in the last 7 days. It then pulls the actual Sentinel-2, Landsat and NASA images taken on those passes. Pass-finding uses SGP4, the standard model for predicting a satellite's position from its published orbital elements. It runs on the CelesTrak catalog of 17k+ objects, entirely in the browser, in about half a second. The orbit view draws all ~17k objects on the GPU and solves Kepler's equation (the iterative step that turns time into a position along an orbit) every frame. One data problem: Sentinel-2 image timestamps are often 5–10 minutes off from when the satellite actually crosses the spot. The author corrected for this but doesn't say how. The post also doesn't say whether SGP4 runs in JS, WASM or workers. Code is at github.com/dev-Armaan/satellites, and the site doesn't store your location. In the HN thread, commenters praised how well the data sources are tied together. A geospatial worker said it's rare to see the exact image a given satellite took. Suggestions: colour-code satellites by orbit type, since the Central Park example already shows sun-synchronous Sentinel-2 passing near 10:50 local time, geostationary GOES-19 watching constantly, and ISS/Tiangong passing at a different time each day. Another asked for Sentinel-1 radar (SAR, which sees through clouds) and the Umbra/Capella open archives for high-resolution passes.

Under the hood

  • Pass search runs SGP4 over the CelesTrak catalog (17k+ objects) client-side in about 0.5 s for a 7-day window.
  • All ~17k objects are drawn on the GPU, which solves Kepler's equation for each object every frame.
  • Sentinel-2 image timestamps are often 5–10 minutes off from the real overpass and needed a correction to match passes. The method isn't described in the post.

Takeaway. Moving per-object maths onto the GPU and running batch work in the client can make a 17k-object job feel instant with no backend. Also, don't trust timestamps in external metadata until you've checked them against an independent model, the way this project checked Sentinel-2 times against its own orbit predictions.

Google ships Gemini 4 Carbon for long-horizon agents, priced the same as Argon

Google's new top model is pitched at long multi-step agent work. It comes with a multi-agent orchestration pattern and measured honesty and prompt-injection numbers, but no architecture details.

Google DeepMind has made Gemini 4 Carbon generally available. It sits above Gemini 4 Argon and is aimed at the hardest tasks: multi-step coding and debugging, cross-subsystem root-cause investigations, multi-package migrations and open-ended research. The post says it uses more tokens than Argon to "explore thoroughly, reason deeply, and run extended verification loops." Argon is still the recommended default for most work. The API price is the same introductory rate as Argon, $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. The post gives no parameter count, architecture, context length or training recipe. It is a product and capability announcement, not a technical report.

The headline claims are about cost per task. Carbon ranks #1 on CursorBench 4.0, Perplexity's WANDR and Zapier's AutomationBench at more than 2x lower cost per task than "the next best external model," which the post does not name. On Artificial Analysis's new Multimodal Knowledge Agent (MKA) benchmark, which tests agents reasoning over dense files with charts, schematics and tables, it ranks #2 at more than 6x lower average cost per task than the top model. Alongside the model, Google is previewing /swarm in its Antigravity IDE. It splits one prompt into a coordinated team of agents: Carbon takes the architecture, coordination and auditing roles, faster models work in parallel isolated workspaces, and adversarial verifiers test every change. One demo built a 906K-triangle Three.js building reconstruction from seven planning PDFs over 10 overnight loops.

The most useful numbers for agent builders are on honesty and safety. On a 375-task agentic honesty benchmark, Carbon passed 92% of tasks versus 70% for Argon. It declined to invent missing experimental data 98% of the time and flagged methodological flaws 68% of the time. It scored 0% attack success on the Gray Swan indirect prompt injection benchmark (attacks hidden in content the model reads, not typed by the user). Google says this is the first time any model has reached that score, and it also says prompt-injection robustness still needs work.

Under the hood

  • Cyber safeguards: the per-attempt breach rate against an adaptive red-teaming agent fell to 0.8% (Argon: 3.1%), and 99% of attack trajectories were blocked. Gray Swan's automated cyberattack query success rate was 2.17% (Argon: 9.86%). Trajectory Labs found zero new universal jailbreaks across 132 hours and more than 13,000 requests.
  • Project Fortify, Google's autonomous scan, triage and fix pipeline: 100% scanning recall and 95% triage precision on an internal benchmark, plus a more than 25% relative recall gain over Argon on open-source repos. Wiz reports finding 0-days in two major SaaS providers in under two hours.
  • AlphaProof Code: an agentic formal-verification framework in which Carbon generated millions of lines of Lean (a language for machine-checked proofs) to prove security properties of the code behind Signal's cryptographic protocol. Google says this took weeks, against years for expert teams.
  • Protein homolog search: a multi-agent Carbon setup searched petabase-scale sequence archives and found 40x more remote homologs (evolutionary relatives) on average across 7 low-homolog CASP17 families. This feeds models like AlphaFold 3 without retraining them. Google notes that broader benchmarking is still underway.
  • Availability: Gemini API paid tier in AI Studio, Gemini for business, and CodeMender. Ultra subscribers get it in Antigravity, the Gemini app and AI Mode in Search. The security-focused 'Carbon for cyber' variant is available through the Fairwind Program.

Takeaway. Carbon costs the same per token as Argon but spends more tokens, so compare models on cost per completed task in your own harness, not on token price. The /swarm setup is worth copying: the expensive model plans and audits, cheap models work in isolated workspaces, and adversarial verifiers check every change.

Meta releases Muse Ember, a cheap closed model for agent subtasks

This is a price-point model for the high-volume tool calls, extraction and coding steps that make up most of an agent's tokens, with an unusually cheap cache rate.

Meta's Muse Ember (muse-ember-1.0) is the smallest model in its Muse line. It is aimed at the steps agents repeat all day: tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction and long-document parsing. Meta does not disclose the parameter count, architecture or training recipe. The only training detail given is that Ember is the first model trained using Muse Wonder Full, Meta's largest model. Pricing is $0.10 per million input tokens, $0.004 per million cached input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens. It has a 1M-token context window, up to 512K output tokens, and takes text, image, audio and video input natively. Meta says it matches Claude Haiku 5.5's best score at about a third of the cost, beats every GPT-6 Luna setting, sits on Artificial Analysis's cost-versus-score frontier, and costs about $0.14 per Artificial Analysis task. Meta suggests a split where Muse Wonder plans and Ember handles the volume of smaller steps.

On HN, minimaxir pointed out that cached input costs 4% of the normal input price, against 10% for Luna and Haiku. doodlesdev noticed that Ember's Terminal-Bench 4.0 score comes from the mini-swe-agent harness, not from Meta's own tooling. Each competitor was scored on its own best harness: Claude Code for Haiku, Codex for Luna. simonw found that across the six effort levels, more reasoning did not visibly improve output quality. Commenters also confirmed there are no open weights, and one suggested it was OpenRouter's "crouton-alpha" stealth model.

Under the hood

  • Six reasoning effort levels: minimal, low, medium, high, xhigh, max. Meta's published comparisons were all run at max.
  • Meta's Personal Superintelligence Index is an internal composite of eight evals: Agents' Last Exam, AutomationBench, FocusBench, JobBench, OfficeQA Pro, GDP.pdf, GDP.xlsx and VBT. OSWorld 2.1 is scored with partial credit, and PerceptionBench is scored with tools allowed.
  • Ember supports the Responses API and is available on Muse Code, the Meta Model API, Google Cloud and Oracle Cloud.
  • Meta's safety testing under its Superintelligence Scaling Framework found Ember below the high-risk thresholds in every category assessed. This testing also covered resistance to prompt injection from web pages and tool outputs, and asking a human before high-impact actions.

Takeaway. If your agent re-sends a large stable prefix (system prompt, tool schemas, documents) on every step, the 4% cached-input rate matters more than the headline price, so put the stable parts of the prompt first so the cache can match them. Re-run the benchmarks on your own harness before trusting them, because Meta's numbers mix harnesses across models.

Fireworks AI breach exposed Notion AI chat transcripts from under 1% of workspaces

Your inference provider can leak your users' prompts, so data it retains is part of your attack surface.

Fireworks AI, one of Notion's inference vendors, told Notion about a security incident that affects fewer than 1% of Notion workspaces. The exposed data is Notion AI chat transcripts. Notion has contacted the affected customers. Workspace owners can find a Security incident review page under the sidebar ? menu, on desktop only. From there they can export a CSV of affected members and share a link that shows each member only their own exposed rows. Owners with a verified domain also get affected_content.csv, which holds the text exactly as it was accessed. A second, annotated file adds record, workspace, user and thread IDs plus an __analysis column. That column flags likely secrets so they can be rotated. Under the hood

Zen 6 and Zen 6c are now two different out-of-order cores

AMD's dense core now has a bigger out-of-order engine than its classic core, which matters when you pick server parts for memory-bound backends and quantized inference.

Chips and Cheese's George Cozma profiled AMD's Zen 6 family. The front-end changes come first. A branch mispredict now costs about 3–3.5 ns, down from 3.5–4 ns on Zen 5. One thread can now use both decode clusters. The core retires 5.96 64-bit adds per cycle against Zen 5's 5.25, with the same six ALUs. The bigger story is that the classic and dense cores have split apart. The dense Zen 6c has larger out-of-order structures (the buffers that hold in-flight instructions while they wait on memory): a 640-entry reorder buffer against 576, 352 integer registers against 288, and a 330-entry load queue against 280. Zen 6 is tuned for clock speed. Zen 6c is built to tolerate latency and sits on 32-core CCDs (the core chiplets) with 128 MB of L3. In estimated single-threaded SPEC CPU 2026, the Zen 6 EPYC 9686F beats the Zen 5 EPYC 9575F by about 21% in integer and 18% in floating point. With clocks matched at 4.1 GHz, Zen 6c comes out ahead per clock: 6.7% in integer and 4% in floating point. Top-down profiles show Zen 6 losing more pipeline slots to memory stalls, and on large codebases to front-end latency stalls (for example 35.2% against 16.6% in 727.cppcheck).

On Hacker News, commenters asked whether PDEP/PEXT really share hardware with the multipliers or whether the source's wording just runs two cuts together. One wondered if the multiplier cut freed die area for the extra VNNI units. Another asked how software is meant to handle 32 cores as the smallest NUMA unit (the level where memory access cost is roughly uniform). A third said what they want most is a better AM5 I/O die and memory controller on the client parts.

Under the hood

  • L3 latency: about 47 cycles on Zen 5, 55 on Zen 6 (10.7 ns, against 10.3 ns on the Zen 5 EPYC 9355P) and 66 on Zen 6c (16.4 ns). Both parts keep 4 MB of L3 per core: 48 MB on the 12-core CCD, 128 MB on the 32-core CCD.
  • VNNI (vector int8/int16 dot-product instructions used in quantized inference loops) goes from 2 to 4 units: 512-bit throughput rises from 1.99 to 3.88 instructions per cycle. Integer multiply units drop from 3 to 2, and PDEP/PEXT throughput falls from 3 to 2 per cycle; AMD says the third multiplier did not justify its area.
  • 256-bit vector loads go to 4 per cycle (512-bit stays at 2), so AVX2 code also reaches 128 B/cycle. L2 read bandwidth is fixed at 64 B/cycle, where Zen 5 managed about 42 across most of the L2.

Takeaway. For memory-bound services such as databases, compilers and large-codebase workloads, a lower-clocked dense part with a deeper out-of-order window and a bigger L3 can match or beat a high-clock part. Run a top-down profile to see whether you stall on memory or on compute before choosing between the 'F' high-frequency parts and the dense parts.

NVIDIA compiles LLM reasoning traces into reusable CPU code plus a 1B model

This is a concrete design for procedural agent memory: a solved trace is turned into parameterized code that a 1B model can run, and per call it costs 16–291x less than the source model.

Peter Belcak, Greg Heinrich, Saurav Muralidharan, Shizhe Diao, Pavlo Molchanov — NVIDIA

Belcak, Molchanov and colleagues at NVIDIA start from the point that most agent calls reuse the same instructions with a different payload, yet the LLM works the problem out again every time. Agentic reasoning caching (ARC) splits finding a solution from running it. A builder model (Claude Opus 4.5) reads a source model's transcript and turns it into a network of semantic functions. These are Python callables that take a Context and return a dict keyed by natural-language descriptions. Inputs, outputs and callees are all looked up by description through vector search, never by name. do(ctx, query, payloads) pushes an isolated memory layer and dispatches to whichever function best matches the intent. load() pulls a value from the layered context. lm() and extract() call a small target model for value extraction, classification or type coercion. narrate() records a human-readable trace. Because callers bind by description, if a function is generalized with a new parameter, its existing callers do not need to change.

Building a cache entry has five phases: isolate the input and output, break the reasoning into steps, find the parameters and their valid ranges, plan the function graph bottom-up, and then run an agentic implement-and-test loop with a judge, which includes N repeated runs of parameter extraction to test robustness. At serving time the steps are: cosine-match the query against registered entry points (a miss goes to the big model), extract parameters with the small model, then execute the code. Once a cache stops changing it can be compiled: do() becomes a direct function call and load() a dict lookup, which gives a standalone Python module with no embedder at runtime.

Results come from MATH-500, AIME 2024–26, GPQA Diamond, GSM8K, MMLU-Pro and SciBench. Cache accuracy matched or beat the source model on 7 of 8 benchmarks: AIME '25 went from 83.3% to 93.3%, because deterministic code avoids the source model's arithmetic slips. GSM8K was the exception (98.0 vs 98.5). Under phrasing, parameter and combined perturbations, at least 90.8% of variants were still answered correctly. Some limits: the generator, builder and judges are all Opus 4.5. Each cache is built from the very problem it is then tested on, so hit rate on genuinely unseen traffic is not measured. And a cache entry costs 12–84x the original solve to build.

Under the hood

  • Runtime stack: Embedding Gemma (~300M params, 768-dim) with a FAISS flat index on CPU for every do()/load() lookup. Compiled caches remove this entirely.
  • Target model: Claude Haiku 4.5 at first, later a Llama 3.2 1B fine-tuned on ~10x synthetic augmentation of 20% of real usage logs. It was costed at $0.023/M input and $0.194/M output tokens, against $5/$25 for Opus 4.5.
  • Memory model: a stack of vector-store layers. load() searches from the top layer down, so the most local scope wins. Each do() pushes a layer and pops it on return, which keeps sibling calls from interfering.
  • Type safety: when programmatic conversion fails, smart_convert asks the small model for a Python literal and validates it with ast.literal_eval().
  • Perturbation tests run only on entries that are already golden-correct. Each candidate must be accepted unanimously by a 2-judge Opus committee. For parameter changes, the generator must first re-derive the original golden answer, as a calibration check.

Takeaway. If your agent keeps sending the same prompt template with different payloads, consider having a strong model distill one solved trace into parameterized code with small-model hooks, and put a similarity-gated lookup in front of the expensive LLM. A build cost of 12–84x the original solve against 16–291x cheaper calls means you only come out ahead on templates that get hit dozens of times.

Self-evolving agent harnesses trade solved tasks; they don't just add them

If you score harness or prompt self-improvement with one aggregate number, this study shows what that number hides: lost tasks, higher deployment cost, uneven transfer, and select/gate rules that pick the wrong parents.

Xiaobo Wang, Zimu Wang, Siyu Ye, Jiaqi Li, Min Tang, Jun Bai, Yuling Shi, Ruobing Wang et al. — University of Science and Technology of China

Authors from USTC, BIGAI, SJTU, UC Berkeley and Simple Agent Lab audit harness evolution: the model weights stay frozen while the system iteratively rewrites the prompts, skills, tools or source code around it. They ran four methods (AHE, HyperAgents, A-Evolve, GEPA) on GPT-5.4. Each method ran with two harnesses: MiniSWE 2.4.5, where the agent can edit the harness's own source code, and Codex 0.143.0, where it can only change prompts, skills, config and orchestration. Each pairing ran on two benchmarks: τ3-Banking (97 general tasks) and Terminal-Bench 2 (89 coding tasks). That gives 16 evolved "Champions", each compared with its starting Seed over 8 passes. They report Avg@8 (mean success, i.e. reliability) and Pass@8 (solved at least once, i.e. coverage) separately.

All 16 Champions gained some tasks and lost others. In one general-task case, reliability rose 5.2 points even though ten previously covered tasks were lost. Reliability improved in 11 of 16 runs and coverage in 10 of 16, and four runs moved the two metrics in opposite directions. Eight of the 11 reliability gains came with higher cost per trial. Cost per trial is not the whole story, though: MiniSWE+AHE on Banking reached 56.7% coverage in 4 passes, beating the Seed's 54.6% in 8 passes at lower total cost. Transfer depended on the target. Coverage fell for 7 of 8 Champions on τ2-bench and rose for 7 of 8 on DeepSWE.

The mechanism probes look at the evolution loop's operators: select (pick a parent agent), analyze (diagnose its traces), mutate (propose a change) and gate (decide whether to keep it). The native select rules picked the parent with the most improvement potential less often than random (below 25%) on general tasks, but more than 55% of the time on coding. The authors fold their fixes into A-Evolve and call the result AGE. It beats A-Evolve on reliability and coverage in all four settings and loses fewer Seed-covered tasks in three of them. The fetched text shows that head-to-head only against A-Evolve, with no absolute numbers.

Under the hood

  • Specific, trace-grounded changes ("Components") beat generic reminders such as "check tools, verify results" in 15 of 16 comparisons. Combining Components beat either one alone in 6 of 8 Champions. In two general-task cases, a subset did better than the full combination.
  • Requiring a candidate to show higher mean success with at most 0.05 regression against the current version before admitting it cut best-candidate retention from 15 pools to 6. Stricter gating discards good alternatives.
  • AGE select: still takes the top-scoring parent, but breaks exact ties by fewest per-task regressions against the Seed on Validation. Tie-breaking only runs when the evidence is comparable: matching task-set hashes and equal repetition counts.
  • AGE analyze: an LLM judge scores trajectories 0–10 and marks each claim as Observed or Inference. Each trajectory is capped at 12k characters, keeping about 2/3 from the start and 1/3 from the end. Benchmark rewards, verifier output and hidden tests are excluded. mutate must state the observed behavior, mechanism and expected trade-off, and make one change that brings its dependencies along. gate admits strict score gains, or equal scores only with a verified USD cost reduction.
  • Run setup: 20 generations, one candidate per generation, 50 Train tasks re-run each generation, 20 or 19 Validation tasks, and 8-pass evaluation on the held-out Test split. Code is in the RSIHub repo.

Takeaway. When an agent rewrites its own prompts, skills or code, track tasks gained and lost against the baseline per task, and report Avg@k, Pass@k and cost per pass separately; never rely on one aggregate score. Base change proposals on specific failures in traces rather than generic reminders, and test the combined change as a whole before admitting it.

On-policy distillation tolerates far more rollout mismatch than RL does

If you run RL-style post-training, this measures how much mismatch from FP8 rollouts and async rollouts OPD can absorb, and gives a 24-minute check for whether a rollout setup is safe.

Cong Yu, Ziting Wang, Songlin Jiang, Sitong Zhang, Chengliang Chai, Youhe Jiang, Eiko Yoneki, Tuo Shi et al.

On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on responses it samples itself, and a fixed teacher scores every token. Researchers from Aalto, Cambridge (Yoneki) and others measure mismatch, the per-token KL between the rollout engine and the trainer, in nats per token. With that one number they can compare FP8/NVFP4 quantization, stale async weights and injected noise. On a DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B student, standard vLLM BF16 sits at 0.0008. OPD keeps its quality up to 0.184, more than 200× that level, and fails at about 0.39. REINFORCE and GRPO break at roughly 12× and 60×. The reason is that OPD's advantage, log pT − log pθ, goes to zero once the student matches the teacher, no matter which engine produced the rollouts. Inside that budget, FP8 plus up to 8-version staleness reaches the standard run's final held-out KL 1.62× sooner.

Under the hood

  • Layers matter more than bit width. On the 7B student, the first layer's FP8 KV cache causes 93–99% of the mismatch, which the authors tie to its large keys: the model has a key-projection bias and no query/key normalization. Keeping that one layer's cache in BF16 cuts the estimate from 0.52 to 0.0037. Every-layer FP8 on Llama-3.2-3B is only about 0.01. Staleness of up to 8 versions adds just 0.0028.
  • The admission check works like this: the candidate engine generates 2 responses for each of 16 problems, a BF16 engine re-scores them, and the config is admitted if 1.5·D̂ < 0.41. Checking six configs took 24 minutes on one GPU, versus 5.5–6 hours on 8 GPUs for one training run. It matched all 8 outcomes decided in advance, but no tested config fell near the cutoff. During training, mismatch reached 1.56–1.68× the offline estimate, above the 1.5 safety margin, so you still need to monitor it.
  • Per-token fixes borrowed from RL (truncated importance sampling, which reweights each token by a capped probability ratio, and score centering) did not help OPD. Part of the damage comes from the engine reaching different prefixes (partial responses), which per-token corrections cannot touch. That prefix-shift error grew 5.3× between a passing and a failing noise level, against 2.1× for the per-token error. Limits: 200-step runs, math-only, and full bracket tests only on the 7B pair.

Takeaway. Log the per-token KL between your rollout engine and your trainer at every step instead of judging a setup by its bit width. If you use FP8 KV cache, test whether keeping the first layer's cache in BF16 removes most of the mismatch. For OPD, async rollouts are close to free. For RL, fix the mismatch at its source rather than relying on per-token corrections alone.

Learn · today's concept

On-policy distillation: a teacher that grades every token the student writes

The problem: you want a small student model to act like a strong teacher. Classic distillation trains the student on text the teacher wrote. But when the student runs on its own, it makes small mistakes and ends up in states the teacher's text never covered, and those errors build on each other. RL fixes this by training on the student's own outputs, but its signal is thin. A 2,000-token attempt usually gets one scalar reward at the end, so the model can't tell which tokens deserved the credit.

On-policy distillation (OPD) uses both ideas. Step 1: the student samples a full response from its current policy. Step 2: the teacher scores that same sequence and gives its log-probability for each token the student actually picked. Step 3: each token gets the reward r_t = log p_teacher(token) − log p_student(token), which is the negative of the per-token reverse KL. Step 4: update the student with a policy-gradient or KL loss on those per-token signals. Every token now gets its own grade, given in the states the student actually reaches.

A small example: the student writes 'Paris' with p_s = 0.5 and the teacher gives it p_t = 0.8, so r = ln 0.8 − ln 0.5 ≈ +0.47 and that token is pushed up. Later the student writes a token with p_s = 0.4 that the teacher gives only 0.05, so r ≈ −2.08 and it is pushed down hard. RL would have given the whole sequence one number.

Trade-offs: the teacher has to run a forward pass over every rollout and share the student's tokenizer. Reverse KL seeks modes, so the student commits to the teacher's main answer instead of covering all its options. The student also can't go beyond the teacher. Today's paper adds a practical point: rollouts sampled with FP8 or asynchronously come from a slightly different policy than the one being trained, and OPD tolerates that mismatch far better than RL does. Use OPD when you have a stronger teacher and want a cheaper model for agent subtasks. Use RL when you need to go beyond the teacher.

Further reading: Agarwal et al., "On-Policy Distillation of Language Models: Learning from Self-Generated Mistakes" (GKD, ICLR 2024); Gu et al., "MiniLLM: Knowledge Distillation of Large Language Models" (ICLR 2024)

Junlong Li, Jindong Jiang, Yonggan Fu, Jiarui Yao, Hanrong Ye, Wonmin Byeon, Peter Belcak, Hao Zhang et al. — NVIDIA

NVIDIA, with HKUST and UIUC, tested one-time handoffs inside a single long-horizon agent task, using mini-SWE-agent on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and DeepSWE v1.1. The direction of the handoff matters. Starting on a cheap model and escalating to a strong one loses accuracy or saves little: Qwen-to-GPT-5.6 Sol at 30% saved only 6.1%. Going the other way, from strong to cheap at the right point, mostly keeps accuracy. They found three signs that a task is ready to hand off: a real artifact exists, it has been run and produced feedback (a failure counts if it narrows the problem), and what's left is local repair or validation. Simply prompting the strong model to hand off didn't work, because Kimi K3 never did it. So they trained AgentBaton, a 2B classifier that runs after every turn. Across 11 model pairs it cut API cost by 65.9% on average, with up to +8.8pp accuracy. It also cost 46.7–86.6% less than the per-turn router MTRouter at matching or better accuracy. Caveats: local Qwen is counted as zero cost, only 12 of 22 settings stayed within a 5% relative accuracy loss, and Terminal-Bench results were less consistent.

Under the hood

  • The detector is Qwen 3.5 2B, fine-tuned on 4,000 trajectories from 2,000 SWE-rebench V2 tasks. Codex labelled the earliest valid handoff turn in each, and every turn after that point was also used as an extra positive example to simulate a missed handoff. Median latency is 338 ms and p95 is 1.11 s on one H100, for inputs of 2K–121K tokens.
  • History was passed in one of two ways. In-context transfer copies the earlier chat into the cheap model's context. Logfile transfer writes a .jsonl file with one record per turn (response, command, return code, stdout, stderr), and the cheap model reads it with cat or grep. Neither method won everywhere. Logfile was the default for GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-5.6 Luna as source models, possibly because Sol hides its chain-of-thought. In-context was the default for GLM-5.3, Kimi K3 and the other source models.
  • On DeepSWE, Kimi K3 handing off to GPT-5.6 Luna (high effort) reached 79.6% at $1.60 per task. The best single model, Opus 5 at xhigh effort, reached 75.2% at $8.90. Opus-to-Luna rose from 67.3% to 76.1% accuracy while costing 70.5% less.

Takeaway. If your agent harness pays frontier prices for every turn, consider letting the strong model run until there is a tested artifact and only local fixes remain, then switch permanently to a cheaper model. A small classifier run after each turn can make that call more reliably than prompting the strong model to step aside.

LowRAM compresses the KV cache by predicting values from keys in each head

Within each attention head, values can largely be predicted from keys. That gives a fourth way to compress the KV cache, alongside dropping tokens (eviction), lowering precision (quantisation) and low-rank factorisation, and it can be stacked on all three.

Teresa Delgado, Hugo Hazard, Martin Benfeghoul, Jun Wang, Zafeirios Fountas

Huawei Noah's Ark Lab London and UCL found that, within one attention head, a value vector is largely predictable from its key. They call the resulting method value cache amortisation. During prefill they fit a small per-head MLP that maps keys to values: two layers plus a linear branch, initialised from the Slim Attention pseudoinverse W_K^+ W_V. They then rank tokens by reconstruction error and keep exact residuals only for the worst top-p fraction. At decode time the values are rebuilt as f(K) + G⊙ΔV, where G marks the tokens that kept a residual. Keys are not predicted, because key errors shift the attention weights. Instead they are compressed with xKV, a low-rank basis shared across groups of layers, after RoPE (the rotary position encoding) is removed. Tested on Mistral-7B, Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen3.5-4B, the method keeps 97.9–99.4% of baseline at 3× compression. At 4× it keeps 96.0–99.6%, except Llama on RULER, which drops to 93.9%. Stacked with TurboQuant and CNN-based eviction, it reaches 26.9× compression at 94.5% of baseline on LongBench samples over 10K tokens. The costs are real. Full per-context fitting (150 gradient steps) makes time-to-first-token 26.3× slower. A meta-learned initialisation cuts this to 2 steps and 1.3× slower, but costs 1.4–4.4 points on RULER. The predictor's weights are a fixed cost that only pays off after a few thousand tokens, so the method is weak on short contexts. Long generations were not evaluated.

Under the hood

  • Choosing what to predict from what: at a matched 3× budget they compared previous-layer K, previous-layer V, the two combined, current-layer V and current-layer K as predictors. Current-layer K→V recovered the most LongBench performance. Reconstruction error correlated only weakly with downstream accuracy.
  • Ablations: using one predictor for all heads in a layer costs almost 20 points. At a fixed budget, choosing residuals by error recovers almost all accuracy, while choosing them at random loses most of it. Values alone at 4× (Llama, exact keys) keep 98.2% on RULER and 99.3% on LongBench, beating xKV applied to values on LongBench.
  • Selective reconstruction: the prefix is split into chunks, each represented by its mean key. Only the top-k chunks for the current query are rebuilt, and a buffer reuses chunks across steps. At 4× compression this supports batch 28 and about 5× the baseline's peak decode throughput at 262K context. Replacing exact SVD with randomised Cholesky-QR makes that step 4× faster.
  • Quantisation detail: only the shared, context-length factor A of xKV is quantised (4-bit), and value residuals use 2-bit. TurboQuant's 1-bit QJL correction step was dropped because softmax amplified its variance. Adding quantisation raised Llama RULER at 4× from 93.9% to 99.4%.

Takeaway. Compress keys and values differently: errors in values do less damage than errors in keys, so a predictor plus exact residuals for the worst tokens is a good fit for values. The per-context fitting cost is paid once, so this suits reused contexts such as shared prefixes or document QA, rather than single queries on short prompts.

ByteDance replaces its rerank and mix-rank rules with one RL-trained decoder

A production example of RL post-training against a slate-level reward model, with a concrete recipe for combining several objectives and hard constraints.

Feng Cheng, Yu-Hsuan Huang, Fengyi Wang, Chaofan Zhou, Zhennan Zhu, Huan He, Bo Zhang, Ruoqi Zhang et al. — ByteDance

ByteDance's Global E-Commerce Recommendation team replaced its rerank and mix-rank stages with OneTrans-GSR, a single decoder-only transformer that builds the shown list ("slate") of products, videos, livestreams and ads one item at a time. The old setup relied on hand-written rules plus a shallow scoring model. The decoder is pretrained with next-token prediction on user-behavior sequences. It is then post-trained with GMR-PO, a GRPO-style method (each sampled slate is scored against the others drawn for the same request), against a frozen reward-model ensemble used only in training. Business rules are split in two: hard ones are enforced at decode time with masks and logit penalties, and soft ones enter as a separately standardized reward. In online A/B tests against a Generator–Evaluator baseline, it lifted GMV per user by 2.63%, advertiser value by 2.06% and repurchase rate by 2.03%, and cut latency across the two stages by 13%. Offline, slate quality rose as reward-model, decoder and rollout compute grew.

Under the hood

  • Reward: an EOS token placed after the slate, under causal attention, gives logits for list-level clicks, orders and GMV, trained with weighted logistic regression. A separate LTV model predicts 14-day repurchase, combined per item as pCTR·pCVR·pREP; this assumes at most one item is used per slate, which holds for 98.3% of logged slates for clicks and 99.99% for purchases. The ensemble penalizes disagreement with mean − 0.01·std. Each objective is min-max normalized within the rollout group, floored at 1e-4, and combined with a weighted geometric mean, so a low score on any one objective cannot be offset by the others.
  • Decoder: user history and all candidates are encoded once (prefill) with block-causal attention. Each step scores the request's own candidates by temperature-scaled cosine similarity, with the inverse temperature capped at 100. Stop-gradient on the output-side candidate embeddings prevents embedding collapse. Serving samples several slates in parallel and returns the one with the highest log-probability; no reward model or evaluator runs online.
  • KV cache: one prefill cache is shared by all parallel samples, and each sample keeps its own small cache for the items it has generated. Peak memory is about 17% above the no-prefill baseline. KV caching sped up training 2.09x and serving 4.52x over the previous BF16 setup; cumulative speedups over eager mode were 4.85x and 11.57x.

Takeaway. When an RL policy has to meet hard rules, enforce them at decode time with masks or logit penalties, and put only soft preferences in the reward. Standardize each reward separately before adding them, and combine competing objectives with a floored geometric mean so the policy cannot trade one objective away for the others.

One recipe for RL fine-tuning of 1–4 step diffusion models

Tencent Hunyuan and Westlake show that FlowGRPO, AWM, DiffusionNFT and ReFL all have the same gradient form, then adapt that form to few-step generators, which makes RL rollouts much cheaper.

Haitao Lin, Minsi Ren, Yang Li, Xingtong Ge, Zheyuan Hu, Peiyan Hu, Tailin Wu — Tencent Hunyuan

RL post-training for diffusion models needs fresh on-policy samples (rollouts) at every step, and with 32–64-step samplers those rollouts take most of the compute. Few-step models such as MeanFlow, DMD and AnyFlow make rollouts cheap, but they predict an average velocity over a noise interval, while RL objectives assume the instantaneous velocity. The authors (Tencent Hunyuan, Westlake University, UCAS) take three steps. First, they rewrite every objective in x-space, as a predicted clean sample; under a linear noise schedule, a velocity loss is just a scaled distance between those predicted points. Second, they show each method's gradient is the sum of a self-regression term, which pulls toward a reference anchor such as the old policy and limits reward hacking, and a reward-augmentation term along x_rwd − x_base. That leaves each method as a choice of three anchors and three weights. Third, they compute those anchors from a few-step model. In u-form the anchor sits at the end of the transport. In v-form it sits on the tangent, with the instantaneous velocity recovered either from the network itself through the MeanFlow identity or from an auxiliary network as in DMD. The paper reports these findings. Naively plugging the average velocity into FlowGRPO, AWM or DiffusionNFT gives a lower best GenEval2 score than the adapted versions. When the reward is differentiable, gradient-based methods win, and ReFL alone is a strong baseline. DiffusionNFT-style methods reach high reward in few iterations. Sampling with more steps at inference adds little overall. The fetched text has no exact scores.

Under the hood

  • Seven implementations share one training step, split into hooks: rollout, reward/advantage, anchors/weights, loss, auxiliary-network fitting, and an EMA refresh of the old policy. Each method overrides only the hooks where it differs.
  • Test beds: AnyFlow-distilled SD3.5-Medium for images, with a non-differentiable OCR reward and a differentiable mixed reward, and AnyFlow-distilled Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B for video.
  • DMD-based variants are faster per iteration on images but slower on video, because the relative cost of forward and backward passes differs. They also tend to show more degradation on held-out evaluation.

Takeaway. If your reward is differentiable and its gradient is affordable, start with a ReFL-style baseline that backpropagates the reward. Otherwise, use a group-advantage method like DiffusionNFT. In either case, the self-regression and reward-augmentation split is a useful way to read any new RL objective as just its anchors and weights.
