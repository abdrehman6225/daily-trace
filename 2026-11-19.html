A minimal RAG memory ties agent-memory SOTA, and the benchmarks can't tell them apart

Why read · For a memory engine: a strong baseline you can rebuild in a day, and evidence that facts-vs-form storage, not graphs or reflection, decides what agents actually do with memory.

Jingyu Liu, Zongze Li, Zach Xu, Zhanhui Zhou, Tahseen Rabbani, Dawn Song, Ce Zhang

UChicago and Berkeley (Ce Zhang, Dawn Song; NeurIPS 2026) built Bridge, a deliberately minimal memory. Conversation turns are grouped into blocks that close at a natural boundary (end of session, end of a tool call) or at 2,000 tokens. One extractor LLM call per block writes an XML observation note, and it sees the previous 10 notes. Both the note and the raw text are stored. At query time, cosine search over note embeddings and BM25 over note plus raw text each return their top 60, merged with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (k=60). Of the top 10 blocks, the 3 closest by cosine are shown as raw text and the rest as summaries. The blocks are then re-sorted by time, and one reader call answers. There is no graph, no reflection step, and no supersession (overwriting outdated facts).

With the same reader model as the baselines, Bridge is within bootstrap CI of prior SOTA on LongMemEval-S (1.4 points behind EmergenceMem) and BEAM (0.370 vs LIGHT's 0.345). On EverMemBench it beats MemOS by 5.7–6.3 points. It uses about 5–7K reader tokens per query, 17–154x fewer than full context. The authors take the tie as evidence that recall benchmarks can't separate designs. So they wrote five probes where the request never names the memory it needs. Example: add POST /forgot-password after an earlier aside that public endpoints need slowapi+redis rate limiting. LangMem, which extracts atomic facts, leads by 45 points on applying rules. Bridge leads by 33 on inducing procedures. Mem0 and Graphiti lead on no probe.

Bridge's rule failures come from presentation, not retrieval. The block with the rule was retrieved in all 20 scenarios, and in 12 of 13 failures it reached the reader as raw text. Adding one call that moves standing instructions into a labelled block at the top of the context lifts Bridge to Oracle level. Sending each request to whichever design suits it would add 13.6 points, but a classifier that sees only the request is no better than guessing. Limits: 20 synthetic scenarios per probe, gaps under about 15 points not interpreted, and static tests rather than live agents.

Under the hood

  • Ablations: removing the raw-text slots costs 30.2 points on single-session-assistant; removing the time re-sort costs 7.7 on knowledge-update; BM25-only vs cosine-only swings EverMemBench memory-update by about 38 points.
  • On the probes, showing all 10 blocks as raw text instead of 3 improves rule application by 13 points; hyperparameters were tuned only on the standard benchmarks.
  • Harder probe levels: filler sessions with similar topics erase LangMem's rule lead entirely; Bridge's procedure lead shrinks but holds with demos cut from 4 to 2.
  • A judge from a different vendor than the generator and reader scores applied/(applied+violated); two other graders agree on 81–86% of verdicts and keep every direction.
  • The routing classifier is right 49–61% of the time vs 60% for always guessing the majority class. Code: github.com/Jingyu6/rag-in-a-trenchcoat; the extractor prompt is adapted from Mastra's observer agent.

Takeaway. Before adding graphs or reflection, beat this baseline: hybrid BM25+dense retrieval with RRF, store both notes and raw text, and render in time order. Test with requests that never mention the memory they depend on, and put retrieved standing rules in their own labelled block.

Learn · today's concept

Hybrid retrieval with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)

A memory store needs two kinds of matching. Lexical matching with BM25 finds exact tokens such as names, IDs and error codes. Dense embeddings find paraphrases, like "cancelled my plan" for "ended subscription". Neither one covers both. Combining them is awkward because their scores are on different scales. BM25 has no upper bound and changes with the query and the corpus statistics, while cosine similarity often falls in a narrow band such as 0.70 to 0.90. If you add or min-max normalize the raw scores, one retriever can quietly dominate depending on the query.

RRF avoids the problem by ignoring scores and using only ranks. Step 1: run each retriever on its own and keep its top N results (say 50 to 100). Step 2: give each document a fused score, the sum of 1/(k + rank) over every list it appears in. The constant k is 60 in the original paper. Step 3: sort by the fused score. Worked example: doc A is ranked 1st by BM25 and 5th by dense, so it scores 1/61 + 1/65 = 0.0318. Doc B is 2nd in both lists and scores 2/62 = 0.0323. Doc C appears only in the dense list, at rank 1, and scores 0.0164. B comes out on top: being near the top of both lists counts for more than one first place.

The trade-offs: RRF needs no training and no score calibration, and it holds up when you add a third retriever. But it throws away confidence. A near-perfect match counts the same as a weak one at the same rank. A larger k flattens the differences between ranks, and a document gets nothing from a list that cut it off, so the depth N matters. Use RRF as the default first stage. Then, if you can afford the latency, run a cross-encoder reranker on the fused top 20 to 50.

Further reading: Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher, "Reciprocal Rank Fusion outperforms Condorcet and Individual Rank Learning Methods" (SIGIR 2009); Robertson & Zaragoza, "The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond" (2009)

Energy-Based Reasoners: looped models that run gradient descent on a learned energy

Why read · A learned energy score gives a looped reasoner three uses: it shows which way to refine the answer, when to stop, and which candidate to keep.

Chen, Gladstone, Li, Ji and Du train a scalar energy E(z;x) over latent reasoning states, where lower energy means a better state. Each loop step is a projected gradient-descent step on that energy. A small gradient norm tells the model to stop, and the lowest-energy sample is the one chosen. A 1.8M-parameter EBR scores 99.4% single-sample on Sudoku-Extreme, against 97.9% for Looped flows and 87.4% for TRM. It reaches 99.9% with 5 samples while using 345× fewer FLOPs than EqR. The headline's 385× figure does not appear on the page. HN commenters point out that results are shown only on small discrete puzzles and that EBMs (energy-based models) are hard to train.

Under the hood

  • Update rule: z_{t+1} = Π_M(z_t − α∇E). Stopping when ‖∇E‖ < τ gives the same 99.9% accuracy with 10.9× fewer FLOPs than running a fixed depth.
  • Maze-Hard: 87.4% single-sample and 88.3% with 5 samples, against FPRM's 87.0% and PTRM's 86.7% at 100 samples.
  • Exploration-based training finds extra modes. With 20 samples, coverage of distinct solutions is 98.1% on 8×8 N-Queens versus 89.7% for GRAM, and graph coloring has 0 conflicts.
  • An HN commenter who spent a year on EBMs calls them strong validators but very hard to train and niche. Others doubt they generalise beyond fixed-rule puzzles.

Takeaway. If your agent loops or refines its answers, a learned score of the current state does three jobs: it guides updates, gives a stopping rule, and picks the best of several samples. The evidence so far covers only Sudoku, mazes, N-Queens and graph coloring.

Partial evaluation turns interpreters into compilers, demonstrated on JavaScript

Why read · The idea behind Truffle, weval and query compilation, with runnable JS examples and a new Rust-based evaluator.

Jimmy Miller explains partial evaluation: run every part of a program whose inputs are known at build time, and output code only for the parts that depend on runtime input. He is releasing split-shot, a partial evaluator for JavaScript written in Rust, which he calls limited. If you give it a Brainfuck interpreter plus a fixed Brainfuck program, it outputs a few lines of plain JS with no interpreter left in it. That is the first Futamura projection. Specializing a JSON-schema validator to one fixed schema made it about 5x faster.

Under the hood

  • Projections: mix(interp, prog) gives a compiled program; mix(mix, interp) gives a compiler; mix(mix, mix) gives a compiler generator. Miller has only limited versions of the second and third working.
  • Second-projection demo: a toy prompt/print interpreter becomes a generated compile() that emits var v0 = io.prompt(); io.print(v0).
  • HN: NebulaStream's nautilus C++ library compiles queries by tracing wrapper types. Another commenter notes that folding across function calls breaks binary compatibility unless callers are recompiled.

Takeaway. If a hot path is an interpreter over data that rarely changes (schema validators, rule engines, query operators), you can specialize it on that data and drop the dispatch overhead without writing a separate compiler. Look at weval and Truffle for production versions of this idea.

Registrar's clientHold drops codeberg.org from DNS while its servers stay up

Why read · The registrar is a single point of failure that health checks on your own infrastructure will not catch.

At 06:30 UTC, Codeberg's registrar put codeberg.org on clientHold. That is a status the registrar sets to tell the registry to stop publishing a domain, so the name vanished from the .org zone and stopped resolving. The servers kept running. Per Codeberg's status page, as cited on HN, the registrar was NameSilo. The domain had not expired. One linked post suggests someone persuaded the registrar that Codeberg was malicious. The outage also stalled a Forgejo security release whose patches had already been briefly made public.

Under the hood

  • The service stayed reachable by IP: IPv4 217.197.84.140:443 and IPv6 2a0a:4580:103f:c0de::1. Users were told to add an /etc/hosts entry as a stopgap.
  • Forgejo told admins using the internal SSH server (Docker with ssh.START_SSH_SERVER = true) to turn it off or block access to it until the delayed release ships.
  • One HN commenter says Namecheap demands passport and selfie checks from some customers and won't say how it picks them. That is another way a domain can be held without warning.

Takeaway. Monitor resolution of your apex domain from outside your network, including its EPP status in WHOIS/RDAP, and keep a runbook with registrar escalation contacts. Treat your registrar as critical infrastructure.

Nginx HTTP/2 bugs allow remote code execution without authentication

Why read · If you run Nginx with HTTP/2 enabled, patch or turn off HTTP/2 now. The fix and the exploit write-up are both public.

Wiz disclosed two Nginx vulnerabilities, CVE-2026-123633 and CVE-2026-123007, that let an unauthenticated attacker run code remotely. The blog URL ties them to HTTP/2, and Xint.io has published the exploit chain as "NGINX2Root". Almost none of the write-up could be fetched, and the CVE records came back empty, so the bug mechanism, affected versions and patched releases are not confirmed here. According to HN commenters, turning off HTTP/2 blocks the attack. Xint's post has a section on checking whether you are exposed and one on emergency workarounds.

Under the hood

  • HN commenters say the fix sat in the open-source repo about a week before disclosure, with a "Reported by Xint.io" commit message. Anyone watching the repo could have spotted it and built an exploit.
  • Commenters note that Nginx does not enable HTTP/2 by default. They cite w3techs data that about 12% of servers use it.
  • One commenter says the exploit needs about 128 GB of HTTP/2 traffic and specific CPU instructions. This is unverified and comes from a post on X.

Takeaway. Find every Nginx instance with http2 enabled and patch it. If you can't patch right away, turn HTTP/2 off on those instances. Also treat security-fix commits in upstream repos as early warnings, because they can show up days before the advisory.

Java4Shell: OpenJDK's AVX-512 Base64 fast path writes past array bounds

Why read · Any Java 17+ service on recent x86 that decodes untrusted Base64, such as auth headers or uploads, is exposed until it is patched or a JVM flag is set.

Calif disclosed CVE-2026-114938, an out-of-bounds write in HotSpot's x86-64 Base64 intrinsic. An intrinsic is a hand-written assembly fast path, here the one behind java.util.Base64.Decoder. With a padded tail such as 64 As plus =, the output length underflows to -1. bzhiq then builds an all-ones store mask, so a 64-byte vector store lands 62 bytes past a 50-byte array. Calif says it has unauthenticated remote code execution (RCE) on Keycloak, Jira, Jenkins and others. No exploitation in the wild has been seen. Fixed in 17.0.21.1, 21.0.13.1, 25.0.5.1 and 27.0.1.1. ARM64 is not affected.

Under the hood

  • Who is exposed: OpenJDK 17.0.6+ on CPUs with avx512f, avx512bw, avx512vbmi and bmi2 (Ice Lake Xeon and later, AMD Zen 4+). Java 8/11 are safe, except Oracle's 8u511-perf.
  • Workaround: -XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics, or -XX:UseAVX=2 on x86-64 only. A JVM on ARM rejects that flag and won't start. Code that decodes a lot of Base64 gets slower.
  • Exploit: the fixed bytes overwrite a neighbouring array's length, giving a chosen-value write and heap leaks. Each app needs its own heap layout, sprayed over streamed partial requests.

Takeaway. Today, run grep -m1 '^flags' /proc/cpuinfo on your x86 hosts and look for the four AVX-512/BMI2 flags. Where all four are present, add -XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics until you have patched every JDK, including the ones bundled in container base images.

Gemini 4 Neon: Google's mid-tier model aimed at agents, priced at $3/$15 per million tokens

Why read · A cheaper, faster frontier-class model for long-running tool-heavy agents, and the results show the harness can matter as much as the model.

Google DeepMind's Gemini 4 Neon sits between Carbon (hardest long-horizon work) and Hydrogen (fast everyday execution). The model card says only that it is adapted from Gemini 4 Carbon. It gives no architecture, parameter count or training detail. Neon takes text, images, audio and video in a 1M-token window, returns up to 64K output tokens per synchronous call (up to 1M through a background "long decode" mode), and has a March 2026 knowledge cutoff. Pricing is $3/M input tokens, $0.15/M cached input and $15/M output. It has four thinking levels: high is the default and xhigh is the top.

The agent results are the useful part. Neon is #1 on the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index (58.6), scores 65.2% on Terminal-Bench 4.1 and is #2 on Agent Arena (1336 across 4,200+ sessions). The harness matters a lot: on SWE-sweep it scores 12.8% in the minimal mini-SWE harness and 39.0% in Google's Antigravity harness. Google says Neon makes fewer redundant file reads and handles context compaction reliably. All results are self-reported, and the full safety report is not out yet.

Under the hood

  • At high, cost per task is 32% lower than Carbon; Google claims roughly 50–80% lower cost than competing frontier models on Vals and Agent Arena.
  • At xhigh it scores 61 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index (#3 overall) and is said to cost 36%+ less per task than rivals at their highest settings.
  • Integrity evals: 0.0% cheating on Terminal-Bench 4.0, shortcuts on 2.45% of coding tasks, 0.09% misaligned actions, 99.2% pass on agent honesty.
  • It skipped a full Frontier Safety evaluation because its gain over Gemini 4 Argon fell below the material-capability-change threshold; Argon's risk determinations carry forward.

Takeaway. The jump from 12.8% to 39.0% on SWE-sweep is a reminder to test models inside your own agent loop, because a vendor's leaderboard score partly measures its harness. If you run long agent sessions, the 95% cached-input discount means a stable, cacheable prompt prefix will cut costs a lot.

Exchange Online's 24-hour outage: a 3-month-old lock bug, a failover and 14% lost capacity

Why read · This postmortem shows a latent defect, a routine failover and broken isolation combining into a global failure that rollback and auto-recovery could not fix.

Exchange Online was down for 23h50m starting 16:20 GMT on 12 November. Tony Redmond summarises Microsoft's preliminary Post-Incident Report, which names three conditions that had to coincide. First, a Microsoft 365-specific Windows change, rolled out globally from July, could cause lock contention under certain loads. That exhausted CPU and memory until network gateway servers stopped responding. Load tests missed it because the bug shows up mainly on newer, higher-core-count hardware than the test machines had. Second, about 15 hours before the outage, Teams failed over to the primary gateway that Exchange also uses. This was a routine move, but it added load and changed the traffic pattern. Third, an unrelated fault had already taken about 14% of gateway capacity offline.

A separate problem let the failure cross the bulkheads (isolation boundaries between capacity units and regions), so it went global. Teams, OneDrive and SharePoint could route away from the gateway and recovered sooner. Exchange could not do that at the scale needed. Recovery ran into a thundering herd: queued traffic immediately knocked restored servers over again. Automated recovery wasn't built for that much of the fleet being down at once, and rollback couldn't undo a change shipped three months earlier.

Under the hood

  • Failure chain per Microsoft: more traffic plus less capacity, then resource exhaustion, server failures, traffic redirection, more overloaded servers, and finally a global cascade.
  • The fix put back the previous known-good Windows code path. Microsoft took large groups of servers offline, patched and restarted them, then returned capacity gradually.
  • Mailbox resilience (Database Availability Groups, Exchange's replicated database sets) held. The weak point was the shared layer that accepts and routes connections.
  • Full-month outage works out to 96.69% uptime under the SLA, which falls in the 50% service-credit tier. Customers must claim the credit by the end of December.

Takeaway. Load-test on the same core counts you run in production, because contention bugs scale with cores. Plan for a fix that can't be rolled back by keeping the old code path behind a flag, and admit capacity back with throttling so returning servers aren't flooded.

PostgreSQL 19 adds online REPACK, plan pinning, and WAIT for standby reads

Why read · Several long-standing Postgres operational problems now have built-in fixes, and a few default changes will hit you during the upgrade.

The release notes (dated as of 2026-10-09; RC 2 shipped Nov 12) lead with REPACK, which replaces VACUUM FULL and CLUSTER. REPACK (CONCURRENTLY) rebuilds a table without an access-exclusive lock and adds a new max_repack_replication_slots setting. pg_plan_advice lets you control planner decisions, and pg_stash_advice applies that advice automatically by query ID. A new WAIT command makes a standby wait until an LSN (a position in the write-ahead log) is written, flushed or replayed, so reads on a replica can see your own writes. Autovacuum can now process a table's indexes with parallel workers and picks the order of tables with a weighted score. Logical replication now carries sequence values and turns on without a restart when wal_level=replica. INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO SELECT ... RETURNING returns the conflicting row and can lock it.

Upgrade traps: JIT is now off by default. max_locks_per_transaction goes from 64 to 128, but lock sizing changed, so double any custom value to keep the same capacity. io_workers is gone, replaced by min/max worker settings. Parallel vacuum now shares one cost limit, so it may run slower. standard_conforming_strings is forced on, so old dumps made with it off will not load. Default TOAST compression is now lz4. RADIUS support is removed.

Under the hood

  • NOTIFY now wakes only the backends listening on that channel; before, it woke most backends.
  • Planner: NOT IN becomes an anti-join when no NULLs are present, and some aggregation can now run before joins to cut the number of rows.
  • Table scans can now set pages all-visible in the visibility map, which only VACUUM and COPY FREEZE could do before. Also new: radix sort, and SIMD parsing for COPY FROM text/CSV.
  • Other new tools: EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, IO) for async I/O, COPY TO JSON output, log_lock_waits on by default, and wraparound warnings at 100M transactions (was 40M).

Takeaway. Before upgrading, check your JIT, lock and io_workers settings, and make dumps with the v19 pg_dump. Then try REPACK (CONCURRENTLY) on bloated tables and WAIT for read-your-writes on replicas.

Hugging Face streams MoE experts from SSD to run a 180B model on a 32 GB Mac

Why read · The method keeps a bounded cache of expert weights and fills it from SSD on demand, close to the speed of a dedicated engine, and the same tiered-memory design works outside LLMs.

Tarek Ziadé of Hugging Face added SSD expert streaming to Transformers (PR #50717). With it he ran Qwen3.8-Flash-Next on a 32 GB M5 MacBook. The model has 48 layers of 512 experts with 10 active per token, and its experts take 121 GB in FP8. The key idea is a slot pool for the device cache: one preallocated tensor plus a per-layer table that maps expert id to slot. The router's expert ids are remapped to slots, so the stock grouped_mm kernel runs on the cache unchanged. Cache misses are read by 8 I/O threads, one contiguous read per expert. The next two layers' routers run early to choose which experts to prefetch. Final speeds: 13.9 tok/s decode against 14.8 for Strata, a dedicated MoE engine, and 228 against 220 tok/s prefill.

Under the hood

  • Experts are quantized to 3-bit (49 GB on disk, 12 GiB cached on the GPU). Dense layers are 8-bit (+0.2% perplexity vs BF16). A 51 GB n-gram table is read from disk one row at a time. Memory peaks at 25.4 GiB on a 6K prompt.
  • Speeds on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on one A10G (24 GB GPU): Strata 82–88 tok/s, llama.cpp Q8 63–64, vLLM FP8 offloading only the experts 20.1, Transformers + Accelerate 0.16.
  • Smarter cache policies barely beat LRU. Most of the remaining gap is ~10 ms per token of CPU–GPU round trips. PyTorch on Apple GPUs (MPS) can't pause the GPU at each layer to wait for the CPU. Forcing PyTorch to commit queued GPU work early gave +10%.

Takeaway. When you put a cache in front of a large weight store, use an id-to-slot table so the cache keeps the original layout and existing fast kernels run on it unchanged. Before tuning the eviction policy, measure how long the GPU waits on the CPU.

Self-evolving agents stall because noisy evals can't pick the better candidate

Why read · If your system accepts or rejects prompts, skills or memories based on eval scores, this paper shows that step can be a coin flip.

Yuanhao Zeng, Shaocheng Lan, Ao Lu, Kuang Yan, Heng Lin, Xinang Xu, Yuan Luo, Yueting Wang et al. — ShanghaiTech University

ShanghaiTech, with ByteDance, BIGAI and SUSTech, argues that self-evolving agents stall because noisy evaluation cannot pick out real improvements, not because proposers fail to write them. They tested 7 methods, including GEPA, SkillOpt-Lite, ReasoningBank and Meta-Harness, on 6 models over 1,030 evolution runs. Selecting on execution scores picks the better of two skills only 49.7% of the time, and LLM judges do no better. Gains measured inside the loop reach 6.9–9.0 pp but shrink to between +0.4 and −3.2 pp on re-evaluation. The largest reported gains mostly came from one sentence that games the grader, and once grading was fixed the median gain fell from 17.6 pp to 0.8 pp.

Under the hood

  • Published method margins have a median of 3.4 pp. A single run has a 1.7–4.1 pp standard deviation, and detecting 3.4 pp on Terminal-Bench takes about 23 runs per method. A one-run results table picks the best of four methods 33% of the time, against 25% for a random guess.
  • Proposal is not the bottleneck. The expected best of K skills rises from 49.2% at K=1 to 61.8% at K=100, staying about 3.3 pp above an equal-quality null baseline.
  • Suggested fixes: score intermediate behaviour with programmatic checks (13–57× less noise than pass/fail), evaluate proposer and selector separately, and report both evaluation noise σ and run-to-run variance τ.

Takeaway. Before an evolution loop commits a new memory or skill, re-score it over several rollouts and use a power calculation to check that the gain is bigger than the noise. When the end-to-end metric is too noisy, use cheaper process-level checks.

HF Daily Papers · 1 min

Reverse-KL to the behavior policy stabilizes asynchronous off-policy RL for LLM agents

Why read · If you train agents with asynchronous RL rollouts, this is a cheap token-level fix that helps most on long, branching agentic tasks and when the sampler lags far behind the learner.

Hao Bai, Chenlu Ye, Xuanchang Zhang, Aviral Kumar, Tong Zhang — University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign

UIUC and CMU (Aviral Kumar, Tong Zhang) target the gap between the learner and the policy that generated the data in async LLM RL. The gap comes from stale samplers and from training and inference computing slightly different probabilities. Score centering (SC) subtracts the behavior policy's mean score so reward baselines stop biasing updates without full importance sampling. But SC still scores tokens using how the behavior policy continued each response. RSC adds a token-level reverse-KL penalty toward the behavior policy, with or without truncated IS (TIS, capped token weights). RSC+TIS ranks first on all six agentic tasks. It scores 14.3% higher than SC on AppWorld and reaches 9.9x the strongest BabyAI baseline, IS-PPOClip.

Under the hood

  • Token update: A_i(rho_TIS*psi - E_mu[rho_TIS*psi | h]) - beta*E_mu[rho_KL*log(rho_KL)*psi | h]. The KL reference is refreshed with the sampler, and no extra rollouts are needed.
  • They replace the original SC center (top-128 tokens plus a modeled tail) with Monte Carlo auxiliary tokens drawn from mu at each history. This beat top-128 in every variant tested, with BF16 and FP8 rollouts.
  • At the longest refresh interval on Countdown, RSC+TIS gains about 65% absolute heldout reward over SC. On dense-model math, KL changes accuracy by less than 1%. Theory: O(log(N/eps)) refresh rounds to match best-of-N, against O(H*log(N/eps)) without KL (H = response length).

Takeaway. In async agent RL, add a reverse-KL term to the behavior policy that produced each sample, and estimate the score-centering mean by sampling extra tokens from that policy. Expect the gain in multi-step tasks where actions change later states and in setups with large policy lag, not in single-turn math.
