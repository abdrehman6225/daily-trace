600 experiments on a JEPA world model: the planner is the bottleneck, not the predictor

Four planner changes, with no retraining, took LeWM from 22% to 85% on long Push-T tasks, and its standard evaluation turned out to hide most of its weaknesses.

Haddy Alchaer ran 600 experiments on LeWM, the JEPA world model from LeCun, Balestriero and colleagues. The test is Push-T, a simulation where a robot pushes a T-shaped block into a goal position. LeWM has three parts. An encoder turns each frame into an embedding, a predictor maps (embedding, action) to the next embedding, and a planner tries hundreds of action sequences and keeps the one whose end state is closest to the goal embedding. When he swapped the predictor for the simulator's exact physics, nothing changed: 78% vs 77% on tasks with the goal 75 steps away and expert subgoals every 25 steps. The weak part is planning, not prediction.

LeWM solves 22% of 75-step tasks but 89.7% of 25-step ones. At 75 steps, exact physics adds 20 points. Chaining 25-step subgoals adds nearly 60, and 4x more search adds under one. To replace the expert subgoals, he embedded about 460,000 demonstration frames into a latent memory graph. Each state links to the next state in its own demo and to nearby states in other demos, and graph search supplies the subgoals. Embedding distance works as a reachability signal: it ranks a reachable subgoal as closer 87% of the time. A cost on each jump between demos removed impossible paths and lifted success from 62% to 85%, the same as with expert subgoals.

The standard benchmark makes this look better than it is: 84% of test goals had a near-twin already in memory. He built a harder test where the start and goal come from different demos and the block must stay on target for the last 10 steps. There the graph planner drops from 75% to 45%. Adding shifted, rotated and mirrored copies of demo segments brings it to 56%. The biggest lever was the goal itself, because a goal image also fixes where the pusher must end up. With exact physics, scoring only the block turned 30 of 50 failures into successes. LeWM's embedding can't separate out the pusher, so he scored plans against the closest of 16 goal images with pusher positions taken from demos. That doubled success, from 11% to 23%.

Under the hood

  • Benchmark noise: the official 50 episodes give 68% success at 50 steps, but 250 fresh episodes give 44%. Changing only the planner seed flips 38 of 250 results.
  • Before the jump cost was added, 318 of 1,015 graph plans used six or more jumps between demos, chaining paths the block could never follow.
  • Coverage test: deleting stored states near the goal cut success from 93% to 32%. Transformed segments restored 85%. Memory from 1% of demos plus transforms matched 10% plain.
  • Goals against the direction the demos run: plain memory 27%, transformed memory 46%. The learned hierarchy H-JEPA so far only matches or slightly beats flat LeWM.
  • HN comments: hierarchical planning with subgoals is well-known prior work, JEPA planning is close to MPC (model predictive control) with a learned latent model, the eval leans too heavily on demo frames, and Gumbel MCTS solves Push-T without training.

Takeaway. Before paying for a better model, swap in an oracle such as exact physics or perfect retrieval to find where the error really comes from. Often it is the planner, the eval set or the goal definition. Write goals as a set of acceptable end states, not one exact target to match.

Why pgvector's HNSW slowed small tenants, and how turbopuffer cut tail latency

If your retrieval layer stores many tenants' vectors in one table and filters by tenant and time, this failure mode and the replay-harness fix apply directly.

TheFutureIsNear

Modem kept every tenant's embeddings in one shared pgvector table. Each new customer concern triggered an unindexed brute-force similarity search, filtered by organization_id and a time window. By August 2026 the median search took 0.81 s and p99 took 28.1 s. Adding an HNSW index (a layered nearest-neighbour graph) made things worse. HNSW walks the whole table's graph and applies filters afterwards, so a small tenant's rows often get filtered out entirely. With hnsw.iterative_scan, which keeps widening the walk until enough rows pass the filter, tenants with fewer than 500 groups slowed 89× at the median (9.1 s vs 103 ms). About 7% of results changed, and storage nearly doubled. The brute-force slowness itself came from the shared buffer cache: busy tenants kept their rows in memory, while quiet tenants' rows had been pushed out, so their searches read from disk.

A binary-quantized shortlist table cut p99 to 6.3 s, but the full-precision vectors still took up Postgres memory. The team then moved to turbopuffer, which stores vectors on object storage with memory and SSD caches in front, gives each customer its own namespace and applies filters inside the search. Against brute force, p90 dropped 76% (7.6 s to 1.8 s) and p99 dropped 85% (28.1 s to 4.1 s). Postgres CPU fell 62%, and the whole storage bill fell about 10%.

Under the hood

  • Binary quantization: binary_quantize() keeps one bit per dimension, so a 1,536-dimension vector shrinks from 3 KB to 192 bytes. Fetch about 50× the target count, then rerank exactly at full precision.
  • Quantized stopgap: p90 fell from 7.6 s to 3.5 s, but the median rose from 0.81 s to 1.16 s.
  • Shadow mode against Postgres: 88% of results identical, 11% minor reorderings. Asking for one result per topic left 0.43% mismatches, all ties.
  • Latencies are end-to-end, network included. Some searches ask turbopuffer for exact results, which scan all of a customer's recent rows.

Takeaway. Break search latency down by tenant. If the smallest tenants are the slowest, look for memory contention in the cache before you change the query. Before trusting any filtered ANN index (approximate nearest-neighbour search), replay real production queries against it and compare results with exact search, because filtered HNSW can return different or fewer results without any error.

Aspen: open-source Git server that handles 567 agent pushes a second

If you run hundreds of agents against one repo, this shows which Git guarantees you can drop so pushes stop queuing behind each other.

svemyh

Reason Machines released Aspen under Apache 2.0. It is an independent build of the design behind Pando, the internal Git backend OpenAI described in "Scaling Git at OpenAI." Aspen gives up two usual Git server guarantees. First, there is no single consistent snapshot of all branches: each branch is its own row in Postgres, and a push moves it with one compare-and-swap, so pushes to different branches never wait on each other. Second, there is no custom pack per client: pushed packs (Git's bulk file format for objects) go into object storage unchanged, and fetches stream the stored packs instead of recompressing them. On one 8-core server with S3 Standard, Aspen sustained 567 durable pushes a second (p50 208 ms, p99 314 ms).

Under the hood

  • During a push the server advertises only the trunk branch, so one pack serves anyone who has trunk; force-push and delete need an extra -o force.
  • Packs are merged in the background 16 at a time, in levels, like an LSM tree. For CI, the depth-1 pack for each branch tip is built once and reused.
  • Baseline: Cursor's Origin does 120 pushes/s on S3 Standard (over 300 on S3 Express One Zone) through one log per repo. Landing on main runs one at a time, about 4 a second.
  • A differential test suite checks Aspen against stock Git's own server, run nightly on Git 2.30 through latest plus JGit. Still pre-alpha: no partial clones, SHA-256 repos or RSA SSH keys.

Takeaway. When many writers share one store, order writes only per key (here, per branch with a compare-and-swap) instead of per container. Store what clients upload as-is so reads stream stored bytes rather than recomputing them for each client.

Resend moves its email API from Node to Go: half the median latency, one-seventh the memory

A production rewrite of a public API, checked by a harness that compared the stored data and email bodies as well as the HTTP responses.

mateus-resende

Resend rewrote its email API from Node to Go in seven weeks and about 140 PRs. The trigger was bursts of large requests freezing whole tasks: parsing and serializing big JSON bodies blocked the Node event loop, even for requests the rate limiter was already rejecting. To prove the two versions behaved the same, a harness sent identical requests to Node and Go and compared responses, database rows, S3 objects, idempotency and rate limiting. With traffic split 50/50, Go cut POST /emails median latency from 58 to 31 ms and p99 from 498 to 420 ms. Memory per task fell from about 1.3 GB to about 180 MB, and tasks at peak from 34 to 10. Most of the remaining slow tail is time spent waiting on S3 and the database.

Under the hood

  • CPU per request fell to about 0.38× of Node's. Batch median latency went from 149 to 74 ms and batch p99 from 1,721 to 1,363 ms.
  • The Go HTML-to-text library gave different output, so they ported Node's exact behaviour. The port matched all 60 fixtures generated from Node.
  • Even so, they doubled task sizes twice during rollout, added per-request S3 upload timeouts, tuned Redis pools and added capacity before the top-of-hour spike.
  • They rebuilt by hand how Node turns the SDK's JSON-encoded Buffer attachments back into bytes. A dashboard of status-code percentages per service caught behaviour drift during the random split.

Takeaway. When you rewrite a service, compare everything it changes (database rows, stored objects, generated content) and not just the HTTP responses, and roll out on a random traffic split. Size capacity for bursts, not averages, and put a timeout on every call to storage.

dspy-wsp: a frontier model edits a small model's weights, no gradients

A new kind of optimizer: an LLM reads activations, finds a failure inside a small model and patches the weights by reasoning, with no training loop.

dbreunig

cmpnd released dspy-wsp, a DSPy optimizer based on MIT CSAIL's Weight-Space Programming post. A "writer" model, GPT-6-Astra, edits a small model's weights with no gradients. It works in a sandboxed Python kernel, has a fixed budget of forward passes, and its best edit ships as a hash-checked patch. On the Typed Decisions benchmark, a 25-pass run cost $5–10 and took 68–167 minutes. It added 1.9–16.1 points to five decision models (0.6B–9B), or 6.5–20.0 when stacked with ReAnchor, which refits answer thresholds. Most of the gain came from the writer recomputing the decision head itself. Under the paper's stricter rule, the gain fell to 2.8 points.

Under the hood

  • MIT Exp 8: Astra traced Qwen3.5-0.8B's looping to one direction written by the MLPs of layers 15 and 19, then removed it from 131,072 weights (0.016%). MATH-500 rose from 7.9% to 48.1%.
  • Limits: editing removes obstacles but does not add abilities; Gemma 3 270M, which does not loop, moved by 1 point at most. The paper's 50-pass run took 20 hours, and cmpnd has not reproduced it.
  • Guardrails block edits to embeddings, the output layer, the final norm and whole-tensor rescales. The writer model matters: with gpt-6.1-sol, d1-2B lost 0.6 points; with Astra it gained 13.4.

Takeaway. Treat weight editing as a repair tool for one failure you can diagnose, such as looping, not as a replacement for fine-tuning. Check where the gain comes from: here, recomputing the decision head and refitting thresholds did much of the work.

TigerBeetle widens all ledger amounts and balances to 128-bit integers

A short case study in sizing numeric fields for decades of accumulated balances, and what the extra width costs in the CPU and in client SDKs.

ksec

In this 2023 post, TigerBeetle explains why it moved every amount and balance from 64-bit to 128-bit unsigned integers. Each ledger counts in a user-defined smallest unit, such as cents. It also keeps debit and credit totals as two separate positive counters instead of one signed balance. At cent scale, a u64 tops out around $184.5 quadrillion. Smaller units shrink that limit quickly, and finer units are already needed: fuel priced to three decimals, stock ticks of 0.0001, micropayments, and digital currencies counted in micro-cents. Balances also grow for years. Because one transfer must be able to move an entire balance, amounts had to be widened along with balances.

Under the hood

  • Napkin math: $100 trillion at micro-cent scale (1e20) × 1M transfers/s × 1,000 years ≈ 3.15e36, still under 2^128 ≈ 3.4e38.
  • On x86-64, 128-bit add and subtract each add one extra instruction (adc or sbb), and multiply takes a few. Division calls the software routine __udivti3.
  • Most client languages (Java, Go) have no native 128-bit type, and .NET only added Int128 in .NET 7. So the clients pass each value as a pair of stack-allocated u64s, with helpers to convert to BigInteger, UUID or byte arrays.

Takeaway. Size money and counter fields for the total a balance can reach over the system's lifetime, not for one transaction, and remember that a transfer must be able to move that total. In SDKs, pass wide integers as fixed-size pairs of 64-bit values and leave arbitrary-precision math to the caller.

Featured · claude.com · Nov 13 · 2 min

Anthropic's blueprint for always-on agents: inbox, timers, memory, permissions, outbox

A concrete reference architecture for proactive agents that keep state across sessions, built from ordinary database tables and background workers.

Anthropic

Anthropic (William Hu) argues that an "always-on" agent mostly sleeps. It wakes for a run: it takes an event, does the work and saves state inside a session (the transcript of messages and tool calls). Five primitives sit around the ordinary agent loop. The event inbox is a durable table, an HTTP handler that acknowledges quickly, and a worker. Use one inbox per user with one active worker. Deduplicate on source IDs and batch pending events into one run. The inbox is the source of truth, so a crashed worker's events get retried. Timers live in a separate table: one-off follow-ups and recurring schedules. A scheduler polls that table and pushes due timers into the inbox as events.

Memory is files backed by a database or object store. It holds only what the agent can't look up again, with pointers for everything else: user instructions, user notes, past messages and task-progress files. Each run loads only an index and pulls individual files when it needs them. On permissions, deferred tool calls end the run. The user's answer comes back as an inbox event that resumes the saved session. An outbox with a dispatcher is optional. It lets you pace messages and recover from errors.

Under the hood

  • Failure modes the inbox prevents: concurrent events starting competing runs, and crashes losing events. Deep checks such as prompt-injection screening run inside the run, not in the handler.
  • If an event arrives mid-run, choose one of three options: defer it to the next run, fold it into the current run, or interrupt and restart.
  • Credentials stay outside the agent. A proxy injects tokens into outgoing requests, and Claude Code's auto-mode classifiers block risky actions.
  • Claimed cost trend: on one long-horizon coding benchmark, Opus 5.5 cost 78% less per solved task than Opus 5, versus 40% less on typical workloads.

Takeaway. Run every trigger, including user replies, timers and approvals, through one durable per-user inbox with a single worker. Concurrency, retries and resuming after an approval then become database problems rather than agent problems. Use evals to tune how often the agent messages the user, and start quiet.

Learn · today's concept

Planning in a latent world model with the Cross-Entropy Method (MPC)

A JEPA-style world model predicts the next latent state from the current latent and an action. It does not choose actions. To act, you need a planner that searches over action sequences and finds one whose predicted final latent lands close to the goal's latent. The cost of a plan, as a function of its actions, is bumpy and full of local minima, so gradient descent on actions often stalls. The standard answer is a sampling optimizer called the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM), used inside model predictive control (MPC).

Step by step: encode the current observation and the goal image into latents. Sample N action sequences of horizon H from a Gaussian. Roll each sequence through the predictor and score it, for example by the L2 distance between its final latent and the goal latent. Keep the top K sequences (the elites), refit the Gaussian's mean and standard deviation to them, and repeat for I iterations. Then execute only the first action or first few, observe the result, and plan again from the new state. This is called receding-horizon control.

Example: N=300, K=30, H=5, I=30 gives 9,000 rollouts, or 45,000 predictor calls, for every control step. That is why planning is usually the main compute cost. The trade-offs: the number of possible action sequences grows exponentially with the horizon, and prediction errors compound over long horizons. A fixed sample budget therefore misses good plans on long tasks, and scoring against one exact goal latent penalizes end states that are acceptable but not identical. That is where the lead's fixes apply. Use CEM-MPC when the action space is small and horizons are short. For long tasks, add subgoals, a goal set or a learned value function.

Further reading: Hafner et al., "Learning Latent Dynamics for Planning from Pixels" (PlaNet, ICML 2019); Zhou et al., "DINO-WM: World Models on Pre-trained Visual Features enable Zero-shot Planning" (2024)

Anthropic's method for safety evals: rubrics, simulated users, validated graders

A practical recipe for honest LLM-judged evals that carries over to agent and memory evals: how to build the grader, how many test items you need, and how to report uncertainty.

Anthropic describes how it builds safety evals, borrowing the reforms psychology adopted after its replication crisis. It scores an appropriate response rate against expert rubrics written for each risk area, not a refusal rate. One bad turn fails the whole conversation. Multi-turn tests always open with the same first prompt. After that, a Claude model plays the user, drawing persona, tone and tactic from a spec written by a domain expert, and a separate grader model scores the transcript. Single-turn suites are saturated: every recent model scores 94–98%. They recommend writing the analysis plan down before running (preregistration), at least 100 items for multi-turn and 1,000 for single-turn evals, paired comparisons, and confidence intervals on every score. Opus 5 at 98% versus Opus 5.5 at 94% was not a statistically significant difference.

Under the hood

  • Grader rules: atomic yes/no criteria; spell out what doesn't count; flag and exclude items where the grader breaks; check agreement with human labels using Cohen's kappa (agreement corrected for chance).
  • Known grader biases: judges favour their own model, longer answers and whichever option comes first. Show the grader only the transcript and re-grade with a different model or rubric.
  • Configuration matters: API and claude.ai scores differed by up to 34 points. One system-prompt line lifted the self-harm eval from 60% (API) to 94% (claude.ai).

Takeaway. Before trusting any LLM-judged agent or memory eval, write down what you are trying to measure, check the judge against blind human labels using kappa, and report the score with a confidence interval from paired runs on the same items. Use UK AISI's Inspect or Anthropic's Petri rather than building your own harness.

runway.com · Nov 11 · 2 min

Runway open-sources Praxis-1, a 27B MoE world model that outputs robot actions

You can download and inspect a world model that predicts video and robot actions together, and its technical report says where its errors actually come from.

Runway released Praxis-1 under Apache 2.0: the weights plus inference code and code for full and LoRA fine-tuning. The technical report describes a 26.9B mixture-of-experts video diffusion transformer (128 routed experts and one shared expert, each token uses 8 routed experts, about 2.8B parameters active per token). Its attention was retrained to be chunk-causal, meaning each 1.6 s chunk can only see earlier chunks. A separate 314M action expert reads eight backbone layers through cross-attention and outputs 24 actions at 15 Hz along with the predicted video. On NVIDIA's RoboLab-120 benchmark it reaches 46.2% success, against 42.9% for FLUX 3 Action and 28.0% for π0.5. Most of the remaining error comes from the video the model imagines, not from the action expert.

Under the hood

  • Each call takes about 0.55–0.6 s round trip on one H100 using 2 joint Euler steps and returns 1.6 s of motion. Opt-in real-time chunking cuts server p50 to about 128 ms.
  • Oracle test: when the model is given the true future video, action error falls to about one-fifth. Better video prediction is where the gains are.
  • A small head trained on frozen features of the predicted chunk, using only whole-episode outcomes, predicted real success with AUROC 0.77 (vs 0.64 from elapsed time).

Takeaway. Keep the world model and the policy head separate. Then an oracle test (give the head the true future) tells you which part is failing. The model's own imagined rollouts can also act as a cheap learned success signal.

Gemini 4 Hydrogen ships with Orchestrator, a planner that hands work to a cheaper executor

Google's Orchestrator passes work back and forth between models during a single agent trajectory, instead of routing once on the first prompt. If you build agent harnesses, it is a useful pattern to copy.

Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen, its fast tier. It costs $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens, at the same price across the whole 1M-token context. It has four thinking levels, ending in a new xhigh. It scores 51 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index (54 at xhigh), against 50 for the frontier model Argon. The bigger idea is Orchestrator in Antigravity, Google's coding-agent IDE. Carbon, the strong model, plans in read-only mode and sets explicit triggers for re-planning. Hydrogen then edits code and runs tests, and hands control back to Carbon when it gets stuck. Google says this reaches near-Carbon success while using 24% less quota and finishing 19% faster.

Under the hood

  • Google's case against static routers, which pick one model from the first prompt: long-running tasks change in difficulty partway through.
  • The two posts disagree on the Orchestrator benchmark: one says Terminal-Bench 4.0, the other 4.1. Neither gives the absolute success rates.
  • Hydrogen scores 57% on Terminal-Bench 4.1, against 18.2% for 3.8 Flash. Google says it cuts fabricated claims by more than 5x.
  • Hydrogen batches file reads and searches in parallel. Partners report it runs 2 to 2.8x faster and uses 12 to 23% fewer tokens than 3.8 Flash.
  • Neither post discloses architecture, parameter count or training recipe. All benchmarks are self-reported.

Takeaway. In your own agent loop, have the strong model write a plan with explicit re-planning triggers. Let a cheap model execute, and hand control back when it gets stuck, instead of routing each task once at the start.

Microsoft 365 outage: failing health checks pulled servers out until the rest collapsed

A 27-hour live example of a cascading failure. Health checks removed capacity faster than the remaining servers could absorb the load, and servers brought back failed again as soon as traffic reached them.

Microsoft 365 incident MO1550821 began at 18:26 UTC on November 12. The Register says it was still not fully resolved more than 27 hours later. Exchange Online servers began failing automated health checks and dropping out of rotation. Their load moved to the servers still running, which were then overwhelmed too. Microsoft said traffic had not risen. It said the fault "may originate from a specific component that's responsible for processing server health" and that "a core process on the affected servers" became overwhelmed. Every service that depends on Exchange went down with it: Outlook, Teams, Copilot, SharePoint, Intune and Defender.

The recovery is the part worth studying. Microsoft switched to an alternative health check method to bring servers back, but they "can become unresponsive again once they begin receiving traffic". They were healthy while idle and failed under full load, so they cycled in and out until enough capacity came back at once. When Microsoft scaled up VMs, some machines got "stuck in a bad state" and blocked its changes from syncing. This analysis comes from Control Plane, a hosting vendor, and draws on BleepingComputer and The Register. Microsoft has not published a post-incident review, so the trigger is still unknown.

Under the hood

  • Mitigations: paused all deployments, redistributed connection traffic, restarted servers, and considered reverting a few recent config updates even though no triggering change was found.
  • Microsoft extended the mail delivery timeout to 48 hours to keep delayed messages, and noted that sign-in could recover before mail flow did.
  • A later update refers to "the underlying cause of the traffic influx". Earlier, Microsoft had said the load was not driven by more traffic.
  • A separate Teams incident earlier that day (TM1550488, 9:45 to 10:53 ET) was blamed on resource contention. Microsoft has not said whether the two are linked.

Takeaway. A readiness check should test whether a replica can do its job. It should not fail just because the replica is busy, and it should never be able to pull more capacity than the remaining replicas can absorb. When you restore capacity, add it in batches with headroom. Servers returned one at a time get hit with the full load and fail again.

github.blog · Nov 13 · 1 min

GitHub's October outages: stale leader routing, reconnect storms, held pool connections

GitHub's October report shows the same few retry and connection-pool mistakes causing most of its outages, and these mistakes turn up in almost any backend.

Jakub Oleksy

GitHub now runs 140 of 151 services and 32 of 50 MySQL primaries in Azure Central US. The October incidents came from familiar mistakes. Oct 7: a new node in the system that routes database writes started up with stale records that still listed retired servers. It won leader election, and its bad routing settings passed validation and sent core writes to those servers. A maintenance task then ignored a pause and elected it leader again. Oct 21: brokers on the event-streaming platform were near their memory limits, and clients reconnected immediately after failed requests, overloading them in a cascade. The fix was randomized reconnect delays. Oct 30: update requests held a pooled connection through a lookup that took several seconds, and the shared pool ran dry.

Under the hood

  • Oct 19: every actions server retried its lost cache connections at once and saturated the link between the datacenter and the cloud region. The fix was a cap on how fast the service opens new connections.
  • Oct 6: the Enterprise Importer sent a burst of very large queries that used up a shared database's connections. Its queries are now small and fixed in size, and automatic backoff under database strain is being added.
  • Moving an actions endpoint that serves more than 250M requests a day to a more efficient path cut peak database reads by 85%. Requiring at least three characters for topic search cut slow queries by about 98%.

Langfuse's Rust LLM gateway relays provider bytes unchanged and caps admission by memory

A small, clear design for an LLM proxy: pass-through relay, memory-only admission control, and best-effort telemetry that drops data rather than grow without limit.

Case Western Reserve researchers ran 1,668 GRPO runs on six small Qwen and Llama models, giving every method the same budget in generated tokens and in training GPU-hours. Under the hood

  • On the same token budget, outcome-only GRPO makes 584 updates and the k=4 Monte-Carlo reward makes 50. With k=1 the reward still spends 70% of tokens on continuations.
  • The gradient test ranks the rewards in the same order as training but overestimates final accuracy by 0.8–1.8 points. A reward with alignment cosine 0.84 lost 12.4 points at high weight.
  • Prompt filter: a ridge critic reads the prompt's hidden state, keeps the 6 of 24 candidates nearest 1/2 plus 2 random ones, and helps only when mean predicted success is below 1/2.

Takeaway. When you judge an RL training change, compare it at matched generated tokens and GPU-hours, and against a baseline with a retuned learning rate. Treat gradient-alignment checks as a way to rank options, not as proof that a change helps.

NOPO: RL for LLMs that trains only on failures the verifier can prove

When your checks can only prove that an answer is wrong, rewarding the answers that pass teaches shortcuts. This NeurIPS spotlight learns from the proven failures alone.

Jiacheng Xu, Shuo He, Fuxiang Zhang, Chaojie Wang, Bo An

Nanyang Technological University and Skywork AI (NeurIPS 2026 spotlight) define one-sided verifiability: public unit tests, constraint checks or an LLM error-finder reliably catch bad answers, but a pass only means no error was found. Before RL, 20–77% of passing answers were actually wrong. GRPO, the usual group-baseline RL method, rewards those false passes. NOPO treats passes as unlabeled. It applies the NPO unlearning loss only to failures. That loss fades once a failure's probability falls below the reference model's. Each group's update is scaled by (c/G)^γ, the share of rollouts that passed, so a group where every rollout failed gets no update. On Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B, average pass@16 rises to 52.86 and 52.13, against 48.93 and 48.27 for GRPO.

Under the hood

  • CodeContests, Qwen3-4B, pass@512: NOPO 66.95, base 51.05, GRPO 48.54. GRPO has the best pass@1 (37.23) but ends up below the base model.
  • NOPO ranks first in 9 of 10 benchmark–model cells. It loses OlympiadBench on 8B to GRPO, where the LLM verifier flags only 9–13% of truly wrong answers.
  • Ablation: γ=1 (a linear weight) works best and beats plain NPO and negative-only REINFORCE. All runs use G=16 rollouts per prompt.

Takeaway. If your reward check can only prove failure, don't reward passes. Penalize failures relative to a reference model, and skip prompts where every rollout failed.
