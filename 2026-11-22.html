Riemann Hypothesis proof made with Claude passes independent Lean checks

A 17.4-million-line machine proof was trusted within a day because of how it was checked: a vetted spec, independent checkers and an inert export. That is a pattern worth copying for agent pipelines with verifiable rewards.

On Nov 20 the number theorist Levent Alpöge released a Lean 4 library of about 17.4M lines in 175,187 files. He says he wrote it with Claude, and it proves the Riemann Hypothesis and the Generalized RH for Dirichlet L-functions. The main theorem targets Mathlib's own RiemannHypothesis definition, so the statement does not come from the author's code. By Nov 21 the Lean and Mathlib communities, with the Lean FRO's endorsement, said they had "full confidence" the proof is correct. Scott Aaronson's post, which carries the news, updated to say the same.

The checking is the part to study. Dozens of people compiled the library and got the same raw proof export. A separate file that imports only Mathlib states the theorem, and two independent tools (comparator, con-leche --compare) confirmed the proved theorem matches it exactly. The export then passed Lean's kernel, con-leche and con-ron, plus third-party checkers written in other languages. con-leche and con-ron come with formal proofs, relative to set theory, that they cannot accept a false proof. That answers Aaronson's worry that past Lean kernel bugs once allowed proofs of 0=1. All checks ran on an inert export, out of reach of any malicious code in the sources.

Alpöge's 4-page note describes the mathematics. Stepanov's proof of RH over function fields needs "multiplicity", which has no obvious analogue over the integers. Alpöge steered the work toward a setting that matched a toy problem. Claude then suggested chaining iterated theta lifts between unitary groups, with each lift standing in for one order of multiplicity, and a chain about √X long for a von Mangoldt sum of length X. He says Claude "far outpaced" him near the end. He also generated 250,000+ pages of informal text but published only the Lean. No human understands the proof yet, and Aaronson expects distilling it to take years. On HN, people asked whether anyone can grasp it at all, and one commenter called it "Lean + stochastic predictor = brute-force theorem solver", in the spirit of AlphaGo.

Under the hood

  • Timeline from Alpöge's note: the Stepanov-determinant idea came on 10/6/26, the iterated theta lifts on 11/15, and the full GRH result on 11/16.
  • Other tools in the proof include Iwasawa towers, the Rallis seesaw, the Bost slope inequality, a relative trace formula and a weakened Vandiver conjecture.
  • Mirror's text scan found 0 sorry, admit, axiom declarations, native_decide or unsafe. Four files turn off the heartbeat (proof-time) limit, and one addDecl command still goes through the kernel.
  • Build: Lean v4.33.1 with a pinned Mathlib commit and maxHeartbeats 4,000,000. The archive is 239 MB, and src/Development alone holds 12.2M lines.
  • A rival human-only GRH draft (Masullo, 940 pages, announced by Ravi Vakil) is unfinished: 27 'Return here' footnotes, and its Lean formalization has already found 11 false statements.

Takeaway. When agents produce output too large to read, get trust from a human-vetted spec, a check that the output matches it, and several small independent checkers run on an inert export. Build RL rewards and eval harnesses the same way.

OpenComputer runs many coding-agent sessions on one shared VM instead of per-session sandboxes

A concrete agent-runtime design: one shared VM, git worktrees for isolation, git for session state, and a sandbox only when a session needs one.

Digger's open-source cloud coding agent can be self-hosted on AWS, GCP or Azure. Each team gets one VM. Sessions on it are isolated in git worktrees, and each turn is saved to a git store, so another VM can resume any session. A sandbox is used only for heavy builds or preview environments. In their Slack test (bump zod in 4 repos and open PRs, median of 5 runs), the first push came in 24s, against 53–72s for Devin and three other agents. Commenters asked how it compares to OpenHands; the thread has no answer.

Under the hood

  • Gateway (front door) keeps a small SQLite database: which session runs on which VM, known people and VMs, and messages queued for offline VMs. It runs no agents, so it can restart freely.
  • All 4 PRs open with CI green: OpenComputer 56s, Capy 85s, Replicas 87s, Hoplite 95s, Devin 185s. OpenComputer completed 5 of 6 runs; Devin and Capy completed 5 of 5.
  • The team found that above about 640 session-hours a month, one always-on 4 vCPU VM costs less than per-session E2B sandboxes. They say one shared VM can often handle hundreds of concurrent sessions.

Takeaway. If you run agent sessions, try a shared host with worktree isolation and git-backed state, and start a sandbox only for heavy work. This avoids cold starts and keeps cost from growing linearly with session count. The benchmark is the vendor's own, but the repo is public, so you can rerun it.

OpenAI's Sol 6.2 and Astra 6.2 rank lower than their predecessors on SimpleBench

This independent common-sense test moved the opposite way from other benchmarks, which matters if your agents get vague, underspecified instructions.

SimpleBench is a multiple-choice test of more than 200 trick, spatio-temporal and social-reasoning questions. Nine non-specialist humans averaged 83.7%. The fetched page still shows Claude Fable on top at 81.9% and gives no 6.2 scores. The rankings come from an HN commenter: Sol 6.2 is 30th, down from 10th for Sol 6.1, and Astra 6.2 is 15th, down from 9th for Astra 6. Fable 5.2 took first. Other commenters say the 6.2 models are thorough at code review and maths but poor at working out what a vague request means.

Under the hood

  • Scores are AVG@5 (the mean of five runs) at temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.95. o1-series models are the exception.
  • Per the same commenter, every new Google model is above the human baseline, and Astra 6 Pro is the only OpenAI model above it (8th).
  • Dissent on HN: synthetic 'benchmaxxer' scores don't match real use, and one heavy user rates the 6.2 models comparable to Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2.
  • One user says Sol 6.2 never questions its instructions or existing code and falls back to 'you're right to push back' when challenged.

Takeaway. Being strong at precise tasks doesn't mean being good at inferring intent. If your agent gets underspecified tasks, add evals with ambiguous prompts before you switch models.

DreamCoder: a browser-based, tiling coding harness from Mastering Emacs' author

Most of the design effort went into how one person watches many parallel agents, which is the open problem in harness UX.

The Mastering Emacs author spent 15 months building DreamCoder, a standalone coding harness that runs in the browser. He claims the usual features: MCP, background sub-agents and shell commands, skills, hooks, a "99% cache hit" rate and runs that last for days. The new part is the interface. Workspaces are tiled, and each one has a minimap where panes pulse while working, stand out when blocked and turn green when idle. On HN, one tester reported about 350 MB of idle memory from the Python backend and saw provider integrations fail.

Under the hood

  • Window manager: each pane has a minimum, a comfortable and a maximum size. A constraint solver fits new panes and moves any that don't fit to a hidden bar that still shows them live.
  • Keyboard-first: Alt+x for global commands, Alt+c for pane commands, Alt+m to jump between panes. There is avy-style hint selection and a Helm-style search-as-you-type palette.
  • Imports Pi, OpenCode and Codex configs. The author praises Qwen 3.8 27B for local-only development.
  • HN prior art: a Clojure harness that maps each session to an nREPL namespace, so the agent's variables and functions stay live for the user.

Takeaway. When you run several agents in parallel, the key information is each one's state: working, blocked on a prompt, or idle. Show that state for all of them at a glance so nobody has to cycle through terminals to find it.

onurgumus.github.io · Nov 21 · HN ▲141, 102 comments · 2 min

Zero C#: a bflat fork that compiles C# to native code with no GC, using arenas

A small, clear design for arena lifetimes, plus a convention for marking which lines free memory. Worth borrowing in any language without a GC.

Zero C# forks bflat, a C# compiler built on NativeAOT, to produce native binaries with no .NET runtime and no garbage collector. Hello world comes out at 11 KB on Windows, 40 KB on Linux, 16 KB as a WASI module and 16 KB on DOS/386. new calls calloc and nothing ever frees it, so real allocation goes through bump-pointer arenas (memory freed all at once), one per lifetime. Under C# 15's preview safety rules, calls that free memory are marked unsafe. You give up catching exceptions, reflection and async.

Under the hood

  • Arena blocks grow along a Fibonacci sequence (128, 128, 256, 384, 640…). Reset() merges the blocks into one, so a loop that resets each round stops calling malloc after the first.
  • Known hazard: a collection created before Save() that grows afterwards gets its new buffer freed by Restore(). DebugChecks fills freed memory with 0xDD bytes so stale reads stand out. AllocateObjectsHere sends ordinary new, closures and boxing into an arena.
  • HN pushback: NativeAOT plus Span, unsafe code and Marshal already cover most of this. The standard library is different, so existing code won't port, and Rust or Zig were designed for life without a GC.

Takeaway. Arenas tied to lifetimes work well: one for the whole program, one per request that you reset, one for scratch work, with PeakUsed telling you the first block size. Mark the calls that free memory, not the ones that allocate, and put a SAFETY comment on each, so a code search shows every line that needs review.

How vLLM serves Inkling 2 Mini, a model that loops its middle layers

The model reuses the same weights for three passes but keeps a separate KV cache for each, and vLLM supports that without changing its cache manager.

Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini is a multimodal MoE with 26B total and 3.5B active parameters and up to 1M tokens of context. Its 24 layers split into a prelude (layers 0–5), a loop core (6–17) and a coda (18–23). The core runs three passes with shared weights. Unlike a plain looped model, where each pass feeds the next, every pass here starts from the prelude's output. A later pass sees the one before only through messages. Each core sublayer input (query, key, value and MLP) reads its own message from the previous pass, and a rank-64 gated controller merges it in. That is 48 messages per token. Each pass also keeps its own KV cache and conv-state cache, so 24 layers need 48 of each.

vLLM handles this by registering each extra pass as an ordinary attention layer, so the KV cache manager and prefix caching needed no new logic. It fuses each layer's four message reads into one kernel and runs the controllers as batched GEMMs. A separate research checkpoint lets you choose the number of passes at startup. On one GB200, a batch-1 decode step takes 1.51, 2.67 and 3.85 ms for 1, 2 and 3 passes, and KV cache capacity falls from 352K to 233K to 175K tokens.

Under the hood

  • Controller math: out = (1 − tanh(W_forget·h))·input + tanh(W_gain·h)·message, where h = W_down·[rms(input), rms(message)]. The merge runs in FP32 without FMA (fused multiply-add) to match the reference.
  • In later passes, the sliding-window layers take three separate Q/K/V inputs instead of one, because each input gets a different message.
  • On the research checkpoint, loop_share_cache makes all passes share one KV cache. With 3 passes that gives 194K tokens of capacity, below the 233K from running 2 passes, because conv states stay per pass.
  • The 3-layer DSpark speculative draft reads hidden states from inside the loop, so changing the pass count disables it. The MoE has 128 experts (top-8) in NVFP4, with MXFP8 in the last 4 layers. MoE outputs match the reference bitwise.

Takeaway. If a new architecture needs per-step state, try to express it as more of a primitive your system already manages, as vLLM did by treating each extra pass as another attention layer. Check what a cheaper option actually saves: here, sharing the KV cache saved less memory than dropping a pass.

Google puts an LLM judge between agent and model to veto tool calls

If you build agents, this is a clear design for guardrails that sit outside the agent and stop off-intent actions and indirect prompt injection.

Google's Semantic governance policies, now in Preview, work through Agent Gateway, a proxy that carries all traffic between the agent and its model. When the model returns a tool call, an LLM judge checks it before it reaches the agent. It runs two checks. The first asks whether the call matches what the user requested; this is on by default and blocks injected actions. The second checks plain-language policies written by compliance teams. If the call fails, the judge replaces the whole response with a denial message. Responses that are only text are not checked, and neither are streamed responses.

Under the hood

  • The judge sees the conversation so far, the agent's system instructions, the tool descriptions and the policies. It is told to deny calls to tools that have no description.
  • Each verdict, the judge's rationale and the tokens it used go to Cloud Logging. A dry-run mode logs what would be denied without blocking anything.
  • It works with the Gemini API, the Anthropic Messages API and OpenAI-compatible Chat Completions, including self-hosted vLLM. A policy can cover all tools, one tool, or named parameters.
  • Google says verdicts are probabilistic and may vary. Facts a policy depends on, such as an earnings date, must be in the system instructions. Results on the AgentDojo benchmark are promised in a later post.

Takeaway. Put action checks outside the agent, at the response from the model, so a prompt injection cannot talk the agent out of them. Write tool descriptions with care, because the judge relies on them to understand each call.

OpenAI: agents finish 46% of 2–4 hour research tasks unaided, but unevenly

OpenAI's internal numbers show where coding agents work in AI R&D and where they don't. Building RL and eval environments is one of the weak spots.

This is OpenAI's second internal report on research acceleration. From March to October, the share of tasks agents completed with no human intervention rose from 23% to 46% for 2–4 hour tasks and from 13% to 35% for 4–8 hour tasks. Results vary a lot by task: monitoring training runs 79%, answering technical questions 72%, building eval, RL and agent environments 29%, inference and serving code 25%. Median spend per researcher reached $2,400/day at API list prices, and agent workdays per researcher went from 0.5 to 5.7. Interviewed researchers say agents still have poor taste, open "AI slop" PRs and go down rabbit holes.

Under the hood

Valkey 9.2 swaps the sorted-set skiplist for a 61-way B+ tree

A worked example of how node size, allocator size classes and cache behaviour cut per-entry overhead by 36–43% in an in-memory index.

Valkey 9.2, now a release candidate, replaces the skiplist behind large sorted sets (those past the listpack threshold, where small sets are stored packed in one block) with fbtree, a 61-way B+ tree. The hashtable that maps each member to its score stays. Each skiplist node cost about 37B: an 8B back pointer, an average of 1.33 levels at 16B each for pointer plus span, and an 8B score. Every step was also a dependent fetch into a separately allocated node. fbtree sizes its nodes to jemalloc size classes, so a 512B leaf holds 61 pointers. Each stored value packs a normalized 8B score together with the member bytes. Inner nodes keep the prefix the child anchors share, plus a 4-byte "feature" per child. A SIMD compare of those features often picks the right child before any string is fetched. Leaves are doubly linked so scans never climb the tree, and the outermost leaves are cached so pops stay O(1).

With 5M 20-byte members, index overhead per member fell from 50.3B to 28.5B for sequential inserts (−43%, ~109 MB) and to 32.0B for random inserts (−36%). On Graviton3 with 3M members, ZADD score updates rose 105%, ZREM 76%, ZCOUNT 27% and ZRANK 19%. The only visible change is that OBJECT ENCODING now returns btree.

Under the hood

  • The skiplist promotes a node with 25% probability, which gives an effective branching factor of about 4. fbtree's fanout is 61, so the tree is far shallower at the same O(log n).
  • On 32-bit builds the innerNode is rounded up to the 1280-byte jemalloc class. On 64-bit builds, leaf and inner nodes fit their size classes with no wasted space.
  • Random inserts save less (~92 MB vs ~109 MB) because they leave more nodes partly full. The savings are per member, so sets of many small members gain the most.
  • Benchmark setup: c7g.metal, 9 I/O threads, pipeline depth 10, five runs, CI ≤2%, on the PR #4206 branch. Range scans gain only 1–6%; ZSCORE gains 2% because it uses the hashtable.

Takeaway. If an index pays for pointers per entry, pack entries into wide nodes sized to the allocator's size classes, and store the comparison key next to its payload. Keep O(1) end pointers and linked leaves so pops and scans don't get slower. Update any monitoring that checks for the literal string skiplist.

Four lesser-known sketches in Go: fuse filters, UltraLogLog, log-linear histograms, MinHash

Each one is a cheaper or more accurate replacement for a common backend tool, with benchmarks against named Go libraries.

The unsure Go package adds four sketches. The binary fuse filter is a static set: each item's fingerprint equals the XOR of three array cells. It uses 9 bits per item at 0.39% false positives, against 12 for a Bloom filter, and checks are as fast, but builds are up to 2.5× slower. UltraLogLog packs the max rank and two "saw k−1, k−2" bits into each byte register. It matches HyperLogLog accuracy in about half the memory, but is slower. The log-linear histogram gets its bucket from the float64 exponent and top mantissa bits, so every quantile stays within 0.39% relative error. Under the hood

Learn · today's concept

GRPO: group-relative advantages without a critic

PPO trains a language model with a per-token advantage, which says how much better an action was than expected. To estimate "expected", PPO uses a value network (a critic), usually about as large as the policy. That roughly doubles memory, and the critic is hard to train when the only reward arrives at the end of a long answer or a multi-turn episode. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), introduced in DeepSeekMath, drops the critic and takes the baseline from the policy's own samples instead.

Here are the steps. For each prompt, sample a group of G completions from the current policy. Score each one with a reward, such as a unit test or a verifier. Normalize within the group: A_i = (r_i − mean) / (std + ε). Every token of completion i gets that same advantage. Then update with PPO's clipped probability-ratio objective, usually with a KL penalty toward a reference model. For example, take G = 4 with rewards 1, 0, 0, 1. The mean is 0.5 and the std is 0.5, so the advantages are +1, −1, −1, +1. The two passing answers are pushed up and the two failures pushed down. If all four score 0, every advantage is 0 and the prompt teaches nothing.

That last case is the variance warning in MiniMind's takeaway. With sparse rewards, many groups have zero variance. Fixes include harder but still solvable prompts, larger groups, or dropping groups where every reward is the same (DAPO's dynamic sampling). The costs: you pay for G rollouts per prompt, credit assignment is coarse because one score covers every token, and per-response length normalization adds a bias that later variants remove. CISPO, a related method that MiniMind also implements, clips the importance-sampling weights instead of zeroing the gradients of clipped tokens. Use GRPO when rewards are verifiable and a critic is too expensive. Use PPO with a critic and GAE when you have dense per-step rewards.

Further reading: DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models (Shao et al., 2024); Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms (Schulman et al., 2017)

AFFECT: a loss that makes video world models respond to small action changes

Video world models can look realistic and still ignore the actions they are given. This auxiliary loss fixes that with logged action noise, and it needs no repeated rollouts and no outcome labels.

Jinwoo Shin's group at KAIST proposes AFFECT, an extra training loss for robot video world models. A play policy (trained on free-form teleoperation with no task) runs with temporally correlated noise added to its action chunks, and that noise is logged. The loss makes generated videos co-vary with the noise the way recorded videos do. On DROID, the correlation between policy success in the world model and on the real robot rises from 0.47 to 0.79, and FVD (a video-realism distance) drops 23%.

Under the hood

  • The loss matches the covariance of centered perturbation features with frozen video-latent features. It is a finite-feature MMD (maximum mean discrepancy, a distribution-matching distance). Errors that depend only on the scene cancel out.
  • Generated futures come from fresh noise via a short differentiable Euler sampler. One-step denoising of the target would leak the answer. The loss runs every 8 iterations, weighted to 15% of the flow loss.
  • Tested on DreamDojo and Cosmos3. Correlation improved in all 6 backbone–robot pairs; Cosmos3 on ALOHA-2 went from 0.298 to 0.694. Planning success rose 8.3 points. Shuffled-noise and unconditional-matching ablations both did worse.

Takeaway. Judge a conditional generator by whether its outputs move correctly when the input condition changes, not only by how realistic they look. If you inject random perturbations and log them, you can use them as free supervision for matching covariance.

Tideway pools VRAM and RAM to run large MoE models on consumer PCs

A clear case study in tiered caching that survives scans, with a way to keep CUDA Graph replay when weights page in mid-step.

Yifei Wang, Tsun-yu Yang, Yechen Xu, Hancheng Ye, Qinsi Wang, Baijun Zhou, Tingjun Chen, Hai "Helen" Li et al. — Duke University

Duke's Tideway runs MoE (mixture-of-experts) models on an RTX 5090 with 16–128 GiB of host RAM. It treats VRAM and RAM as one deduplicated pool of experts, with the SSD as a read-only backing store. During prefill it pins ("anchors") the experts already in RAM and sends SSD reads through a small streaming window. During decode it uses fixed-address VRAM slots, so piecewise CUDA Graphs (captured kernel sequences replayed without per-kernel launches) keep working when an expert misses. Against the fastest of FreeToken, llama.cpp and reimplemented FlashMoE/SSD-LLaMA, it averages 1.97× end-to-end, 2.40× TTFT and 1.61× TPOT, and is up to 3.14× faster on coding-agent traces.

Under the hood

  • Per-tier LRU breaks under prefill's layer-by-layer cyclic scan: with 64 GiB RAM, baselines read 86–99% of prefill expert traffic from SSD. Tideway reads 52%.
  • When an expert moves up to VRAM, its RAM copy is marked replaceable. VRAM victims with no RAM copy are written back over the idle D2H link (57 GB/s, versus 6.8 GB/s SSD).
  • Ablation on DeepSeek-V4-Flash with 32K input and 1K output: memory manager 1.23×, async pipelining 1.36×, piecewise decode graphs 1.36×, for 2.28× overall.

Takeaway. For any tiered cache that sees scans, pin the reusable working set and send scan traffic through a small separate window. When evicting from a fast tier, move the item down a tier instead of dropping its last copy.

Tencent open-sources Hy4, a 770B/49B-active MoE with 1M context

Another large Chinese open-weight model built on DeepSeek-style sparse attention, with a speculative-decoding drafter that reads the main model's KV cache instead of keeping its own.

Tencent's Hy Team released Hy4, which takes text, image and video input. It is a 770B-total, 49B-active MoE (mixture-of-experts: only some sub-networks run for each token) with 78 layers. The first is dense and the other 77 are MoE, each with 256 routed experts plus 1 shared. Each token uses its top 8 routed experts. Context is 1M tokens. Attention is Gated DeepSeek Sparse Attention on top of MLA: a small indexer picks the top-2,048 past tokens for each query, and IndexCache reuses those choices across layers. The residual path uses identity Hyper-Connections, which keep 4 residual streams per layer instead of one. Instruct, FP8 and base weights ship under the Tencent Hy License.

The serving design is the clever part. DFly2 is a built-in 5-layer, 3.3B-parameter drafter for speculative decoding. It reuses Hy4's KV cache and indexer picks, so each draft token reads about 2K keys at any context length and needs no extra KV memory. In base-model tests Hy4 beats DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base on SuperGPQA (62.7 vs 54.8) and SimpleQA (57.2 vs 52.1), but trails it on MBPP-plus (74.0 vs 79.1). Tencent says a clear gap to top proprietary models remains.

Under the hood

  • MLA (multi-head latent attention): 64 heads, q_lora_rank 2,048, kv_lora_rank 512. Indexer: 32 heads of dim 128. Hidden size 6,144, vocabulary 120,832.
  • DFly2 skips prefill. With prefill and decode on separate machines, the handoff adds only one last-layer hidden state. vLLM is configured with num_speculative_tokens=5 and FLASHMLA_SPARSE.
  • Finance eval with 213 professionals on 579 tasks, scored out of 4: Hy4 3.04, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash 2.97, Kimi K3.1 Pro and GLM-5.3 2.68 each.
  • Internal search evals: hallucination rate fell from 15.76% (Hy4 preview) to 5.45%, and speculative errors from 23.1% to 5.6%.

Takeaway. Hy4's speculative decoding gets most of its value from DFly2 sharing the main model's KV cache and sparse indices: draft cost stays flat as context grows, and the drafter needs no memory of its own. With sparse attention, a drafter can reuse what the big model already computed instead of keeping its own cache.

inclusionAI's 10M-parameter probe adds safety and hallucination scores to DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash output

A small probe that reads the model's own hidden states scores every token as it streams, with no separate 8B guard model to call.

inclusionAI

inclusionAI released SingProbe for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017. It is a 10.10M-parameter attention probe (a small classifier that reads the base model's internal activations) that taps hidden states from layers 12, 25 and 39 during generation. At every token it scores 8 query-intent labels, response unsafety and hallucination risk. Against 8B guard models, it scores slightly higher on query F1 (0.8756 vs 0.8714) and response F1 (0.8668 vs 0.8604). The bigger gain is on streaming token AUC: 0.9522 vs Qwen3Guard-Stream-8B's 0.8893. It trails DRIFT on hallucination detection (0.7951 vs 0.8000).

Under the hood

  • Probe architecture: 8 attention heads, head dimension 64, a 2,048-token window, bfloat16 weights. The method and training code are in a technical report and a GitHub repo.
  • The benign-response false-positive rate is 0.009% over 5 datasets, measured at a pre-calibration threshold of 0.987179. Release thresholds are calibrated to 0.5.
  • Serving needs a custom SGLang branch with a --probe-ckpt flag, run with TP=8/EP=8 on eight H20s. Scores come back in meta_info.token_probe_probs as label_0 to label_9.

Takeaway. If you already serve the model, a probe on its hidden states can replace a separate guard model and give per-token scores for close to zero extra compute. Before you rely on it, check what tapping those layers requires from your inference server.
