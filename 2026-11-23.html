Canary prompts show inference providers leaking and keeping 'zero retention' data

If your agents send prompts through routers or resold models, a privacy flag only covers the first hop, and two million canary prompts show what happens after it.

OpenRouter has removed three providers, taken 125 endpoints off Zero Data Retention (ZDR, the promise that prompts and responses are never stored) eligibility, and notified affected customers. This follows a Dragos study led by HD Moore. Between July and November 2026, Dragos sent about two million prompts. Each one carried its own canaries (fake secrets that reveal any reader who uses them): real AWS keys tied to empty, fully logged accounts, plus DNS names, URLs, and git and SSH endpoints. OpenRouter says it was not breached itself. It plans to run the same tests continuously and to check network paths for undisclosed forwarding.

There were three kinds of finding. First, attackers had broken into three hosting providers. In the largest case they copied every request sent during windows in late October, including requests rejected before any model ran, which puts the copying at the provider's front door. They tried stolen keys within hours, then went through S3, Secrets Manager and Bedrock and added IAM users, making tens of thousands of AWS API calls. Second, marketplace resellers listed models at the lowest price, turned on request logging, and tried the stolen secrets by hand. Third, a Chinese lab's scraper fetched the URLs from more than half of its prompts, including about two-thirds of those marked ZDR. Prompts sent through U.S. resellers of that lab's models triggered the same scraper. Novita confirms it forwarded MiniMax requests to MiniMax's own API from May 28 to Nov 11. Together traces its leak to an AI coding agent that committed a Hugging Face credential to a public repo.

Dragos also built a kind of traceroute for inference. It used image URLs that log every system that fetches them, plus deliberately broken requests whose nested error messages show each hop. In about 2,000 traced routes, several hundred included a system it could not identify. HN commenters noted that a direct ZDR contract is better but still depends on trust. Affected users said they now limit OpenRouter to providers they choose themselves instead of the default pool.

Under the hood

  • Dragos's canary service derives each prompt's hostnames, URLs and credentials from one seed, so any hit traces back to the exact prompt, model, router and privacy setting.
  • One stolen key was used the day after it was seeded and was still in use three weeks later. Over that time the attacker's tools changed from a fake-Chrome script to Boto3 to the AWS CLI on Kali.
  • New detection strings: an LLM-driven pentest framework, ARTEX, called AWS with the user agents artex-agent and artex-judge-r121. They are not in public indicator lists.
  • Together's leaked samples were stored test prompts from MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6. Its fixes include short-lived or scoped credentials, credential scans on every PR, and ZDR on by default for new accounts.
  • Route tracing also found a seller that silently adds about 1,000 hidden tokens to every prompt. It also found a link posted in a Feishu (Lark) chat 24 days after a ZDR request was sent to a U.S. provider.

Takeaway. Put a canary credential in the context your agents already send, and treat any use of it as an incident. Choose upstream providers explicitly instead of taking a router's cheapest default, and keep real secrets out of anything an agent can read.

Riemann Hypothesis proof made with Claude passes several independent Lean kernels

It shows how to trust a huge AI-generated artifact: a small trusted spec, mechanical statement matching, independent checkers and sandboxed byte-for-byte rebuilds.

Levent Alpöge, working with Claude, released a 17-million-line Lean library proving the Riemann Hypothesis and GRH for Dirichlet L-functions (Hecke and automorphic L-functions are not covered). The Lean and Mathlib communities certify it against the Xena blog's four tests. The statement is Mathlib's own RiemannHypothesis, the code compiles, two independent tools match the proved theorem to a challenge file that imports only Mathlib, and several kernels accept the proof. Alpöge published only the Lean, not his 250,000+ page informal write-up. Its central idea is a chain of about √X theta lifts that imitates the multiplicity trick from Stepanov's function-field argument.

Andrew Sutherland's MIT verification page, run with Claude Code, holds the engineering detail. All 175,187 modules compile, and the 61.6 GB export uses only the three standard axioms. A sandboxed rebuild with no network and read-only source reproduced the export byte for byte. One compiled module differed by 38 bytes because parallel elaboration raced to name a bound variable; Lean v4.36.0 fixes this. On HN, commenters noted that a fake Collatz refutation once got past one checker and that the checkers are less independent than claimed.

Under the hood

  • Kernel runs: con-leche accepted 1,468,418 declarations in 3m15s on 64 threads, nanoda (Rust) took 27m42s on 80 threads and con-ron 7m06s.
  • con-leche and con-ron carry formal proofs, relative to set theory, that they cannot accept a false proof. All checks ran on an inert export, not the sources.
  • A cross-check without Mathlib's zeta: combined with an independent Robin1984 library, the proof yields Robin's (n>5040) and Lagarias's divisor-sum inequalities.
  • To reproduce: con-leche --compare needs about 50 GB of RAM and under 4 minutes on 64 threads. The full lake comparator needs about 100 GB of RAM.

Takeaway. When you accept large AI-generated output, keep a small human-vetted spec apart from the untrusted solution, check mechanically that the two match, and verify with independent checkers on an inert artifact. Make the build reproducible enough that you can explain every byte that differs.

miniPG: a full Postgres server and client in one executable, used like sqlite3

A real Postgres you can create and delete per test or per agent task, without Docker, plus a clear account of why the WASM build PGlite failed first.

progrium's miniPG packs real Postgres into one executable for Mac, Windows and Linux. You point it at a directory and run SQL with minipg ./mydb "select ...". It can also act as a server (minipg serve) or start a program with the Postgres connection variables set (minipg run ./mydb -- tool). His first try was PGlite, the WASM build, which deadlocked because it allows only one connection. The second ran an x86 emulator inside a webview and took 7s to start. Hyper9, his Plan 9 runtime, brought startup down to 800ms.

Under the hood

  • PGlite failure: the app opens a transaction, then queries on a second connection. With only one connection, the query waits on a transaction that is waiting for it.
  • Attempt 2 ran v86 (an x86 emulator) in a headless webview. Postgres traffic and the data mount went over a WebSocket to the host. It took 7s to accept connections on an M3.
  • Hyper9 runs Plan 9 executables at near-native speed. It combines system API translation (the Wine/Proton approach) with hypervisors. Startup fell to 800ms.
  • Aimed at single-user local use. The author says it does not replace server deployments with replicas or many users.

Takeaway. If a test or agent needs Postgres features like JSONB, pgvector or multiple connections, a per-task miniPG directory can replace a Docker container. Expect about 800ms to start, and check that it works on your platform first.

fast-anything profiles your app and has an agent port only the hot functions to Rust

A useful pattern for agent tools: record real calls, then replay them against the agent's rewrite before you accept it.

fast-anything is an MIT-licensed CLI. It profiles an app written in Python, TypeScript, Go, Java, Ruby, PHP, C/C++, C# or R, ranks functions by time × portability, records up to 500 real calls, and has a coding agent port one function into a single-function Rust crate. The port is enabled only if the recorded calls and derived edge cases (NaN, -0.0, non-ASCII text, inputs up to 16× larger) all match the original, and only if it is also faster per call. Python fuzzy dedupe went from 4.91s to 42ms. HN commenters replied that real slowness is usually spread thinly across a whole system.

Under the hood

  • It ports the outermost loop it can, so the app crosses into Rust once. Porting only the inner levenshtein gave 35× instead of 116×.
  • Python uses PyO3 and JS/TS uses napi-rs. Every other language calls one C-ABI entry point that takes and returns JSON, via FFI, Fiddle, FFM or dlopen.
  • Verify caught a port that compared bytes instead of characters, failing 148 of 500 calls. Floats must match within 1e-9. FAST_ANYTHING_DISABLE=1 runs the original code.
  • Java gained only 3× end-to-end, 11× on the function itself, because JVM startup dominates the run.

Takeaway. If an agent rewrites code for you, record real inputs and outputs first and replay them, plus edge cases, against the rewrite before you swap it in. When you add a native extension, move the whole loop across the FFI boundary, not the small function inside it.

Classifying tests by scope, hypothesis, oracle and method

Splitting how a test generates inputs from how it decides pass or fail shows you where property-based and simulation testing fit in a stateful backend.

Alperen Keles, who works on testing for Turso (a from-scratch rewrite of SQLite), wants to replace unit/integration/E2E with clearer axes. Scope is function, type, module or system, and either stateful or stateless. Hypothesis is what the test tries to falsify: correctness, timing, security or resilience. Oracle is what judges the result: a human, examples, inline assertions or properties. Method is how inputs are produced: tinkering by hand, examples, static checking, enumeration, or random generation and fuzzing. Assertions and properties are oracles only, so any method can drive them.

Under the hood

  • Main property types: roundtrip (decompress(compress(b)) = b), metamorphic (UNION is a subset of UNION ALL), differential (par(f,x) = f(x)) and idempotency (sort(sort(l)) = sort(l)).
  • Two ways to test stateful systems: keep a simpler reference model (Turso's approach) or query the system's own state (SQLancer's), which can miss bugs that also affect that state.
  • Deterministic simulation testing (DST) injects disk, network and process faults. FoundationDB popularised it; TigerBeetle and Antithesis are named as places to learn more.
  • On HN, one commenter said functional vs non-functional plus unit/integration/system is enough, and static types already rule out many invalid inputs.

Takeaway. Look for roundtrip, idempotency and differential properties in your existing example tests and run them with Hypothesis or fast-check. For stateful services, test against a small in-memory model and check invariants after each step.

simonwillison.net · Nov 22 · HN ▲73, 11 comments · 2 min

mod_wsgi's author builds Muru, a Rust-fronted Python app server, with AI

The fork-safety check and the autoscaler that explains why requests wait are good patterns for any backend you run.

Graham Dumpleton, who wrote mod_wsgi, is building Muru, a Rust server for WSGI and ASGI apps written with AI help. He has documented it in 19 posts. A Rust edge handles TLS and HTTP, then sends already-parsed requests to Python workers as typed messages over a Unix socket. A Python host process imports the app once, calls gc.freeze(), then forks the workers. If the import left open sockets or running threads behind, the host falls back to having each worker import the app itself. The autoscaler reports why requests wait, using GIL wait, garbage-collection share and event-loop lag. HN commenters found the AI-written posts tedious but liked the fork design.

Under the hood

  • Preload check: an import that leaves a connected socket or a running thread triggers a warning and the per-worker fallback. Setting preload = true turns this into an error. Hooks run at set points: pre_fork, post_fork, on_drain and others.
  • In one test, breaking a reference cycle cut GC time from 73% to 0% and raised throughput from 17,977 to 73,637 requests in 40 s. Wrapping a blocking call in asyncio.to_thread cut event-loop lag from hundreds of ms to under 5 ms.
  • On free-threaded Python 3.15t, four CPU-bound requests on one 4-thread worker took about 0.36 s, against about 1.3 s with the GIL (rough laptop numbers). The extra edge-to-worker hop costs tens of microseconds per request.

Takeaway. In a preforking server, check for fork-unsafe resources (sockets, threads, child processes) right after import, and freeze the GC before forking so workers keep sharing memory pages. Scale on why requests are slow (GIL wait, GC share, loop lag), not only on how many slots are busy.

OpenAI publishes internal pretraining loss and proposes it as a cross-lab limit

OpenAI's chief scientist argues base-model cross-entropy still predicts new capabilities despite RL, and wants labs to cap it until monitoring catches up.

Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist, says pretraining is training a compressor: a model that predicts unseen data. OpenAI has tracked its progress with log-perplexity (cross-entropy loss, the average surprise per next token) on a held-out snapshot of its internal codebase since GPT-3.5. The numbers: GPT-3.5 at ~0.99 (fluent chat), GPT-4 at ~0.91 (useful chain-of-thought), and below 0.8 for strong cybersecurity and math work. A ~0.76 model started resolving open math problems. He expects more capabilities, unknown in advance, at 0.68–0.7. The steep 2026 drop is credited mostly to long-term research and compute, with a smaller share from AI automation.

He proposes perplexity as a shared metric and wants labs to commit to limits they will not cross until monitoring reaches a set milestone, such as a successor to CoT (chain-of-thought) monitors. He lists the metric's limits himself. Labs use different datasets. Loss does not map to tasks. CoT and RL gains don't show up in loss. Nobody knows the floor. On HN, Microsoft reported MAI-2 Base at 0.819 on OpenAI's set, using the same tokenizer, and suggested bits-per-byte for comparing models with different tokenizers. Commenters questioned whether capabilities really appear at thresholds and noted that GPT-4.5 is the chart's only discontinuity.

Under the hood

  • Metric: log-perplexity on held-out internal code. Perplexity runs from 1 (perfect prediction) to the vocabulary size (random guessing), so the log runs from 0 to log(vocab).
  • Code is a poor proxy for GPT-2 and GPT-3 because of what they were trained on. Pachocki concedes that choosing the dataset is a real problem for a shared metric.
  • Unknown floor: as loss nears the data's own entropy, progress will look slower, but each small gain should mean a bigger jump in capability.
  • Pachocki warns that AI doing pretraining algorithm research could bring an 'avalanche' of advances as soon as 2027, faster than alignment can keep up.

Takeaway. Base-model loss on held-out data you care about is still the most stable progress signal; benchmarks saturate, and loss does not. Comparing models with different tokenizers requires bits-per-byte, not per-token perplexity.

Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini loops 12 shared layers three times

A 26B-total, 3B-active multimodal MoE for local machines that gets 48 layer passes from 24 stored layers, with day-zero llama.cpp, MLX and SGLang support.

Thinking Machines Lab's Inkling 2 Mini is a mixture-of-experts model (each token is routed to a few expert sub-networks) with ~26B total and 3B active parameters, a 1M-token context, and image plus text input. Layers 0–5 and 18–23 run once. Layers 6–17 run three times with the same weights, so each token gets 48 layer passes from 24 stored layers. Each loop pass gets a learned message from the previous pass, mixed in by a gating controller. Hugging Face reports it leads 32 of 48 benchmarks against Gemma 4 31B, Qwen3.8-27B and others. Most of the evidence is vibe tests.

Under the hood

  • Hidden size 2,304. Each pass routes a token to 8 of 128 SwiGLU experts. Each six-layer block has five 1,024-token sliding-window attention layers and one full-attention layer.
  • A research checkpoint, TML-d24-varloop-research, lets you set loop_num_passes to 1–3 to trade quality for speed. It has not been evaluated in depth.
  • Shipped as NVFP4/MXFP8 mixed-precision weights, with GGUF IQ4_NL and Q8_0 quants. A three-layer DSpark draft model speeds up generation through speculative decoding in SGLang.

Takeaway. Reusing looped weights trades stored parameters for extra compute per token: memory stays small, but every pass still keeps its own KV cache. If you run models locally, try the variable-loop checkpoint and measure the quality and speed trade-off on your own agent tasks.

developers.googleblog.com · Google Developers · Nov 21 · 2 min

Running private MCP servers on Cloud Run for Gemini Enterprise: PSC, scoped DNS, two tokens

A reusable pattern for agent tool servers: keep them off the public internet and check both the calling service and the end user on every tool call.

A Google Cloud forward-deployed engineer shows how Gemini Enterprise can call an MCP server on Cloud Run that has internal-only ingress. Traffic enters your VPC through an Egress Agent Gateway attached to a PSC Network Attachment. From there it reaches Cloud Run in one of two ways: Private Google Access VIPs (199.36.153.8/30), or a global PSC endpoint (240.0.0.10) for VPCs with no default route. A private DNS zone scoped to the one service hostname avoids overriding *.run.app across a Shared VPC. Every call carries two credentials: one for the calling service, one for the user.

Under the hood

  • Failure modes: with internal ingress, public calls get HTTP 404 at Google's edge. Without private DNS, .run.app resolves to public IPs and calls time out with 502.
  • X-Serverless-Authorization carries the Gemini Enterprise service agent's OIDC token. Cloud Run IAM checks it against the Invoker role, then strips it.
  • Authorization: Bearer passes the user's OAuth 2.0 token through unchanged, so middleware in the MCP server can enforce per-user permissions.
  • Cloud DNS picks the longest matching suffix, so the single-hostname zone leaves other services alone. The gateway connects to the VIP but keeps the real hostname in TLS SNI and the Host header.

Takeaway. For agent tool servers, check the calling service at the platform layer and the human user in your own code. Scope any DNS override to the exact hostname, never a wildcard.

Featured · tianyin.github.io · Nov 21 · Lobsters ▲23, 10 comments · 2 min

How a months-old Windows lock bug cascaded into a global Exchange Online outage

A textbook cascading failure: failover and load-shifting automation spread the fault, and a fleet-wide restart turned recovery into a thundering herd.

On November 12, Microsoft 365's network gateway servers ran into a latent defect: lock contention inside an internal Windows process exhausted CPU and memory. The bug came from a July update to a configuration used only by Exchange Online's gateways. Three normal conditions combined to trigger it. About 14% of gateway capacity was out for routine deployment. A routing issue in US East failed all Teams traffic, worldwide, onto the gateway Exchange shares. North American peak then arrived. Newer, higher-core SKUs (hardware generations) suffered more contention, but load tests ran on older, fewer-core machines. As servers hung, traffic management moved their load to other capacity units (shards of servers plus networking) and then to other regions. More than 80% of North American servers became unresponsive in under two hours, and every region was affected within another two.

Recovery took about 24 hours. Servers rejoining the pool were immediately overwhelmed again by queued reconnects. Change auditing focused on recent changes, so it missed one shipped months earlier. There was also no flag or config rollback that could undo it. Each server had to be drained, restarted, patched, restarted again and returned to service, with groups of capacity units recovered together. No mail was lost; the retry window before a bounce was raised from 24 to 48 hours.

Under the hood

  • Timeline: Teams failover at 1:07 AM UTC, ~16 healthy hours, broad failures at ~4:00 PM, first partial fix applied at 7:20 AM next day, impact ended 4:10 PM.
  • Early fixes failed: health-check bypasses, an alternative health check and auto-restart loops; at 1:53 AM they disabled the automatic traffic redirection.
  • Planned fixes: Teams fails over only for the affected region, gateway capacity split into per-workload pools, and smaller fault zones.
  • Rejoin fixes: gradual traffic ramp-up, admission control, traffic shedding by protocol, workload or region, plus better retry and back-off.

Takeaway. Automatic load redistribution needs a cap: if most of a pool is failing, moving its load to the survivors spreads the failure. Add admission control and a slow ramp-up so restarted nodes don't die again, and load-test on your newest, highest-core hardware.

Cloudflare open-sources Clairic, a 27B and 9B schema-driven extraction model pair

A small, cheap model for memory ingestion: you give it typed fields and it returns values with confidence scores, null probabilities and character spans into the source.

Cloudflare released Clairic (27B, fine-tuned from Qwen3.8-27B) and Clairic-flash (9B) under Apache-2.0. You send a text state, up to four images or PDF pages, and typed fields; the model returns structured answers. Since the preview, each field is generated by parallel slot autoregressive generation: every field decodes its own tokens and stops at its own end marker, and all fields advance together in the serving batch. Separate heads handle computed values, estimates and forecasts. On Cloudflare's own evals, Clairic scored 335/336 on fresh text fields, against 304 for Liquid D1.2 with OCR.

Under the hood

  • Latency on fresh text fields, p50/p95: Clairic 358/736 ms, flash 155/328 ms. Image fields: 453/976 ms and 305/605 ms.
  • Each answer carries a full-sequence confidence, a separately scored p_null and character spans. Image-only or ambiguous evidence gets null spans.
  • Supported field types include text, number, date, duration, enum, object, list, compute, estimate (optional quantiles) and forecast. source_only restricts answers to values printed in the source.
  • Workers AI pricing: $0.24 per M input tokens for the 27B and $0.10 for the 9B, with no charge for output tokens. Both cost $0.10 until December 1, 2026.
  • All evals are internal and self-reported, and no public extraction benchmark exists yet. Cloudflare itself calls the results directional.

Takeaway. If your memory pipeline uses a frontier LLM to pull typed facts out of documents, try Clairic-flash on a local GPU. Set acceptance thresholds on p_null and confidence, and use the spans for provenance.

engineering.fb.com · Meta Engineering · Nov 18 · 1 min

Meta cuts per-model ML engineering time 92% by standardizing ads models on shared templates

Constraining the design space before automating it is what made Meta's optimization and agent workflows work across its fleet of ads models. The same idea applies to any agent harness.

Meta's ads ranking group replaced many independently designed production models with a small set of Standard Model Templates (SMT). Each template is an opinionated setup with fixed architecture, features, training settings and serving config. New techniques are tested on a few representative models, checked on held-out models, then rolled out to every model built on that template. Over two years Meta reports 92% less engineering time per model, 5000+ technique-model combinations shipped, +0.63% offline quality at the same serving capacity, and +0.86% cumulative online lift.

Under the hood

  • Multi-model optimization judges a technique on two things: average gain across representative models and how often it causes regressions. A big average with frequent regressions does not ship.
  • 80 new ML techniques reached templates this way, and the +0.86% online lift covers 70% of models by count.
  • Because models share structure, scaling and cost-performance trade-offs are learned once per template instead of once per model.
  • Templates bound the search space for Meta's multi-agent model building. Agents run multi-day experiment loops, diagnose failures and migrate models to new hardware.

Takeaway. Before you automate a fragmented system, cut down how much it varies, so that one validated change can apply everywhere. Gate each rollout on how often it causes regressions as well as on the average gain.

Baseten's design for securing GPU clusters across 20+ clouds: one cluster per credential

A clear pattern for limiting the damage when one cluster in a multi-provider fleet is compromised. It applies to any fleet of unevenly hardened hosts.

Baseten runs inference on 100+ clusters across 20+ providers. Many are neoclouds (smaller GPU clouds) that hand over machines with open public ports. The core rule is that no credential, key or trust root spans more than one cluster. Each cluster has its own CA, so a token signed in one cluster is invalid in another. Each cluster also pulls its config with its own read-only deploy key, so a compromised cluster cannot change what any other cluster runs. The threat model covers four boundaries: provider infrastructure, operating partners, workloads and internal access.

Under the hood

  • Customer builds run in microVMs, each with its own kernel. Customer and platform images are kept in separate registries.
  • RDMA transfers between nodes go through a node agent that authorizes each one. Cross-cluster traffic uses mTLS, and runtime detection watches for escapes through the GPU runtime.
  • Data-distribution credentials rotate within an hour. Audit, host, DNS and SSH logs are shipped off-cluster to one paging system, and every cluster's perimeter is scanned daily.

Takeaway. Scope every key and CA to a single cluster, and make config-pull keys read-only, so a compromised cluster stays contained. The post's closing checklist works as an audit list for your own fleet or for vetting a provider.

OpenCode: open-source TypeScript coding agent with permission-scoped plan and build agents

If you build agent harnesses, OpenCode is a readable open-source example of how one coding agent splits modes and permissions.

OpenCode (anomalyco/opencode) is an open-source AI coding agent written in TypeScript. Bend 2 makes agents prove their code obeys declared laws before committing

If you build agents that write code, this is a concrete design for checking that code by machine proof instead of by human review.

ROAM-GRPO trains on solved problems by asking for a different method

A simple way to stop RL from collapsing onto one reasoning path: trade single-try accuracy for better coverage across many tries.

Jeff Duan and Matt Thomson (Caltech) target mode collapse in GRPO: after RL training on verifiable rewards, repeated samples come out nearly identical, so pass@k (chance that at least one of k tries is right) stalls. ROAM-GRPO adds a router. When all 8 rollouts on a problem are correct, the problem moves to an "off-mode" track. There, the median-length correct solution, cut to 300 characters, is shown as "Method 1", and the policy samples 16 candidates for "Method 2 (a genuinely different approach)". A Qwen3.5-9B judge checks each candidate's final answer and logic, then scores how similar its method is to Method 1. The most distinct sound candidate, plus the shortest original correct solution as an anchor, is distilled back into the policy with cross-entropy loss.

The results are mixed and the paper says so. On Qwen3.5-2B, pass@1 drops sharply (55.5% vs 69.7% on MATH). Pass@16 roughly ties: 89.8% vs 91.0% on MATH and 43.2% vs 41.3% on Omni-MATH. A delayed variant, switched on at step 5000 from a GRPO checkpoint, reaches 46.0% on Omni-MATH, 4.7 points above GRPO's best, but swings in later steps. It is one small model, one domain and unstable training.

Under the hood

  • Setup: LoRA r=8, α=16; 1,000 MATH Level-5 problems; G=8, k=8, H=16, λ=0.2; no KL penalty; thinking mode off; 8×A100.
  • The judge scores similarity from 0 to 100 and is told to ignore voice and syntax. Candidates qualify for distillation below 95. Outputs that mention 'a different approach' are filtered out.
  • Solution dispersion (mean pairwise embedding distance between correct answers, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B) is 0.09–0.14 for ROAM vs 0.05–0.07 for GRPO on MATH.
  • A method-level count by an LLM judge finds +0.25 correct methods per problem on MATH and +0.10 on Omni-MATH (correct answers only). Code: github.com/jduantc/ROAM-GRPO.

Takeaway. Problems the policy always solves give GRPO no signal. You can reuse them to generate verified alternative solutions for distillation. If you have deterministic checks such as unit tests or a Lean kernel, use them in place of the LLM judge.

GRPO: group-relative advantages, and why solved problems teach nothing

Policy-gradient RL needs an advantage: how much better one action was than expected. PPO gets the expected value from a learned critic, a value network usually about as large as the policy. That doubles memory, and the critic is hard to train when the only reward comes at the end of a long completion. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, from DeepSeekMath) drops the critic. It samples several answers to the same prompt and uses the group itself as the baseline.

Step by step: for a prompt, sample G completions from the current policy. Score each one with a reward, often a verifier that returns 1 for correct and 0 for wrong. Compute each completion's advantage as A_i = (r_i − mean(r)) / std(r), and give that value to every token in completion i. Then maximise PPO's clipped probability-ratio objective, with a KL penalty that keeps the policy close to a reference model. Example: with G=4 and rewards [1,0,0,1], the mean is 0.5 and the std is 0.5, so the advantages are +1, −1, −1, +1. Correct answers are pushed up and wrong ones pushed down. With rewards [1,1,1,1], every advantage is 0 (implementations add a small epsilon to the std to avoid dividing by zero), so there is no gradient. A prompt every sample fails gives none either. ROAM-GRPO puts those always-solved prompts back to work.

Trade-offs: GRPO is simple and saves memory, but it costs G samples per prompt. Credit is assigned per sequence, not per token. Dividing by the std can skew how much weight easy and hard prompts get. And most of the signal disappears on prompts that are too easy or too hard, so filtering prompts and choosing a curriculum matter. Use GRPO when rewards can be checked automatically and you can afford the sampling. Use a critic with GAE when rewards are dense or episodes are long.

Further reading: DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models (Shao et al., 2024); Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms (Schulman et al., 2017)

AltRA-Test checks if an LLM judge can replace humans when annotators vary by item

If your evals use LLM judges, this gives you a cheap statistical check of whether the judge keeps the signal your human labels share, and it works with crowdsourced labels where each item has different annotators.

Nagham Omar, Rotem Dror, Almog Gueta, Avigdor Gal, Roi Reichart, Nitay Calderon — Technion Israel institute of technology

Technion, Haifa and Google Research researchers built AltRA-Test because earlier tests (Alt-Test, STT) need the same annotators on every item, and 11 of their 20 datasets don't have that. Their test runs a paired bootstrap. It resamples items, draws k=3 human labels per item and swaps one label for the LLM's. Then it checks that inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff's α) for the mixed panel is no worse than for the all-human panel, within a margin that grows with human disagreement. Across 18 judges and 20 datasets, LLMs passed in 35.3% of judge–dataset pairs with chain-of-thought prompting and 47.3% with each judge's best prompt. About 100 items usually suffice.

Under the hood

  • Margin ε = c·(k_c/k)·(1−R_h), where R_h is human agreement and c=0.1 by default (at most 10% more disagreement). At N=200, better-than-human simulated judges pass 98% of the time and worse ones under 5%.
  • On CIFAR-10H, which has gold labels, 8 of 9 LLM–prompt combinations at least as accurate as humans (0.94) passed, and 0 of 36 less accurate ones did.
  • Use it only when human agreement is at least 0.2, since a random LLM starts passing below 0.1. It can't catch bias that humans and the LLM share. A per-group version on DICES-350 showed 3 of 4 passing judges failing on the Latinx group.

Takeaway. Before you replace human labels with an LLM judge, collect about 100 items with at least 3 human labels each and run this test on that task. Report human agreement next to the verdict, because results depend more on the dataset than on the model.

Under the hood

Under the hood
