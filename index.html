OpenAI math preprints claim matrix multiplication exponent is exactly 9/4

Four preprints claim to settle eight famous open problems, and HN's biggest thread of the week is arguing over whether that is insight or industrial-scale search.

The headline claim: the matrix multiplication exponent ω (the power in the cost of multiplying n×n matrices) is exactly 9/4 over every field. That would refute the long-held conjecture that ω = 2. It comes from four preprints in OpenAI's math repo on GitHub. HN user zone411 counts eight problems from an LLM-ranked "Top 500 Open Problems" list. Strongly polynomial linear programming (Smale's ninth problem) is claimed solved. So are polynomial-time algorithms for mean-payoff, parity and Condon's simple stochastic games. The polynomial diameter bound for polytopes and the hope of a polynomial pivot rule for simplex are both refuted: some vertex pairs are superpolynomially far apart. The Erdős–Rado sunflower bound is proved for every fixed petal count. Scott Aaronson's reaction post, titled "Holy shit holy shit holy shit holy shit", reached #1 on HN with over 2,000 points.

The most useful technical observation comes from _alternator_. Unlike earlier one-off results, these papers share one underlying framework: shared labels in the sunflower proof, high-dimensional placements for arithmetic cuts, and spectral comparison for circuit lower bounds. That makes the work look like a new method rather than a pile of tricks. Verification is still open. Arkhaine_kupo notes that the earlier Riemann Hypothesis claim has not yet been accepted by mathematicians, though early checks find it is not exploiting Lean bugs. NetMageSCW says that proof is down to 500 pages, and nobody in the thread produced a source for that.

The fight is over mechanism. twotwoone calls it "tens of thousands of agents" generating and filtering proofs, "the same old exhaustive, brute-force process." paddybyers, who has a maths PhD, says the leaps would be called genius if a human made them. kortex points out that pruning by heuristic is by definition not brute force. utopiah asks how much of this Lean and Mathlib with plenty of compute would have done without LLMs. alkonaut argues that outside mathematics only P=NP would really change things. nickysielicki doubts the labs are quietly holding cryptographic breaks, because leaks would surface at their size.

“LLMs are making logical leaps which, if you saw a human do them, you'd describe as genius. I have a maths PhD and I am very surprised at the rate of progress this year.”

— paddybyers on HN

Under the hood

  • Claims cover #25 (ω), #89 (Smale's 9th), #64/#65/#340 (games), #183/#130 (polytope diameter, simplex pivot), #137 (sunflower) on proofatlas.ai's list.
  • Commenters say Terence Tao predicted, about a month earlier, that the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis would not fall until around 2050.
  • Arkhaine_kupo predicts that some Lean-checked proofs will fail manual review because of loopholes or missing conditions in the stated lemmas.

Letting the model choose when to compact nearly triples GPT-6.1's score at 32K

The gains depend heavily on the model: Opus 5.5 barely touches the context API, and one line of prompt decides whether Kimi compacts at 84% of its budget or at 29%.

Alessio Devoto, Avinash Vem, Tara Safavi, Sanjay Kariyappa, Yu-Hsuan Tseng, Paul Furgale, Ricardo Silveira Cabral — NVIDIA

On OfficeQA Pro v2 with a 32K-token budget, GPT-6.1 Sol went from 21% to 56% once NVIDIA's NOOA harness let it compact its own history. The usual approach is harness-driven: at 80% full, summarize everything except the instructions and the last 10 events. In the model-driven version, the model calls collapse to replace a range of events with its own summary. The originals are archived and can be fetched back by tag, so compaction is reversible. GPT compacts hardest (median 3.7× shrink per call) and re-fetches archived content after 19% of calls. Opus 5.5 uses the API in only 4% of tasks and tends to just wrap up when warned. The gains are largest at tight budgets and mostly disappear on MLE-bench Lite.

Prompting matters a lot. Without the "compact at 85%" instruction, Kimi's median first compaction moves from 84% of the budget to 29%. At 128K and above, that early compaction costs about 8 points. The authors call this context anxiety and suspect RL length penalties are the cause. Separately, reusing the KV cache of text that comes after an edit, prototyped in SGLang, cut prefill compute by 4–15%. Doubts: the benchmarks are small (90 questions, 22 competitions), long tasks are simulated by shrinking the budget, and they measured prefill compute, not latency.

“Working in a harness that compacts history automatically does not necessarily teach a model when to compact, what to remove, or how to keep useful information.”

— Devoto et al. on the post

Under the hood

  • Model-driven setup: live context-usage indicator, an instruction to consider compacting at 85%, and a context-pressure warning near the limit.
  • Hard budget from 8K to 512K; on overflow the oldest events are dropped and the request retried; after three failures the run counts as wrong.
  • Kimi K3 at 32K: 36M vs 62M cumulative input tokens (41% fewer), scoring 43% vs 41%.
  • Suffix KV reuse with Qwen3.6-27B saves the most on OfficeQA because about 10K tokens (~60% of the prompt) survive each edit.

Takeaway. Make compaction reversible with an archive the model can retrieve from, and tune the compaction-timing instruction for each model, because the same API produces very different timing.

Google will require AI agents to reach Workspace only through its MCP servers

The public Gmail server can't send, reply or edit a draft, but partners like Anthropic's Claude connector can, and memory indexes built from Workspace data may be capped at 30 days.

Google's new API terms take effect on 1 February 2027. After that, any tool that "is or is driven by an AI Agent" must use Google's eight Workspace MCP servers. Agents that call the REST API directly get Standard-tier quotas and break the terms. The public Gmail server can search, read and create drafts, but it can't send, reply, forward or edit a draft. Partners get those tools. Google may also limit how long embeddings and memory indexes built from Workspace data are kept, to 30 days. perdy argues that MCP elicitation, where the server pauses a tool call to ask the user, could already gate sending. To him, making send partner-only looks like a business decision rather than a safety one.

“As a send message tool could be gated behind an elicitation, so making it partner-only seems more like a business decision than a safety concern.”

— perdy on HN

Under the hood

  • Standard tier: 5,000 MCP requests a day per project per product. A Gmail search_threads call counts as 10 requests, which leaves 500 searches a day across all users.
  • OAuth clients must be marked agentic. Clients created before the cut-off that weren't marked stay non-agentic permanently, so their owners need new clients.
  • Workspace data can't be used for training, fine-tuning, evals or distillation, and it must be deleted when the user disconnects.

Plain SFT on archived RL rollouts reproduces the whole RL learning curve

Replaying the original RL algorithm on saved rollouts collapses, but fitting by maximum likelihood to every response, right or wrong and with no rewards, tracks online RL to within about a point.

Jiayi Sheng, Jingwen Gu, Guanning Zeng, Zhaoyi Zhou, Stefano Viel, Qianqian Wang, Fahim Tajwar, Andrea Zanette et al. — Carnegie Mellon University, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Tufa Labs

The obvious way to reproduce an RL run is to replay it: save every batch of rollouts and run the same algorithm on them again. That fails. For Qwen3-1.7B-Base trained with MaxRL, MATH-500 pass@1 under replay drops to near zero by step 100. Truncated importance sampling, a standard off-policy correction, only pushes the crash out to about step 600. The paper's explanation: once the replayed model drifts even slightly from the model that generated the data, the policy-gradient baseline becomes a +KL term that pushes the model further away, so the drift compounds. Their fix reverses that sign. They run plain SFT on every archived response, with no rewards, one update per batch, in the original order. Over 1,000 steps on four benchmarks this stays within 0.2–1.1 points of online RL (mean pass@k gap), and it also works for runs trained with GRPO and RLOO.

The order of batches matters: replaying them in reverse ends up near the base model. More striking, repeated SFT on the rollouts of one late RL step, 0.1% of the archive, recovers the 1.7B model's final performance. On Knights and Knaves the gap drops from 71.9 points to 0.9. My doubts: the models are 0.6B–4B and the tasks are verifiable. Also, the appendix says evaluations use temperature 0, which sits oddly next to pass@32 numbers, since pass@k needs varied samples.

“much of the compute invested in RL goes into discovering an improved sampling distribution rather than learning it from samples.”

— Sheng et al. on the post

Under the hood

  • SmolLM2-360M on GSM8K is the exception: one batch is not enough, and the last 50 batches recover most of the performance.
  • Pooling the archives of two specialists (math and puzzles) keeps most of both skills and generally gives a better trade-off than merging their weights.
  • Qwen3-0.6B-Base trained on the 1.7B model's rollouts roughly matches distillation from the final checkpoint on MATH-500 and Minerva.

Takeaway. Archive RL rollouts with their prompts in step order. They can rebuild the policy, move it to a smaller model, or merge specialists later.

1990s web-cache policies beat Clock and LRU for S3-backed OLAP caches

A 22-policy benchmark finds GDS, GDSF and LRU-SP near the top of a latency-weighted hit ratio, but every trace comes from a single engine, SAP HANA.

Taejun Yoon, Taesung Lee, Joo Yeon Lee, Ki Hong Kim, Byungchul Tak, Wook-Shin Han

On S3, the authors fit repeated download timings and got about 45 ms fixed cost per request plus 12 ms per MiB. A 1 MiB miss therefore costs about 57 ms, and most of that is the fixed request overhead. Even so, Redshift (two-level Clock), Databricks and DuckDB (recency-based) still use page-buffer policies that treat every miss as costing the same. A POSTECH and SAP Labs Korea group ported 22 replacement algorithms into libCacheSim. They replayed Parquet column-chunk traces from HANA reading S3 across seven workloads and ten cache sizes. The scoring metric is Unified Hit Ratio (UHR): each hit is weighted by the latency it saves. That works out to a per-workload blend of object hit ratio (requests avoided) and byte hit ratio (bytes avoided).

Size-aware web-cache policies (GDS, GDSF, LRU-SP), which score each object by its value divided by its size, have the best object hit ratio on most workloads and stay near the top on UHR. Byte efficiency comes from dropping chunks that are never reused (Cacheus, S3-FIFO, LIRS). Under multi-tenant drift, cheap adjustments made on each request held up better than learned policies like LRB that need retraining. The authors' reference policy, LARA, gets the best mean UHR rank. Caveats: the cost model assumes no bandwidth contention, and the CAB benchmark ran read-only because its reference implementation's updates break joins.

“Classical size-aware policies developed outside the DBMS literature remain surprisingly strong, dominating object hit ratio on most workloads and staying near the UHR frontier.”

— Yoon et al. on the post

Under the hood

  • UHR = w_O·OHR + w_B·BHR, where w_O = α/(α+β·Sμ) and Sμ is the workload's mean request size.
  • Median chunk size is 0.50–1.51 MiB; the p90/p10 size ratio runs from 14.8 to 37,250.
  • 27 configurations: latency-cost variants of GDS, GDSF and LFU-DA, two 3L-Cache variants, plus Crystal as a semantic-caching baseline.
  • Every policy was changed to skip pinned objects; the Redbench trace has about 36K statements, about 11K of them DML.

Takeaway. For caches in front of object storage, score eviction by reuse divided by size (GDSF-style) and add a probationary filter for one-hit chunks.

Learn · today's concept

GreedyDual-Size-Frequency: evicting by what a miss actually costs

LRU and Clock assume every cached item has the same size and every miss costs the same. Object storage breaks both assumptions. The S3 timings in today's cache benchmark show about 45 ms of fixed cost per request plus 12 ms per MiB. A 1 MiB miss costs about 57 ms and an 8 MiB miss about 141 ms. Measured per byte of cache space, the small object saves far more latency. A cache that only tracks recency throws that difference away.

GreedyDual-Size (Cao & Irani, 1997) gives each object a priority H = L + cost/size. GDSF adds frequency: H = L + freq × cost/size. L is a global 'inflation' value that starts at 0. To evict, remove the object with the lowest H and set L to that H. A hit increments freq and recomputes H using the current L. Since L only goes up, newly inserted or recently hit items start above items that have sat untouched, so this works as aging without timestamps. Example: A (1 MiB, 57 ms) gets H = 57. B (8 MiB, 141 ms) gets H ≈ 17.6. B is evicted first and L becomes 17.6. When A is hit again, freq becomes 2 and H = 17.6 + 2×57 = 131.6.

Trade-offs: GDSF needs a priority queue, which costs O(log n) per access where Clock only flips a bit. It also needs a cost model, and it penalises large objects, which can lower the byte hit ratio. Items that are read once still enter the cache at L + cost/size and pollute it, so pair GDSF with an admission filter: a probationary queue or TinyLFU. Use it when sizes and fetch costs vary widely, as in caches in front of S3, CDNs and tiered storage.

Further reading: Cao & Irani, "Cost-Aware WWW Proxy Caching Algorithms" (USENIX USITS 1997); Cherkasova, "Improving WWW Proxies Performance with Greedy-Dual-Size-Frequency Caching Policy" (HP Labs Technical Report, 1998)

ByteDance Seed's looped Transformer passes KV between loops to decode in one pass

Two-loop quality at single-loop decode latency, but the latency was measured at 113M parameters, and the training cost is still roughly double.

Lianghui Zhu, Shijie Wang, Xinggang Wang, Jinze Bai, Bo Wang, Xiang Bai — ByteDance Seed

Looped Transformers reuse the same layers several times per token. A 2-loop model on 113M-parameter (S-size) models took 1,974ms for a 4,096-token prefill plus 64 decode steps, against 1,000.6ms for plain OLMo3. ByteDance Seed and HUST's MoLT gets that back to 996.6ms. The trick is in MoLT-KV: each loop restarts from token embeddings instead of carrying the residual stream (the running hidden state) forward. It reads the previous loop's output only as memory KV (keys and values) at strictly earlier positions. Each token then stops depending on its own previous-loop state, so decoding can go through the shared stack once and append new memory KV for later tokens. Training uses "loop forcing": every loop is supervised and trained in parallel, as in teacher forcing. U-MoLT adds a YOCO-style split, with a looped encoder that builds memory and a cross-attention-only decoder that skips work it does not need.

With 400B training tokens, U-MoLT beats OLMo3 at 700M, 1.5B and 3B MoE. At 700M it reaches the same cross-entropy with 1.7× fewer tokens. On the 3B MoE it gains 2.1 points across 10 tasks. Two caveats. That figure is token efficiency, not compute: a 2-loop training step took 942ms against 494ms. And the ablation differences are small (CE 3.318 down to 3.273).

“Instead of relying solely on a residual stream, our answer is to pass computed keys and values (KV) as memory for selective reuse across depths and loops.”

— Zhu et al. on the post

Under the hood

  • MoLA (Mixture-of-Loops Attention) puts sequence KV and same-token depth KV in one softmax with no learned gate between loops; QK RMSNorm keeps their scales comparable.
  • MoLT-R keeps the residual stream and also reads previous-loop KV for more expressivity, but it still decodes N loops in sequence.
  • Continued pretraining of Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base for 100B tokens: lower CE on all 11 domains, 1.4× mean token efficiency.
  • MoL-Hybrid gives up exact equivalence to a full multi-loop unroll in exchange for parallel prefill. Code: github.com/hustvl/MoLT.

Three GitLab bugs pinned Sentry's RPC autoscaler at 256 pods

Weeks of autoscaler tuning went nowhere until RPC calls were broken down by method, because total call volume looked flat.

Sentry's Control RPC deployment normally runs near 32 pods. For hours it sat at its 256-pod ceiling while external traffic was up only about 2x. Retuning the autoscaler helped a little: P95 busyness can stay high no matter how many pods are added. The cause turned up only when RPC calls were broken down by method, which showed get_organization_integrations up 10x. Three GitLab bugs had stacked up. A four-year-old grouping bug had put 2,000+ installations on one record. A webhook backfill fix then switched webhooks on for all of them. And an N² lookup across the silo boundary meant each lookup was an RPC, so one webhook could trigger millions. After the fix, the weekly average fell from about 150 pods to about 42.

“The N² lookup is harmless when N is 1.”

— Gabriel Villalobos on the post

Under the hood

  • KEDA scales on five equally weighted metrics. A few slow, IO-heavy requests kept P95 busyness high while CPU stayed near idle, so they moved to P75.
  • Scale-up added 4 pods every 90s. Scale-down removed 2 every 150s and waited 5 minutes before starting, so every burst ratcheted the count up.
  • Still unsolved: reverting the P75 change restarts the scale-up spiral, so some pods still run hot for unknown reasons.

Takeaway. Track internal RPC volume per method and per caller. Here the total looked flat while one method grew 10x.

Coding agents' extra edits are mostly tests, and unneeded code comes from the fix itself

Agent patches look bloated by line count, but measured by reverting each block, their code is about as necessary as developers' code, and leftover debugging accounts for almost none of the waste.

Aakash Goel, Benjamin Steenhoek, Yu Hu

On SWE-bench Verified, agent patches change 2.33× as many lines as the developer fix. But 61% of those extra edits are tests and 13% are documentation. Microsoft researchers replayed 7,660 resolved Copilot CLI trajectories from 18 models, reverted each changed block one at a time and reran the task tests. Agent code turned out about as necessary as developers' code (0.887 vs 0.864). About 11% of agent code blocks are still unneeded, and 89% of those change behavior. Fix steps wrote 83% of that code, and debug steps almost none. Pruning with the agent's own tests plus the repo's regression tests catches 67% of the unneeded code at 46% precision. Self-audit catches 36% at 66% precision.

Under the hood

  • Under a stricter oracle that adds behavioral probes, the agent-vs-developer gap flips from +2.3 to −4.5 points, so the headline comparison depends on the oracle.
  • Three Gemini Flash models leave significantly more unneeded code than developers. On DeepSWE, higher resolve rate correlates with more unneeded code (Spearman −0.59).
  • Step roles and probes were labeled by an LLM and checked only by a second LLM (κ=0.69), with no human audit.

Loom attention lets each head choose which earlier heads it reads, per token

For 2–3.7% extra FLOPs, a 2.8B model matches Pythia-12B on the authors' benchmarks, but it runs at 84–92% of baseline inference speed and has only been tested on the Pile.

Da Xiao, Qingye Meng, Shengping Li, Xingyuan Yuan

In a standard transformer, which heads feed which later heads is fixed once training ends. LoomFormer, from BUPT and ColorfulClouds, lets every head pick, at each token, which earlier heads its query, key and value come from. It does this with gated linear attention over a small per-token matrix memory (2n slots, where n is heads per layer). That memory works as a second residual stream: the ordinary vector stream decides where to read and write, and the matrix carries the content. On the Pile it matches Transformer++ trained with over 3× the compute. LoomPythia-2.8B, trained on 300B tokens, ties Pythia-12B on 0-shot (59.9) and beats it on 5-shot (61.6 vs 60.8). The gaps: inference runs at 84–92% of baseline speed, and nothing larger than 2.8B was tested.

Under the hood

  • Attention sinks are heads that switch off by attending to a throwaway token. In layers 17–32, 74.4% of heads stay active, vs 22.9% in Pythia-2.8B and 17.7% in mHC.
  • Ablation: freezing the read/write keys and queries as static vectors keeps 56% of the gain, so per-token rewiring accounts for roughly half.
  • Beats the residual-stream baselines HC/mHC, MUDDFormer and AttnRes run at 1.7–2.8× compute. Code at github.com/Caiyun-AI/LoomFormer, with JAX/Pallas kernels for training and Triton for inference.

simonwillison.net · Simon Willison · Nov 26 · 1 min

sqlite3_rsync snapshots a live SQLite database without local free space

The backup API and VACUUM INTO both need room for a second full copy on the same disk. sqlite3_rsync streams the snapshot straight to another machine.

Simon Willison points out a practical edge of sqlite3_rsync, which shipped as experimental in SQLite 3.47.0 and was improved in 3.50.0. The backup API and VACUUM INTO both write a full duplicate locally, so on a small instance with the disk over half full you can't snapshot at all. sqlite3_rsync sends the snapshot over SSH to another machine instead. It works like rsync: the replica sends hashes for groups of pages, and the origin returns only the pages that differ, or asks for finer-grained hashes. When the two copies start out very different, hash overhead adds at most a few percent of extra bandwidth.

Takeaway. For SQLite on small, nearly full disks, back up with sqlite3_rsync over SSH; the backup API and VACUUM INTO both need free space for a full local copy.

Four Qwen3-0.6B agents learn to share information via team-reward GRPO

The task can't be solved without messaging, so this run shows small agents can learn to communicate. It doesn't show that teaming up beats one agent with the same token budget.

baseten.co · Baseten · Nov 25 · 1 min

Baseten splits model credentials by stage: build, deploy and serve

The post admits a weak point: its domain allow list is enforced by IP address, and S3 serves many buckets from the same IPs, so only IAM actually keeps a model to one bucket.

Baseten shows how a model deployment can run without a permanent cloud key. At build time, an OIDC token (a short-lived signed identity token) lets volume sync copy the weights into a sealed, read-only volume addressed by a hash of its contents. Serving replicas mount that volume and never touch your bucket. At serve time, the container trades a short-lived token through AWS STS for read-only access to one adapter prefix. The trust policy matches type=model_container, so build permissions do not carry over to serving. Egress allow lists cut outbound traffic to the needed hosts, but they are enforced by IP, so for S3 the IAM policy does the real limiting. A vendor post, but a sound pattern.

“Short-lived tokens do not end an ongoing compromise, so narrowly scoped permissions and carefully chosen destinations remain essential.”

— Daniel Leef, Matt Howard, Rachel Rapp on the post

Under the hood

  • Runtime token path is exposed as B10_OIDC_TOKEN_PATH; set AWS_WEB_IDENTITY_TOKEN_FILE to it and boto3 handles STS.
  • Default egress: any host on TCP 443 and 80. Empty allow lists block all egress. Changing the lists requires a new deployment.
  • OIDC subject format: v=1:org=…:team=…:*:type=model_container.

Takeaway. Write separate trust policies for build-time and runtime identities, matched on the token's subject claim. A single role shared across both stages gives serving containers write or bucket-wide access they don't need.

jackkelly.name · Nov 25 · Lobsters ▲23, 5 comments · 1 min

Untangling amazonka's code generator by making the plan a value

The pattern itself is well known. The useful part is how to migrate an old codebase onto it one code path at a time, using a fallback constructor and a deliberate crash.

Jack Kelly cleaned up amazonka's decade-old code generator (amazonka is the unofficial Haskell AWS SDK, generated from botocore definitions). He split it into two parts: analysis that builds a Config plan as plain data, and instanceD :: Config -> Decl (), which turns that plan into code. He moved over gradually. A ResponseParser enum got a DoItTheOldWay fallback constructor. For each path he ported, the old code became error "whoops this is still live", and then every AWS binding was regenerated. If the output didn't change, the old path was deleted. The generated bindings served as a free golden test suite, meaning a set of saved outputs that any change gets diffed against.

“The trick is to keep a "do it the old way" constructor in your plan type until the "old way" has been refactored away.”

— Jack Kelly on the post

Under the hood

  • Payoffs he lists: a nearly free --dry-run, plans generated by property tests, coverage thresholds that make sure every plan constructor can be reached, and checks run before execution.
  • Plain data also lets you transform the plan. He regrets once inverting a transform via matrices when invert :: [Transform] -> [Transform] would have been simpler.
  • After the refactor, adding idempotency tokens (AWS client tokens) was straightforward.

Takeaway. When replacing tangled logic, add a fallback 'old way' variant to the new plan type, and make each retired path crash so any code still using it shows up.

Distinct index types per array in Zig, generated with comptime

The compiler catches a row index used on a column slice, but Zig has no private fields, so the guard only works if callers stick to the accessor.

The bug it targets is s.col_width[r], where r is a row index. The fix is a non-exhaustive enum(u32) { _ } per index kind plus a getter as the only approved way to read elements. A 0.8B model edits its own weights and gets lucky

The self-edits lift GSM8K from 11.8% to 62.2%, but the model edits whatever tensor is listed first, and random edits match it on GSM8K.

Guoming Ling, Shanshan Zhong, Kaixuan Zhang, Zhongzhan Huang — Carnegie Mellon University

Qwen3.5-0.8B was shown its own tensors and asked to propose edits. One kept edit changes just 16 numbers, the A_log that sets how fast the first linear-attention layer forgets, and lifts MATH500 from 7.0% to 24.2%. The model's stated reason contradicts the edit itself: it claims to undo a multiplication by −1.9795, then multiplies by that number again. Another explanation reads, in full, "The cost of one chair is $11." The real story is less magical. The base model gets stuck repeating itself and leaves about 90% of answers unfinished within its 32k-token budget, so any edit that breaks the loop pays off. The outer loop, which keeps only edits that score better on practice questions, does most of the work.

“The cost of one chair is $11.”

— Qwen3.5-0.8B, explaining a weight edit on the post

Under the hood

  • Of 11,580 proposals, 69% were invalid and only 12% of the valid ones were kept. When tensors were listed in layer order, 62% of edits hit layer 0, against 2.9% when the list was shuffled.
  • Typical spot-check runs gained 24.7 points on GSM8K and 7.7 on MATH500. The headline numbers are the best 3 of 12–24 runs.
  • Random search matched self-editing on GSM8K (32.8% vs 31.9%) but found nothing on MATH500 with the same budget.
